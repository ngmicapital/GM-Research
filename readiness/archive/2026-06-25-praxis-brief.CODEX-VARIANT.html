Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

Two public notes published or updated on June 24 converge on the same claim: professional empathy is trained work, not a vibe, and AI only compounds teams that already know how to supply context and review. Seth Godin's note on early-adopter feedback, Every's June guides on AI adoption and Codex workflows, and Paul Graham's June essay on startup empathy all point to the same edge: the scarce layer is judgment about users, workflows, and ownership.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Blog · Seth Godin · Empathy and good advice · June 24, 2026

Feedback from the crowd is not the same as signal from early adopters

Seth Godin's June 24 post draws a sharp distinction that product teams routinely blur: the average person you ask for feedback is often not the person who would adopt first. His example is the difference between the broader crowd and the "lonely early adopters" who line up for the first iPhone or buy from unknown artists before consensus forms.

The practical claim is stronger than "listen to users." Godin argues that empathy is a professional skill: the trained ability to imagine what a specific niche actually values, even when your friends or a focus group do not. That matters more now, not less, because faster iteration only accelerates the cost of aiming at the wrong audience.

Guide · Every Staff · AI Is Ready. Organizations Aren't. · Updated June 24, 2026

Most AI rollouts fail at the organizational layer, not the model layer

Every's updated June 24 briefing frames the bottleneck cleanly: AI adoption lags because companies have not redesigned workflows, trust boundaries, and review loops around the tools they already bought. Their linked "eight levels of AI adoption" framework explicitly treats autonomy as a gradient, not a binary.

The same issue highlights a Codex-native inbox workflow that works because it is grounded in context and feedback: the system pulls messages into review cards, drafts the next action, and learns from decisions the operator makes afterward. That is a stronger template than full-autonomy theater because it keeps judgment in the loop where mistakes are expensive.

Essay · Paul Graham · How to Earn a Billion Dollars · June 2026

The deepest economic leverage still starts with user empathy

Paul Graham's new June essay is superficially about startup math, but the operational lesson sits deeper in the piece: exponential outcomes come from making something people love enough to tell their friends. He reduces the founder's job to one hard thing: understanding users so well that you can build what they actually want.

His specific advice to younger builders is to work on problems you and your friends genuinely have, because those needs often predict future demand. The closing claim is the useful one for any operator: the key is not exploitation but empathy. Ownership compounds only when that empathy is correct.

Across all three sources, the moat is not raw output. It is better user-modeling: knowing who the real frontier user is, giving AI only the autonomy the task can bear, and owning the asset that compounds if your judgment is right.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · Seth Godin + Paul Graham

Separate frontier-user signal from crowd comfort before you kill an idea

Before you let feedback collapse a direction, classify the source. Godin's warning about crowd feedback and Graham's emphasis on deeply understood users both imply the same operating rule: consensus is not the right metric in the early stage.

  • Name the frontier user. Write down the few people who would adopt first, pay early, or tolerate rough edges because the problem matters to them.
  • Ask for behavior, not taste. Look for what they already hack around, endure, or spend on rather than whether they "like" your current form factor.
  • Track disagreement explicitly. Keep a short log of where mass feedback diverges from lead-user feedback so you do not average away the signal.
  • Optimize for intensity, not politeness. One group that urgently wants the thing is usually a better starting point than a large group that mildly approves it.
Practice · Every / Codex workflows

Delegate toil to AI, but keep judgment checkpoints stubbornly human

The usable pattern in Every's material is not "let the agent run the company." It is bounded delegation with explicit review: let AI compress collection, drafting, classification, and first-pass synthesis, then spend human time on correctness, taste, and consequence.

  • Front-load context. Provide goals, source documents, decision rules, and examples before asking for an artifact.
  • Delegate artifact creation. Use the agent for summaries, drafts, route options, and repetitive assembly work.
  • Review the deltas. Focus your attention on what changed, what is uncertain, and where a wrong answer would be expensive.
  • Own the reusable asset. Save the prompt, checklist, rubric, or workflow doc so each run improves the system rather than merely finishing a task.

Automation without review becomes theater. Review without reusable scaffolding becomes drudgery. The compounding middle is where the operator keeps judgment and lets the machine absorb repetition.

04 On the Horizon
WORK

Context engineering becomes a managerial skill, not just a power-user trick

As agents spread from one-off prompts to recurring operational loops, the scarce operator will be the person who can define the task, bound the risk, and review the output faster than others can merely ask for it.

AI

Adoption will stratify by error tolerance, not by who sounds most enthusiastic

The "levels of AI adoption" framing implies a widening gap between teams that climb carefully from assistive use to orchestrated workflows and teams that either over-delegate too early or never move past chatbot demos.

OWNERSHIP

Cheap execution increases the premium on assets that compound

If more people can generate competent output, the differential value shifts toward owned workflows, software, audiences, distribution, and datasets. Labor hours get cheaper; correct judgment attached to owned leverage gets more expensive.
