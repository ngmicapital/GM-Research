The GRANITE Act: A Shield, a Declaratory Sword, and a One-Line Cut Into the FSIA

Source Law of Code (Jacob Robinson) · Preston Byrne, Byrne & Storm P.C.
Domain Law & Regulation / Internet Speech
Reliability Mixed — bill identity, sponsor, text and status verified against the introduced text (H.R. 10174, GPO govinfo); the guest is counsel to the affected platforms and drafter of the blueprint, so his doctrinal and predictive claims are advocacy; two historical characterisations fail checking
Content Type Single-guest advocacy interview
Full Mode

⚡ The Bottom Line

  • The bill exists and is exactly as small as claimed. H.R. 10174 (GRANITE Act), sponsored by Rep. Michael A. Rulli (R–OH), introduced and referred to House Judiciary 27 August 2026: one new 28 U.S.C. § 4106 (three subsections) plus a one-line § 1605(a)(7). The transcript's "Representative Roy" is a mis-transcription of Rulli.
  • The shield's innovation is reaching demands, not just judgments. § 4106(a) bars US courts from recognising a foreign judgment or a "fine, order, or demand" enforcing a foreign censorship law — because, on Byrne's evidence, the censorship happens when the email arrives, not when a court rules.
  • The sword has been disarmed relative to Byrne's own draft, and he agrees. Wyoming's version carried statutory damages; the federal bill offers declaratory relief only. Byrne calls the removal "probably a good idea" — damages need deeper FSIA surgery and invite "diplomatic headaches."
  • The published criticism does not dispute the problem; it disputes the remedy. R Street, ITIF and Tech Policy Press accept the shield and doubt whether a declaration changes a foreign regulator's behaviour.

🔑 Key Claims (Ranked)

1. Ofcom obtained a ~98% compliance rate from American companies by email alone.
↳ Evidence: 197 s.100 notices into the US (Ofcom's own confirmed figure, as at 26 Feb. 2026, all by email, bypassing the UK–US MLAT); Byrne counts four public refusals.
↳ Caveat / consensus: the 197 is documented and Ofcom's own; the four-refusals denominator is Byrne's count as counsel.

2. Sovereign immunity is a threshold kill-switch, which is why the case never gets litigated.
↳ Evidence: immunity is subject-matter jurisdiction, raised on a Rule 12(b)(1) motion — "if they succeed on sovereign immunity, the case ends." Ofcom asserted FSIA immunity in the D.D.C.; Brazil intervened asserting immunity for Justice de Moraes (M.D. Fla.).
↳ Consensus: Aligned — settled FSIA doctrine.

3. The federal bill is the fix the critics themselves prescribed.
↳ Evidence: R Street (4 Mar. 2026) and Tech Policy Press (3 Mar. 2026) both argued the Wyoming sword failed on federal supremacy and the FSIA discretionary-function exception, and only Congress could authorise the carve-out. H.R. 10174 does that.
↳ Caveat: the European Commission is immune under the International Organizations Immunities Act, not the FSIA — so a § 1605(a) amendment misses DSA enforcement.
↳ Consensus: Emerging — shield accepted, sword untested.

4. GRANITE is a template, not a bill.
↳ Evidence: Wyoming HB 70 passed the state House 46–12 (23 Feb. 2026) then died in the short budget session; ALEC adopted a shield-only model on 24 July 2026; New Hampshire (Rep. Beaulier) is expected to file on it for 2027; NetChoice endorsed the day of recording.
↳ Caveat: Byrne's "three" federal bills and a forthcoming Sen. Eric Schmitt bill are unverified — no Schmitt bill exists.

5. Two historical set-pieces do not check out as narrated.
↳ The Schooner Exchange v. McFaddon, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 116 (1812) was an ownership libel by the dispossessed American owners, not bailiffs attaching a warship for debts — the holding stands, the facts do not.
↳ The revenue rule's "1712" origin is unverified; the standard citations are Attorney General v. Lutwydge (1729) and Holman v. Johnson (1775).

6. The deterrence theory rests on a cost estimate the guest built himself.
↳ Evidence: an Ofcom FOI response ("Resources and costs — enforcement action against 4chan," closed 17 Apr. 2026) reportedly disclosed 0.9–2.6 FTE over nine months; Byrne extrapolates 2,000–2,800 hours, ~$2.8m.
↳ Caveat: applies US law-firm rates to UK regulator time — rhetoric, not accounting.
