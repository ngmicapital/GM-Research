Kalshi's Licence Is Its Growth Channel — Not Its Compliance Cost

Source Bankless · Date n/a · 67 min
Domain Crypto & Markets — Regulated Derivatives ·
Reliability Mixed — a well-informed operator with direct visibility into Kalshi's perps and crypto business, but several volume and market-share numbers are self-reported and sit above what public trackers support.
Content Type Founder / Executive Interview
Full Mode

⚡ The Bottom Line

  • Kalshi's regulatory posture is an acquisition strategy, not a compliance burden. Being a CFTC-designated contract market is what buys Facebook and Instagram ads, billboards, App Store presence and broker integrations — the only channels that reach what Wang calls the "99% TAM."
  • Perpetual futures, not prediction markets, are now Kalshi's volume monster. Wang says perps did an order of magnitude more volume in their first month than any single prediction market has since launch.
  • A single CFTC order did the heavy lifting. The BTCPERP Order for Approval (29 May 2026, Reg 40.3) opened a template that lets Kalshi self-certify additional liquid digital-commodity perps — the regulatory queue, not the matching engine, is the moat.
  • Kalshi bans insider trading on market-structure grounds, not ethical ones: toxic informed flow makes market makers pull quotes, which destroys the liquidity that produces the signal. Hoffman argues the opposite case hard and neither concedes.
  • The stated ambition is "the largest exchange on the planet" — a CME or NYSE regulatory posture welded to consumer-growth acquisition, with the institutional climb running through bespoke market creation and dragging OTC structured products into lit markets.

🏛️ Kalshi Chose Litigation Over Offshore Arbitrage — And Wang Says That Was The Cheap Path

Hoffman opens by asking whether Kalshi is a crypto company at all. Wang's answer draws the boundary precisely: not an onchain company, but crypto is the second-largest business line behind sports, with what he claims is 90% market share in crypto predictions and order flow routed through Coinbase, Robinhood Crypto and Phantom 00:31–00:49.

"For prediction markets, we actually had to sue the CFTC."

The framing that follows is the load-bearing argument of the entire interview. Wang describes two paths available to anyone building a prediction market six or seven years ago: go offshore and take the unregulated route, or go onshore and "work with the regulators in the US" — which he characterises as "the really hard path," years of legal work while shipping a product in beta that you are not even allowed to market 04:40–05:04.

Crucially, Wang rejects the idea that the onshore path means docility. He points out that Kalshi pushed to legalise both prediction markets and perpetuals, and that in the prediction-markets case this meant suing its own regulator 09:35–09:49. He also argues Kalshi is structurally different from the other companies usually grouped with it: a team of around 180 people, which he estimates is "20 to 40x smaller in terms of headcount" than a Coinbase or a Circle, running what he calls insanely aggressive product velocity 09:14–09:35.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed. Kalshi sued the CFTC in KalshiEX LLC v. CFTC. Judge Jia Cobb (D.D.C.) ruled for Kalshi in September 2024, holding Congress had not authorised the CFTC's public-interest review used to bar election contracts. The D.C. Circuit denied the CFTC's stay motion on 2 October 2024, letting the markets go live for the 2024 election, and the CFTC voluntarily dismissed its appeal on terms agreed with Kalshi in May 2025. Source: CNBC (5 May 2025); KalshiEX LLC v. CFTC, No. 24-5205 (D.C. Cir. 2024) via Justia; Better Markets statement on the dismissal.

The steelman Hoffman asks for is answered in purely commercial terms. Wang concedes the offshore route genuinely is easier for onboarding crypto-natives — no KYC, VPN access, hoops those users have already jumped through 10:58–11:14. But he argues that user set is not the prize. The prize is the "99% TAM," the marginal user who has never touched crypto, and the only way to reach them is the set of channels that regulation unlocks: paid ads on Facebook and Instagram, billboards, partnerships with household consumer names, integration into large brokers and financial institutions, and presence on the App Store 11:15–12:10.

💡 Key InsightThe regulatory moat argument inverts the usual crypto framing. Wang is not saying compliance protects users; he is saying compliance is the only legal way to buy distribution at scale. Everything else in Kalshi's strategy — broker integrations, App Store presence, paid acquisition — is downstream of holding a DCM licence. 11:43

He adds a second-order point that is easy to miss: scale itself attracts scrutiny. "As you grow to a larger scale as well, you're obviously like under much more regulatory and compliance scrutiny" 12:04–12:16. The implication is that an offshore platform that succeeds eventually inherits the regulatory problem anyway, only without the licence.

🎯 Against ConsensusCrypto's dominant narrative treats regulatory engagement as a tax paid by slower incumbents. Wang argues the opposite — that for a consumer financial product, the licence is the growth channel, and that Kalshi's 180-person team out-ships far larger regulated firms while holding it. 09:14

⚖️ The Circle-vs-Tether Analogy Breaks Down On Product, Not On Posture

Hoffman lays out the comparison he and Ryan Sean Adams have used repeatedly: Kalshi is Circle — compliant, onshore, structurally advantaged by working with regulators — and Polymarket is Tether, growing aggressively in the offshore Eurodollar world precisely because it is not constrained 05:53–06:53.

Wang's pushback is sharper than a polite demurral. He argues the analogy fails at the product layer, not the regulatory one. Stablecoins sell the US dollar, and there is enormous latent demand for dollars in countries with weak local currencies — Tether simply found a bigger pain point, which is why its international growth has been "astronomical" 07:01–07:31.

Prediction markets have no equivalent universal product. "It's more about like each country and each type of really just group of people — they have different questions about the world that they care about" 07:42–07:53. The market itself has to be locally relevant, which means the offshore-versus-onshore axis explains far less than it does for dollar-denominated instruments.

He does concede partial validity on approach, then immediately complicates it: Kalshi is "much leaner and much more aggressive" than the regulated comparison set 09:07–09:14, which breaks the implicit assumption that the compliant player is the slow one.

Wang offers evidence that markets do cross borders when the underlying question is global: cricket markets found good international adoption, the World Cup was strong for international users, and crypto travels furthest of all because "everyone in the world knows about Bitcoin, everyone in the world knows about ETH" 08:02–08:45.

🔗 ConnectionCross-border market relevance → the international distribution strategy — this is why Kalshi's overseas play is broker partnerships that embed local market sets rather than a single global app. The product has to be localised in a way a dollar stablecoin never does. (08:27 → 05:25)

📈 Perps Are Kalshi's Volume Monster — And The Regulatory Queue Is The Moat

Hoffman presses on the obvious objection: perps are brutally competitive, with Hyperliquid and Lighter running "pretty insane technical chops" and hyper-optimising around the instrument 14:05–14:30.

Wang does not claim a technical edge. He claims parity plus access. On execution he says Kalshi can now absorb "a six figure order size and not experience like too much slippage," with more liquidity growth to come, and that fees are lower than Binance or Hyperliquid "at every tier currently" 15:49–20:49. Kalshi Pro — which he describes as the first official prediction-market trading terminal released by a prediction market — plus a separate perps pro interface are the professional surface 15:24–15:46.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed on Kalshi Pro. Kalshi announced its advanced trading terminal in July 2026 (press release 13 July 2026), targeting active traders, professional speculators and institutional desks, with advanced charting, a live public-trade feed and expanded order-book data. It remains in beta. Source: Kalshi press release via Business Wire / news.kalshi.com; Finance Magnates.

The regulatory mechanics matter more than the UI. Hoffman asks whether Bitcoin is still the only approved perp contract. Wang explains that Kalshi files under CFTC Regulation 40.3 for each new asset class, and that the Bitcoin approval "essentially approved crypto as an asset class for us," after which Kalshi lists additional crypto perps by self-certification while running CFTC-approved risk models 18:13–19:22. The current book he names: Bitcoin, ETH, HYPE, Solana, Zcash, NEAR, SUI, Dogecoin and XRP, with "over a dozen" crypto assets live 18:25–18:47.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Substantially right, with one important nuance. The CFTC issued an Order for Approval to KalshiEX for the BTCPERP contract on 29 May 2026, under CEA §5c(c)(4) and Regulation 40.3, following Kalshi's 28 May 2026 submission. But the Order's own scope language is narrower than Wang implies: the Commission stated the analysis is limited to BTCPERP "and similarly structured perpetual contracts that reference the spot price of bitcoin or other digital commodities that have deep, active, and continuous spot market trading," and expressly noted the perpetual design "may not be suitable for all asset classes," encouraging other perpetuals through the same 40.3 route. So the Bitcoin order created a template for liquid digital commodities, not a blanket asset-class pass. Source: CFTC Press Release 9240-26; CFTC Order, 29 May 2026.
⚡ TensionAt 18:13 Wang says Kalshi files 40.3 for "every single new asset class that we list," but at 19:01 he agrees Kalshi can now self-certify crypto perps. Both are true and they describe different rungs of the same ladder — 40.3 approval to open an asset class, 40.2 self-certification to fill it out — but the interview never separates them, and the distinction is exactly where a competitor's timeline advantage or disadvantage lives.

On leverage, Wang is unusually specific about the trade-off Kalshi accepts. Crypto perp leverage is capped around 6x, lower than offshore venues. His justification is empirical: Kalshi's analytics on onchain and other perp platforms show only 20–30% of users trade above that limit, and most traders sit between 2x and 4x 19:36–20:12. He signals that the upcoming non-crypto perps will carry much higher leverage limits, comparable to existing RWA perp platforms, on the grounds that metals are less volatile 20:12–20:36.

  • BTCPERP: approved 29 May 2026 by CFTC Order under §5c(c)(4) and Reg 40.3.
  • Kalshi crypto perp book named on air: BTC, ETH, HYPE, SOL, ZEC, NEAR, SUI, DOGE, XRP — "over a dozen" assets live 18:25.
  • Crypto perp leverage cap: ~6x. Wang's data: only 20–30% of users on other venues exceed it; modal range 2–4x 19:56.
  • Metals perps: gold and silver filed, expected "in a matter of weeks" from recording 16:24.
  • Deposit rails: fiat plus crypto/stablecoin in the US; crypto/stablecoin ONLY outside the US, across 140 countries 03:28.
  • CFTC filing tracker: https://www.cftc.gov/IndustryOversight/IndustryFilings

Wang's thesis for why a regulated latecomer wins perps rests on where the next frontier is. He argues around half of Hyperliquid's volume now comes from real-world assets, and that the centre of RWA liquidity is the United States — US retail and large US institutions who have never had regulated access to the perpetual's benefits (no roll, no expiries, cheaper carry) 16:42–17:32.

Hoffman probes the roadmap by reference to Lighter's stated plan — tokenized RWAs plus a spot market plus the perp, three ingredients that unlock baskets, strategies and optionality 21:04–21:41. Wang says spot is "definitely something we've considered," names margining in Bitcoin or non-cash collateral as the interesting version, and flags it as regulatory-unlock-dependent and something he is pushing internally 21:54–22:14. Near-term the roadmap is instead: incentive programs, deepening Kalshi Pro, and unlocking new asset classes starting with metals 22:16–23:51.

🎰 The Real Perps Differentiation Is Comingling Predictions Into The Trading Screen

The most product-specific stretch of the interview is Wang's description of what Kalshi's perps have that no competitor does. It is not matching-engine latency. It is the prediction market bolted into the perps UI.

Kalshi's prediction markets already price the odds of Bitcoin hitting a given level on hourly, daily, monthly and annual horizons. Wang says those distilled odds are surfaced inside the perps product — on the chart, in an insights section, as market-expectation stats — and that this has measurably helped first-time perp traders 24:37–25:08.

Alongside it sits Kalshi Social, a social/trading feed Wang compares to FOMO and to a product Robinhood recently released that he says looks similar to Kalshi's, plus a troll box on the perps interface that he says "gives me flashbacks to BitMEX... 2017, 2018" 24:11–24:36.

📜 AnalogThe BitMEX troll box comparison is not incidental. BitMEX's 2017–18 dominance was built on a retail-facing chat window bolted to a leveraged derivative — engagement as liquidity strategy. Kalshi is reviving that pattern with a CFTC licence instead of a Seychelles entity. 24:29

The interface decisions follow the same logic: perps are packaged as "up or down" instead of "long or short," and leverage is presented in terms Wang says are much more understandable to a mainstream user base 25:18–25:34. Hoffman asks whether there is a grand unified Kalshi. Wang's answer is that perps already have their own tab in both mobile and web apps, and the real remaining split is not predictions-versus-perps but simple-versus-pro, which Kalshi is working to make more seamless 26:23–27:04.

🔗 ConnectionUp/down packaging → the 6x leverage cap — both are the same decision applied twice. Kalshi is deliberately narrowing the instrument's expressiveness to widen the addressable user, which is the identical trade regulation forces on it externally. (25:18 → 19:56)

🕵️ Kalshi Polices Insider Trading For Market-Structure Reasons, Not Ethical Ones

Hoffman sets up the central tension of the entire category with unusual precision: prediction markets are accurate because they pay people with private information to reveal it through trades, funded by uninformed retail flow. Too permissive and retail concludes the game is rigged; too restrictive and "the market degrades into just being like a sentiment poll rather than actual news or actual information" 27:04–29:00.

Wang's answer starts from positioning. Kalshi is "a markets first company" building an exchange; better public information is a byproduct, not the mission 29:06–29:36. From there he draws what he calls a bright red line on insider trading 29:56–30:02.

His defence of that line is the most analytically interesting moment in the interview, and it is deliberately unsentimental:

"You just have to draw this line against insider trading if you want to have market integrity and healthy markets that thrive and scale."

The mechanism he gives: insider trading is banned in equities not primarily for ethical reasons but for market-structure ones. Once a venue carries heavy toxic flow from informed traders, market makers stop quoting and liquidity providers pull their quotes — "and then you won't have a signal anyways" 33:33–34:12. The signal that permissiveness is supposed to buy is destroyed by the illiquidity that permissiveness causes.

💡 Key InsightThis reframes the fairness debate as an adverse-selection problem. Retail fairness and price accuracy are not opposed goals that need balancing — under Wang's model they are the same goal, because market-maker participation is the thing that produces both. 33:43

Wang argues edge is still obtainable legitimately, and his example is unexpectedly concrete: a trader known as Grande Top 10, a music-chart YouTuber deep in Ariana Grande Billboard chart analysis, who has consistently found edge in Kalshi's music-chart markets by being embedded in the relevant communities and information sources 30:39–31:35. He also notes that building such systems is easier now "in the age of Claude, AI" 30:32.

On enforcement infrastructure: Kalshi maintains lists of politicians, campaign staff, sports-team employees and peripheral associates, drawing on the same commercial surveillance vendors that serve sports betting operators and equity-market insider-trading compliance 31:41–32:31. At the market level there are UI-layer guardrails — Wang says politicians and politically associated persons are barred from Kalshi's political markets while remaining free to trade sports, though he caveats he is "90% sure" and notes his own remit is crypto, commodities and perps 35:38–36:35.

⚡ TensionAt 29:56 Wang says Kalshi draws a "bright red line" on insider trading, but at 36:04 he cannot confirm whether the political-markets ban on politicians is categorical, saying he'd "have to check on the specific." The line is asserted with more confidence than its implementation is described — a gap worth noting given that political markets are where Kalshi's litigation win is most load-bearing.

He is candid about the ceiling: "there's no bulletproof solution to prevent insider trading in the stock market. Same thing applies in prediction markets" 36:53–37:11. The fallback is disclosure — flags before entering higher-risk markets, and per-market disclosures about insider-trading risk, how to report it and what Kalshi does to prevent it 37:34–38:01.

🧩 Nobody Has Defined What Insider Trading Means For A Prediction Market

Hoffman's best line of questioning is whether the securities-law definition of insider trading transfers cleanly to event contracts at all. He works through two cases at opposite poles.

The first is the 2024 election whale — the French trader who financed his own swing-state polls with precisely designed questions and took a very large Trump position on the result. Hoffman's verdict: "Is that insider trading? Absolutely not. Like not in the slightest. That is a very motivated trader just being very shrewd and clever" 38:16–39:03.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed, with the mechanism Hoffman describes. The trader known as "Théo" commissioned private polls that asked respondents who they thought their neighbours would vote for, rather than their own intention, on the theory that some Trump voters would not disclose their preference to a pollster. He traded through multiple Polymarket accounts (reports cite as many as 11) and profits were ultimately estimated at up to $85M. French regulators subsequently moved against Polymarket's availability in France. Source: Sherwood News; NBC News (Nov 2024); Yahoo Finance reporting on the revised $85M figure.

The second is a Google employee who traded on the release date of Google's next model — information Hoffman argues the corporation, not the employee, owned. His framing is a property-rights one rather than a securities one: the employee "sold information, sold IP that wasn't his" 39:06–39:59. Hoffman's complaint is that Kalshi is left "sticking its finger up in the air" and deciding by feel, when a CFTC rulemaking or an act of Congress would let platforms regulate less and give traders clarity 40:00–40:39.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Unverified as stated. Hoffman himself does not recall which platform the Google-employee trade occurred on, and no primary source for a specific enforcement action on those facts was located. Treat the example as an illustrative hypothetical rather than a documented case.

Wang's response is a straight endorsement of codification: Kalshi's public position is full support for clearer rules, which he says would largely codify the extra steps Kalshi already takes voluntarily to protect traders and market integrity 40:40–41:33. He characterises the company as "very pro-regulation when done the right way."

Hoffman then presses the genuinely contrarian case, and it is the sharpest disagreement of the episode:

"I do think that just like a little bit of insider trading is good too. Just a smidge."

He grounds it in Robin Hanson's originating argument for prediction markets — that this is precisely how information gets pulled into the world — and extends it to the second- and third-order structures built on top, naming futarchy as a design that needs exactly this financed information flow to function 42:34–43:26.

Wang's counter is historical and commercial rather than philosophical. Those information-maximalist prediction markets did exist: they were the dominant form before Kalshi scaled commercially over the past two to three years, and they lived as play-money experiments or academic and think-tank thought exercises 43:36–44:22. His conclusion is blunt — build for pure information extraction and "it probably won't look like a multi-billion dollar asset class that is like very liquid and traded by a lot of people," staying instead a nice thought experiment permanently constrained in scale 44:26–45:09.

🌐 Consensus CheckWang's position is now the industry-dominant one among regulated venues, and it is the position US regulators implicitly endorsed by letting Kalshi's litigation win stand. Hoffman's is the position most crypto-native prediction-market designers and the academic literature (Hanson, futarchy advocates) still hold. Neither has been resolved by rulemaking — the CFTC has not issued a prediction-market-specific insider-trading framework. 43:26
🧵 Running ThesisEvery Kalshi answer resolves toward scale over signal purity — insider-trading enforcement, 6x leverage caps, up/down packaging, onshore licensing. Status: confirmed. Wang never once argues a design choice on accuracy grounds alone; the argument is always that scale is a precondition for accuracy.

🏦 The Climb From Sports Book To CME Runs Through OTC And Structured Products

Hoffman frames the valuation gap that motivates the whole strategy. Prediction markets today look like an upgraded sports book, valued in the low tens of billions, which positions them as a FanDuel or DraftKings competitor. Everyone watching wants them to be a CME — three-digit billions, insurance companies and hedge funds using them, and by implication a large drop in sports as a share of volume as everything else grows 45:10–46:50.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Hoffman's valuation framing was accurate when the comparison was drawn but is now stale. Kalshi raised at $11B in November 2025, then $22B, and by June 2026 was reported seeking capital at roughly $40B with a possible 2027 IPO; Polymarket was last reported seeking funding at around $15B. The "$10–20 billion" range Hoffman uses 45:38 has been left behind on the Kalshi side. Source: TechCrunch (20 Nov 2025); CoinDesk (24 Jun 2026); DeFi Rate.

Wang's answer names the ambition outright:

"That is our ultimate goal — to be the largest exchange on the planet."

The positioning he gives is a hybrid: CME or NYSE on the regulatory axis, fast-shipping tech company on the product axis, aggressive consumer growth company on the acquisition axis. The differentiator he keeps returning to is owning the trader relationship directly — the thing he credits for Robinhood's and Interactive Brokers' growth — combined with a leading regulated position in what he calls the two newest instruments in traditional global finance, prediction markets and perpetual futures 46:51–48:16.

The institutional motion is already underway in several forms. Kalshi is in essentially all of the largest US broker platforms, and has plugged into the order and execution management systems that large hedge funds and institutions trade through (transcript renders this as "SCMs") 48:35–48:55. Block trades are growing. And there are hedging use cases that read as genuinely novel: an ice cream shop using weather markets, and dozens of bars hedging World Cup promotions — offering free drinks if the US won a given night's game and laying that risk off on Kalshi 48:56–49:33. Wang says these worked well, and that alongside them sit "seven to eight figure trades" and block trades on Kalshi's CLARITY Act odds 49:34–49:51.

💡 Key InsightThe bar promotion hedge is the most economically interesting example in the episode. It is a genuine commercial hedge against a non-financial operational risk, executed by a small business, at a size no OTC desk would ever quote. That is the actual mechanism by which a prediction market becomes infrastructure rather than a casino — and it is far more persuasive than the theoretical farmer-and-insurance example Hoffman raises at 55:27.

Hoffman's structural worry is that prediction markets are inherently a retail instrument: hedging is precise, event contracts are blunt, and the further up the capital stack you go the harder precise expression becomes 56:27–57:40. Wang's response has two parts.

First, retail-first adoption is symmetric across all new markets — you need early adopters and speculative capital to bootstrap liquidity before institutions can size in — and he argues Kalshi has now crossed that threshold 57:40–58:14.

Second, and more concretely, the bluntness objection is solved by bespoke market creation. Kalshi's first block trade came from a Houston-based environmental hedge fund that wanted to hedge whether a specific price for May California carbon allowance auctions would occur; a counterparty was found and multiple traders provided liquidity and priced it 58:17–58:49. Institutions can contact Kalshi and have a bespoke market spun up, which then becomes available to the world to price and provide liquidity against 58:49–59:19.

The strategic bet underneath this is the clearest statement of Kalshi's institutional thesis: take the OTC structured-products market — bilaterally negotiated deals in dark markets — and bring it into what Wang calls lit markets, openly traded marketplaces, which he claims increases the addressable market and activity while cutting the barrier to entry by an order of magnitude 59:20–60:00.

🔗 ConnectionBespoke market creation → the insider-trading regime — these are in direct tension. A market spun up at one institution's request, priced by that institution's counterparties, is exactly the structure where informational asymmetry is hardest to police, yet it is the mechanism Kalshi is betting its institutional growth on. The interview never connects the two. (58:49 → 29:56)

Wang is honest about what is missing. Margin on prediction markets is "probably our top requested feature" from institutions for event contracts and is still being built; in the meantime perps provide the margined, capital-efficient exposure institutions want without contract rolls 60:16–60:50. And he makes a category-specific argument for politics: those contracts cannot be entered anywhere else, and given how central politics has been to market behaviour over the past year, he argues any hedge fund needs some prediction-market position to achieve proper risk parameters or pricing efficiency 61:01–61:51.

📊 Wang Pushes Back Hard On The "Kalshi Is A Sports Book" Framing

Hoffman's premise throughout is that 70–80% of prediction-market volume is sports. Wang disputes that this describes Kalshi today, and does it with numbers.

His argument runs in four parts. Perps are entirely a non-sports category and did an order of magnitude more volume in their first month than any prediction market has since launch 50:02–50:16. Crypto prediction markets are, he says, 20–30% of total exchange volume on their own, excluding politics and economics 51:13–51:20. Commodities markets have just launched with the same market structure and UI, and grew "at a rate of 100x within the first few days" compared with the equivalent point in the crypto markets' launch 50:34–51:24. And political markets are being rebuilt as research products — indices that batch and weight related prediction markets, including a product he calls KPAL, described as an S&P 500 for the US political landscape 51:46–52:28.

His projection: if commodities reach another 20% category share, sports drops to 20–30% of exchange volume 51:24–51:44.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ The direction is right; the levels are contested. Third-party trackers have sports at 68–80% of Kalshi's notional volume through 2026, peaking above 90% in individual weeks (DeFi Rate had sports at 91.1% of notional for one week in April 2026, down from a September 2025 peak around 90% as a sustained level). Kalshi closed May 2026 at $17.91B notional, its ninth consecutive monthly record, and June 2026 at roughly $21.1B against Polymarket's $9.7B. Wang's "crypto is 20–30% of total exchange volume" is hard to reconcile with sports at 68–80% in the same period — at least one secondary aggregator puts crypto nearer 7% of Kalshi volume. The sports-share decline he predicts is real and visible in the data; the crypto share he claims is at the outer edge of what any public tracker supports. Source: DeFi Rate prediction-market volume trackers; TRM Labs (2026); Sportico; KuCoin research aggregation.
🌐 Consensus CheckNote the definitional trap in this dispute. "Exchange volume" may or may not include perps notional, which Wang says dwarfs individual prediction markets. Including perps mechanically shrinks sports' share; excluding them inflates it. Neither Wang nor the public trackers state which convention they use, and the gap between "7%" and "20–30%" is plausibly this and nothing more. 51:13

He also flags a genuine first: Kalshi was "first to rip a compute forward curve," building out compute markets as a category 64:26–64:32.

🚣 The Mirror-Image Deal War With Polymarket Cooled Because Kalshi Says It Won

Hoffman describes a pattern that ran for over a year: Kalshi and Polymarket announcing partnerships with xAI in the same week in 2025, both becoming official NHL prediction-market partners a day apart, both doing a pop-up grocery store thing. "If Kalshi does it, Polymarket does it. If Polymarket does it, then Kalshi does it" 61:51–62:37.

Wang's reaction is the most human moment in the episode. Hoffman says it must be infuriating to watch from the outside; Wang replies that it is "more infuriating to see that from the inside" 62:38–62:45.

His explanation is competitive rather than conspiratorial: it is what happens when two big companies push each other, "kind of like two teams like rowing their boats" — and it makes both perform better 62:46–63:05.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Partially confirmed. The NHL deal is exactly as described: on 22 October 2025 the NHL announced landmark multi-year partnerships with both Kalshi and Polymarket, making it the first major US league to partner with prediction markets. No primary source was located confirming same-week xAI partnership announcements by both platforms in 2025, so that specific example is unverified. Source: NHL.com; CNBC (22 Oct 2025); ESPN.

He says it has subsided over roughly the last six months, and gives a reason that is also a claim:

"We've really grown to be like four to five times larger, at least in crypto — we're 10 times larger than them in volumes."

The consequence, in his telling, is that Kalshi no longer competes as hard for deals because partners simply go with Kalshi, and the alternative is deals getting "overbid heavily" with "people just like burning money in the air" 63:28–63:50.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Overstated on the headline number, directionally right on the trend. Kalshi's June 2026 volume of $21.1B against Polymarket's $9.7B is roughly 2.2x, not 4–5x; DeFi Rate's tracker put Kalshi near 73% market share against Polymarket's 27% in early July 2026. As recently as March 2026 the two were near parity at roughly $12B each. The 10x crypto-specific claim could not be independently verified from public trackers. Kalshi is clearly ahead and pulling away; the multiple Wang quotes is not supported by public monthly volume data. Source: DeFi Rate; CoinDesk; TRM Labs.

He also lists where Kalshi has separated on substance rather than marketing: focus on perps, institutional adoption, crypto and commodities markets, being first to a compute forward curve, and getting its perps platform regulated and released while — he claims — Polymarket's has not yet left beta 63:53–64:42.

Hoffman raises a rumour to close: that third parties like sports leagues could smell the competition and bid the two platforms against each other, extracting from both. Wang says he was not part of the NHL deal and will not speak to it, but confirms the general dynamic — "if you have two job offers, you're going to like try to make them give you a higher offer" — and says Kalshi has seen much less of it in the past six months 65:07–66:26. He notes it reached absurd levels at the small end, with creators who had 20,000 followers trying to play the two platforms against each other 66:32–66:46.

📜 AnalogThis is the standard duopoly-formation pattern from ride-sharing and food delivery — matched announcements, bid-up exclusivity deals, marketing spend that transfers value to partners rather than users — resolving the same way it usually does, with the leader's cost of competing for each deal falling as its share lead becomes obvious to counterparties. 63:28

What It Includes

Bitcoin, ETH, HYPE, Solana, Zcash, NEAR, SUI, Dogecoin and XRP, described as "over a dozen" crypto assets 18:25–18:47. The Evidence

Wang claims six-figure order sizes clear without excessive slippage 15:56, fees lower than Binance or Hyperliquid at every tier 20:41, leverage capped near 6x with data showing only 20–30% of users on comparable venues exceed it 19:56, and first-month volume an order of magnitude above any prediction market since launch 50:02. The "first regulated perps exchange in the US" framing 02:28 is contestable — Coinbase self-certified perpetual-style futures in July 2025. Kalshi's genuine first is CFTC approval of a true perpetual design.

🟣 Kalshi Prediction Markets — The Litigated Franchise

The event-contract business Kalshi sued its own regulator to keep, now diversifying hard away from sports into crypto, commodities and political indices.

What It Includes

Sports, crypto (hourly, daily, monthly and annual Bitcoin price odds), politics, economics, commodities, weather, music charts and entertainment (24:41, 30:39, 50:34, 48:56). Kalshi says it holds 90% market share in crypto predictions specifically 00:31.

The Evidence

Judge Jia Cobb's September 2024 ruling and the CFTC's May 2025 voluntary dismissal of its appeal left Kalshi as the first regulated election prediction market in the US. Volume reached $21.1B notional in June 2026 against Polymarket's $9.7B. Wang claims crypto alone is 20–30% of exchange volume 51:13 — a figure that sits above what public trackers support.

The Caveat

Third-party trackers still show sports at 68–80%+ of Kalshi notional. Wang's diversification story is directionally supported but his crypto share is at the outer edge of the evidence.

🟣 Kalshi Pro — The Institutional Front Door

The first trading terminal released by a prediction market itself, launched July 2026 and still in beta.

What It Includes

Advanced charting, a live feed of public trades, expanded order-book data, management of resting orders and multi-leg positions across multiple markets, and TradingView integrations on the perps side (15:24, 22:51–23:17).

The Evidence

Announced 13 July 2026 via Business Wire, explicitly targeting active traders, professional speculators and institutional desks. Wang positions it as parity with traditional brokerages like Moomoo or Tastytrade for the same user set 23:12.

🟣 Kalshi Commodities Markets — The Fastest-Growing Category

Kalshi copied its crypto market structure onto gold, silver and oil, and says the launch curve was 100x steeper.

What It Includes

Gold, silver and oil prediction markets using the same market structure, user interface and growth motions developed for crypto 50:34–51:24. Gold and silver perpetual futures were filed and expected within weeks of recording 16:24.

The Evidence

A 100x comparison against a launch-week baseline is a growth-rate artifact, not a level. It tells you the crypto launch started small, not that commodities are now large.

Kalshi's answer to the bluntness objection: batch and weight related political prediction markets into a tradeable index.

What It Includes

An index product that batches multiple political prediction markets together with weightings, allowing traders to express a view on the aggregate US political landscape and observe how macro and political developments move the index 51:46–52:28. Wang compares the research posture to Bloomberg (a second comparison name is garbled in the transcript).

The Evidence

Wang names KPAL directly on air and describes the S&P 500 analogy himself. He pairs it with the argument that politics contracts are unavailable anywhere else, making Kalshi the only venue for that hedge 61:08.

🟣 Block Trades & Bespoke Market Creation — The OTC Migration Play

Institutions can commission a custom Kalshi market, which then becomes publicly priceable — the mechanism Kalshi is betting will pull structured products out of dark markets.

What It Includes

Institution-initiated bespoke market creation, block trade execution, and integration with the order/execution management systems institutions already use (58:49, 48:44). Weather markets used as commercial hedges by an ice cream shop and dozens of bars hedging World Cup promotions 48:56–49:33.

The Evidence

Kalshi's first block trade was a Houston-based environmental hedge fund hedging a specific price level for May California carbon allowance auctions; counterparties and liquidity providers priced it 58:17–58:49. Wang also cites seven- and eight-figure trades on Kalshi's CLARITY Act odds 49:34.

The Gap

Margin on event contracts is the top institutional request and is not yet shipped 60:16. Until it exists, capital efficiency pushes institutions toward Kalshi's perps instead.

🟣 International Distribution — Brokers Plus A Stablecoin-Only Rail

Kalshi's overseas strategy is to embed inside each country's largest broker rather than ship a global app, funded entirely by crypto deposits.
