Should peer-to-peer crypto transactions be regulated differently online vs. in-person?

Source Law of Code · 2025-04-29 · 38 min
Domain Law, Policy & Financial Regulation
Reliability Expert explainer — a former SEC enforcement and legislative-affairs lawyer arguing from her own published work, with the historical claims checkable against the archival record.
Content Type Expert interview
Full Mode

⚡ The Bottom Line

  • The argument turns on a fact most people get wrong: handing someone a paper stock certificate for cash is already a lawful, unregistered securities transaction. Nothing in the federal securities laws requires a broker. TuongVy Le's question is why the identical economic trade should become regulated because it now runs through a smart contract.
  • Technology neutrality, as she states it, is narrower than the slogan. If the same economic activity is permissible physically, new technology should not by itself trigger additional regulatory burdens. She is explicit that plenty of DeFi activity does fall inside the perimeter on its own merits.
  • The test she proposes is control and discretion, not scale. Code that merely enables communication or execution differs in kind from an intermediary holding assets. Scale and automation alone, she argues, should not be enough.
  • The strongest material is historical. The intermediated securities market was built to fix the late-1960s paperwork crisis, and the contemporaneous SEC and congressional record treats it as a necessary evil pending better technology — which reverses the burden of proof in the whole debate.
  • This episode is securities law, not money transmission. The title's online-versus-in-person question has the opposite answer under the Bank Secrecy Act, where FinCEN treats in-person and online peer-to-peer exchangers identically — and both as money transmitters.

🔑 Key Claims (Ranked)

1. Unintermediated securities transactions are already lawful and unsurveilled ↳ Evidence: "It's perfectly legal for me to say, hand you a paper stock certificate, and you hand me a lot of cash." 02:18 ↳ Context: Almost all trading now runs through brokers, so people infer a legal requirement that does not exist in the statute.

2. Capability to regulate is not the same as authority to regulate ↳ Evidence: "Just because new technology enables the government to regulate something doesn't mean that they retain the same justification or legal authority to do so." 03:30 ↳ Context: Van Loon v. Treasury is the judicial version — the Fifth Circuit held immutable smart contracts are not IEEPA "property" because they cannot be owned.

3. The third-party doctrine's reach is a function of market architecture, not constitutional principle ↳ Evidence: The doctrine expanded as life moved online and nearly all online activity came to involve regulated intermediaries. 04:14 ↳ Context: Every intermediary the 1970s settlement fix inserted is also a point at which financial data is voluntarily shared and therefore constitutionally unprotected.

4. Where DeFi touches securities law is the exchange and broker definitions, and control is the right test ↳ Evidence: Facilitating securities transactions can make you an exchange under Exchange Act § 3(a)(1); Le asks whether the protocol has control, governance or discretion over trades. 06:47 ↳ Caveat: She concedes the regulatory fear is rational — DeFi can replace parts of the financial system with nobody to register, examine or sue. 07:45 ↳ Context: The SEC's 2022 Rule 3b-16 proposal would have swept in "communication protocol systems"; it was reopened for comment in 2023 and never adopted.

5. The intermediated market was designed as a stopgap ↳ Evidence: "The system we have now was always meant to be a temporary solution... a necessary evil until the technology existed to create something better." 19:13 ↳ Context: NYSE daily volumes quadrupled between 1960 and 1968; roughly 160 member firms failed; the fix was the DTC, the NSCC and the Securities Acts Amendments of 1975.

6. Importing traditional intermediaries into crypto can manufacture risk rather than reduce it ↳ Evidence: Requiring a broker alongside a crypto exchange adds principal-agent risk and forces customers to trust two third parties instead of one. 22:02 ↳ Context: Transfer agents and delayed settlement are likewise solving problems the ledger already solves.

7. Self-regulation is a serious model, not a dodge ↳ Evidence: Acheson's The Lobster Gangs of Maine — informal territorial rules enforced by social sanction, producing one of the world's better-managed fisheries. Robinson adds bar associations as the professional analogue. 10:15 ↳ Caveat: Neither example resolves what supplies the backstop for a protocol with no members.

8. Some crypto risks are genuinely new, but existing law may already reach the harms ↳ Evidence: Irreversibility, key loss, hard forks and 51% attacks are real; but common law fraud statutes already offer recourse, and a court can order a token returned. 26:56 ↳ Context: A cash-for-goods trade cannot be undone either without going to court.

9. FIT21's flaw was classifying by provenance rather than by risk ↳ Evidence: Hinging status on how an asset was acquired and whether it was issuer-affiliated produced "bifurcated and fragmented markets." 28:37 ↳ Context: The CLARITY drafting that followed moved toward her preferred framing — information asymmetry and manipulation as the organising risks.

10. The fix for regulatory whiplash is statutory, not personnel ↳ Evidence: Le calls for adding the promotion of innovation to the SEC's mission alongside capital formation, investor protection and market integrity. 33:40 ↳ Caveat: The three-part mission is the Commission's own longstanding description rather than a single codified mandate, so a fourth limb needs real drafting.

💬 Best Quotes

"If the same economic activity is permissible in the physical world, then the use of new technology to facilitate that same activity should not by itself suddenly trigger additional regulatory burdens." 03:43 — Le's technology-neutrality principle, stated almost verbatim from her Fortune op-ed four days earlier.
"The system we have now was always meant to be a temporary solution, right? Like no one wanted this." 19:13 — On the archival record behind the intermediated securities market.
"There really is a difference between infrastructure that aggregates liquidity and makes markets like NASDAQ and on the other hand autonomous software that lets users transact peer-to-peer." 07:16 — The line she wants the exchange definition to track.
