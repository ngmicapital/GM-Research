Executive Summary

  • The entire American prediction-market conflict reduces to a chain with two links, and only the second is genuinely contested. Link one asks whether an event contract satisfies the Commodity Exchange Act's definition of a "swap" in § 1a(47) — payment contingent on the occurrence of an event with financial, economic or commercial consequence. Link two asks whether § 2(a)(1)(A)'s grant of exclusive CFTC jurisdiction over swaps traded on a designated contract market displaces state gambling law. Josh Sterling, who ran the CFTC division that oversees these exchanges, puts both answers at yes and calls the reasoning textual rather than clever. 74:59–76:19
  • Congress gave the CFTC a discretionary veto over event contracts, and the agency's implementing regulation turned it into something closer to a prohibition. CEA § 5c(c)(5)(C) — the "special rule" — says the Commission may determine that a contract involving gaming, terrorism, assassination, war or unlawful activity is contrary to the public interest. Regulation 40.11, adopted in 2011, says a registered entity shall not list anything that "involves, relates to, or references" those categories. That single drafting choice is the states' strongest argument and the reason the June 2026 rulemaking exists. (63:19–68:16, 124:55–125:13)
  • Prediction markets in America were never outlawed out of existence; they were made unenforceable, and then abandoned by the institutions that had been quietly settling them. Vischer v. Yates stripped election wagers of any court remedy in New York in 1814, which forced private escrow and pushed the trade into the Wall Street curb market; in May 1924 the New York Stock Exchange and the Curb Market both barred members from election betting, and the supply collapsed. Scientific polling then filled the forecasting vacuum after the Literary Digest's 1936 failure and Gallup's success. 33:33–37:57
  • The Iowa Electronic Markets asked the CFTC for permission in 1992 and got a no-action letter, and in doing so set the jurisdictional premise everything since has been argued on. The Division of Trading and Markets wrote that the interest at issue "more closely resembl[ed] a futures contract than a stock" — CFTC territory, not SEC territory. Thirty-four years later, states, tribes and gambling incumbents are attacking precisely that premise. 45:38–46:52
  • The honest distinction between a sportsbook and a designated contract market is structural, and it sits on the back end. A book quotes the line, takes the other side, manages its own exposure and earns an embedded margin. A DCM runs a central limit order book, holds no directional position, and charges a fee proportional to the contract's variance — 7% of price times one minus price, rounded to the cent — that pays the same whoever wins. Thania Charmani argues courts look to the economic reality of a transaction, and that these two are not the same transaction. (92:46–98:12, 99:36–100:26)
  • The states escalate through four distinct instruments, and taxation is the fallback when litigation looks lost. Cease-and-desist letters from at least fourteen states, civil actions by gaming commissions and attorneys general, criminal charges in Arizona, and then prohibitive taxes — Kentucky's 14.25% levy on transaction fees drew a CFTC counter-suit. Stefan Schropp's view is blunt: if you cannot regulate something out of existence, taxing it out of existence runs into the same supremacy-clause wall. (73:30–74:10, 87:36–88:27)
  • The June 2026 proposal is the CFTC's answer to the question the courts have been fighting over, delivered while they are still fighting. It ties "involve" to the contract's settlement trigger, defines "gaming" as the game rather than the wager on it, deletes "relates to" and "references," and publishes the factors the Commission will weigh. Brad Bourque's assessment is that it strips out the core of the states' litigation argument; Michael Passalacqua, adviser to the Chairman, frames the problem being solved as ambiguity. (71:54–72:36, 129:56–131:28)

⚖️ Every lawsuit in this field reduces to the same two-link chain, and the fight is over the second link

Josh Sterling, who spent his CFTC career as Director of the Division of Market Oversight before joining Milbank and now acts for Kalshi across multiple matters, was asked to state the core question in the litigation. He gave two, in order.

"I think there's two. One is really the foundational question is does the commodity exchange act give exclusive jurisdiction over DCMs to the CFTC?" 74:59

The second, he said, is whether these contracts are swaps at all. His answer to both is yes, and the reason is textual rather than doctrinal. Section 1a(47) of the Commodity Exchange Act, added by Dodd-Frank in 2010, sweeps in any agreement, contract or transaction where payment is dependent on "the occurrence, non-occurrence, or the extent of the occurrence of an event or contingency associated with a potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence." An event contract is, by construction, exactly that instrument. Section 2(a)(1)(A) then grants the Commission exclusive jurisdiction over swaps executed on a designated contract market.

🔍 Source Verified✅ CEA § 1a(47)(A)(ii) reaches any agreement providing for payment "dependent on the occurrence, nonoccurrence, or the extent of the occurrence of an event or contingency associated with a potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence." Sub-clause (A)(v) separately captures "any combination or permutation of, or option on" the preceding categories — the textual basis for treating parlays as swaps. Source: 7 U.S.C. § 1a(47).

Sterling's point about exclusivity is that the statute does not require a magic word. Preemption analysis is often a search for congressional intent in murky drafting; here Congress used the word "exclusive," and Sterling argues there is consequently no presumption against preemption to overcome, because the presumption operates where the law is silent or ambiguous rather than where it is explicit. 75:41–76:19

He is also candid about how the other side proceeds — starting from the intuition that trading sports contracts on a federally licensed exchange simply cannot be right, and working backwards.

"I'm going to apply interpretive canons. I'm going to apply doctrines. I'm going to do everything I can to get around the words of the statute." 76:44

The canon he is parodying is the elephants-in-mouseholes principle — that Congress does not hide major policy decisions in minor statutory provisions — deployed to argue that Congress would not have legalised nationwide sports wagering through a definitional clause in a financial-crisis statute. Sterling's response is that § 1a(47) is not a mousehole: it is the operative definition of an entire regulatory category, written broadly on purpose and drafted to keep growing, since it expressly reaches instruments that "now are, or in the future come to be, known to the trade as swaps."

Thania Charmani, a litigation partner at Winston Taylor, makes the same argument from the other direction — by narrowing what is actually in dispute. The exchanges, she says, are not claiming that Congress handed the CFTC the gambling industry.

"That should be the end of the discussion. That's the truth. These cases have one question presented before the courts." 65:35

Her framing is that the Commodity Exchange Act granted the CFTC exclusive jurisdiction over transactions on designated contract markets whether or not the underlying subject matter involves gaming, and that Congress anticipated the problem by writing the special rule rather than carving jurisdiction back to the states. On that reading the states are not defending a jurisdictional boundary; they are contesting one Congress already drew.

💡 Key InsightEvery link in the chain is load-bearing, which is why the industry's opponents need only win once. If sports event contracts are not swaps, exclusive jurisdiction never attaches. If they are swaps but exclusivity runs only against other federal agencies, the state laws survive alongside. If both links hold, the states lose everything except whatever the CFTC chooses to prohibit under the special rule. The host's summary: "every chain in this link is necessary." 88:45

That third possibility is the states' principal fallback — that "exclusive" in § 2(a)(1)(A) means exclusive as against the SEC and other federal regulators, not the states, and that because gambling is among the most traditional state regulatory domains, displacing it requires an unambiguous statement. 85:41–86:13 Charmani's counter runs through impossibility: the two regimes do not merely differ, they cannot both be satisfied.

🌐 Consensus CheckThat is the classic formulation of conflict preemption — where compliance with both federal and state law is impossible, or state law obstructs Congress's purposes, the state law yields under the Supremacy Clause. Her practical point is that a DCM cannot simultaneously run one national order book under CFTC core principles and satisfy fifty divergent state licensing regimes. The host notes that four preemption doctrines are available and that conflict and obstacle preemption are the ones most obviously engaged. 80:41–82:17

📜 Prediction markets did not die of prohibition — they died of unenforceability

The history matters because the mechanism by which prediction markets vanished from American life was a legal one that maps onto the present dispute.

"We should shut down this market. Or you could say maybe we want to regulate this and sort of, you know, tamp down some of the bad stuff and hopefully the good stuff will rise up later." 06:10

That is Koleman Strumpf — an economics professor at Wake Forest who has taught a course on prediction markets since 2008 — describing the choice a regulator faces when a young market misbehaves, and framing the present moment as analogous to the equities market of the 1920s: visibly full of abuse, yet worth regulating rather than abolishing. He and Paul Rhode traced organised political betting to fifteenth-century Italy, where wagering on papal conclaves was an old custom by 1503, covering not only who would be elected but when the conclave would end and how long the new pope would reign. In March 1591 Pope Gregory XIV banned it outright — his motive, Strumpf explains, being less gambling morality than what a market on "will we have a new pope next year" actually prices, which is one specific man's death. 02:19–02:36

🔍 Source Verified✅ Gregory XIV's prohibition was issued by the bull Cogit nos in March 1591, imposing excommunication on anyone wagering on the outcome or duration of a papal election or on the creation of cardinals, and banning the practice in the Papal States. The underlying research is Paul W. Rhode and Koleman Strumpf, The Long History of Political Betting Markets: An International Perspective (NBER Working Paper 14377). Source: nber.org/papers/w14377.
📜 AnalogThe ban did not end papal betting; it moved it out of sight, where it became more opaque and more dangerous for those still doing it. The host draws the line to the modern pattern in which American restrictions push activity offshore — the argument Bobby Denault makes at the close of the episode about venues that KYC nobody. (02:36–02:54, 169:21)

The English strand runs through eighteenth-century London, where political betting took place in gentlemen's clubs, coffee houses and down at the docks — one of those coffee houses, Jonathan's, became the London Stock Exchange. The practice arrived in America with early settlers, encouraged by politicians who asked supporters to bet publicly because a visible wager signalled confidence, and by the late nineteenth century it had centralised in New York, with the Times, the Sun and the World carrying near-daily quotes in the 1896 through 1924 cycles — the Times writing in 1924 that "these Wall Street odds represent the consensus of a large body of extremely impartial opinion that talks with money." (08:02–10:14, 33:00)

The abuses were structurally identical to those regulators worry about now. Ahead of the 1832 election the New York attorney general — son of the sitting vice president — placed more than a hundred bets on the outcome, and in early 1840 it was reported that "some heavy bets were made between members of the House on the approaching presidential election": legislators who could withdraw at will, holding positions on their own races. 10:40–11:15

🔗 ConnectionNineteenth-century legislators betting on their own elections → Kalshi's 2026 name-matching controls. The specific conduct the historical record documents is the exact conduct Kalshi's enforcement team now blocks at onboarding, by matching KYC identity against contract strike names. The abuse did not stop being possible; it stopped being possible on a venue that knows who its customers are. (11:15, 14:25)

The states' first response was not to prohibit the market but to disenfranchise the bettor: several barred anyone who wagered on an election from voting in it. The perverse incentive was promptly exploited — in 1864 Abraham Lincoln encouraged opposing Democrats in swing states to wager, disqualifying their votes. 26:25–27:03

The decisive legal fact is older and quieter. In 1814 a New York court held election wagers unenforceable in the civil courts — you could not sue to collect your winnings.

🔍 Source Verified✅ The case is Vischer v. Yates, 11 Johns. 23 (N.Y. Sup. Ct. 1814), an action against a stakeholder to recover money deposited on a wager on the election of governor. The rule was reaffirmed in 1830 on the ground that all gambling on election results "would have a pernicious influence and, therefore, cannot be sanctioned by law." The host's account of the holding and its effect is accurate.
💡 Key InsightUnenforceability did not kill the market — it institutionalised it. Because bettors could not sue to collect, someone had to hold the stakes and guarantee payment, and Wall Street firms took that role. That need for private escrow is precisely what drew the trade into the New York Curb Exchange, where NYSE members were eager participants. Denying a market the courts does not remove the demand for settlement; it relocates the settlement function to whoever will perform it. 33:54–34:31

Which sets up the extinction event. By 1924 Wall Street had become acutely self-conscious about the reputational line between investment and speculation — a live concern, since a bet on how many cars Ford will sell is not obviously different in kind from a bet on Ford's share price. In May 1924, a presidential election year, both the New York Stock Exchange and the New York Curb Market passed formal resolutions barring members from election gambling. 34:43–35:21

The combination was fatal. The government would not enforce these contracts, and now the institutions that had been enforcing them privately would not either. What remained went underground, and the demand for forecasting was met by public polling. From 1916 the Literary Digest ran a national straw poll that became the most trusted forecasting tool in American politics, mailing twenty million postcards by 1932 — a self-selected mass survey biased toward its own readership, a flaw that did not surface until it mattered. In 1936 the Digest predicted Alf Landon; George Gallup, using a far smaller but statistically adjusted representative sample, predicted Roosevelt and explained why. Roosevelt won by the largest electoral college landslide on record, and the Digest folded two years later. 35:57–37:57

💡 Key InsightThe framing most people carry is backwards. Market-based forecasting was the historical norm and polling the newcomer; Gallup's 1936 vindication did not displace a primitive method with a scientific one so much as supply a substitute for a method already legislated and self-regulated out of availability. Modern prediction markets are not a novelty competing with polling — they are the older technology returning to a niche it was pushed out of. 37:57–38:09

Strumpf is unusually honest about having doubted the historical markets would work at all: placing himself in 1904, he asked what information a bettor could even have used, with no scientific polls, no radio, no television. The record says they worked anyway, in the sense economists care about. 39:28–39:57

"They basically are, you know, what an economist would say is was well calibrated when they said a candidate has a 60% chance of winning." 40:02

Calibration is the demanding test: across the elections where the market said sixty per cent, the favourite won about sixty per cent of the time — a far stronger claim than "the favourite usually won." The markets also carried magnitude, identifying Harding's 1920 landslide months out. 40:02–41:01

The modern revival began at the University of Iowa in the 1980s, where Professor Robert Forsythe and two colleagues wondered, reportedly over a three-beer lunch, whether market-based forecasting might beat polling. They ran a political stock market across nine elections between 1988 and 1991 and beat the polls every time; in 1988 the Iowa Electronic Markets forecast George H.W. Bush at 53.2% of the popular vote against an actual 53.4%, and over the following decades they outperformed polls roughly 74% of the time. Iowa also pioneered the binary structure the industry still uses — a contract paying one dollar if the event occurs and nothing if it does not. (41:20–42:36, 47:07)

By 1992 the researchers wanted to open participation beyond state lines and were unsure whether they were committing a federal crime. Their reasoning is the origin point of the modern jurisdictional question. The Commodity Exchange Act regulates instruments relating to commodities, and § 1a(9) defines "commodity" not merely as a list of tangible goods but as extending to all "services, rights, and interests" in which contracts for future delivery are presently or in the future dealt in — an open-ended clause, and the reason the CFTC's remit grew from agricultural goods to interest rates, foreign exchange and swaps. They asked, and the Commission's staff said no action was necessary. 43:43–45:16

🔍 Source Verified✅ The Iowa Electronic Markets have operated since 1992 under a no-action letter from the CFTC's Division of Trading and Markets. A no-action letter is a written assurance from agency staff that it will not recommend enforcement so long as the activity stays within the described parameters; it binds the issuing division, not the Commission, and — as PredictIt would later discover — can be withdrawn. The relief rested on the market's small scale, its research purpose and a cap on individual investment, which the host reports at $500, and the letter characterised the interest at issue as "more closely resembl[ing] a futures contract than a stock." 45:53–46:52
🔗 ConnectionThe 1992 letter → every case now pending. The reason the CFTC rather than the SEC regulates this field traces to that single line about futures versus stock. The states, the tribes and the sportsbook incumbents are now attacking a jurisdictional allocation settled informally in a three-page staff letter to a professor. 46:52

The intervening timeline runs through the Hollywood Stock Exchange in 1996, Intrade and its sports-focused sister TradeSports from 1999 — the first for-profit venues, noted internationally in 2003 for correctly pricing the capture of Saddam Hussein — and the internal forecasting markets at HP, Google and Microsoft through the 2000s. 47:21–48:15

🧾 Dodd-Frank pulled event contracts into federal law, and Congress wrote its own escape valve on the way

The structural change came in 2010, and it came for reasons that had nothing to do with sports.

After the financial crisis, Congress moved to regulate event-contingent derivatives that had sat largely outside the perimeter. The instrument everyone remembers is the credit default swap: an agreement paying a binary amount if a specified credit event occurs. A prediction market contract is the same species, which is why the definitional expansion caught it. Dodd-Frank added the definition of "swap" to the Commodity Exchange Act as paragraph 1a(47); before that, "swap" was not a defined term in the Act at all. The clause that matters is the one quoted earlier — payment dependent on the occurrence, non-occurrence or extent of occurrence of an event or contingency associated with a potential financial, economic or commercial consequence. 48:15–49:38

💡 Key InsightThe change was consequential because it made federal coverage automatic rather than elective. Once an event contract satisfies § 1a(47) and trades on a designated contract market, exclusive jurisdiction under § 2(a)(1)(A) follows without any further agency decision — and the state law that would otherwise apply is displaced without anyone having to argue about it. The host's formulation is that exclusive jurisdiction "follows automatically." 62:07

The obvious objection is legislative-history-based: this language answered a credit crisis and cannot have been meant to legalise betting on the Super Bowl. The episode goes back to the Congressional Record, where the exchange is unusually direct. Senator Dianne Feinstein asked Senator Blanche Lincoln — then chair of the Agriculture, Nutrition and Forestry Committee and author of the event contract provision — whether the CFTC would have power to deem a contract a gaming contract if its predominant use is speculative rather than hedging. Lincoln's recorded answer:

"That is our intent. The commission needs the power to and should prevent derivative contracts that are contrary to the public interest because they exist predominantly to enable gambling through supposed 'event contracts'." 52:40

She went further, naming examples: it would be easy to construct an event contract around the Super Bowl, the Kentucky Derby or the Masters, and such contracts "would not serve any real commercial purpose. Rather, they would be used solely for gambling." 52:57

Daniel Wallach, the sports betting and gaming lawyer who founded Wallach Legal, cites this colloquy as evidence that Congress never intended sports contracts to sit on federally regulated exchanges, and notes that the CFTC read "gaming" consistently with Lincoln's approach when it adopted Regulation 40.11 in 2011 — a unanimous adoption whose twenty-page release referred specifically to the legislative history on sporting events. 53:31–54:23

The host's reading turns on a modal verb. Lincoln said the Commission "needs the power to" prevent such contracts; the statute says the Commission may determine them contrary to the public interest. Granting an agency power to prohibit something is a grant of jurisdiction over it, not a prohibition. 54:26–54:56

🎯 Against ConsensusWallach makes the sharpest version of the opposing case, and it is not primarily about text. If Dodd-Frank had legalised nationwide sports betting in 2010, the entire gambling industry would not have spent the following eight years litigating to overturn PASPA. His argument is that a sports betting regulatory scheme that nobody in the industry noticed for eight years is not a scheme at all, and that accepting Kalshi's reading would retroactively undercut the reasoning of Murphy v. NCAA. 148:14–149:00

Sterling's response to the legislative-history argument is procedural. Two senators said something in a colloquy inserted into the record; then the whole Senate passed a bill and the President signed it, and what the bill did was give the CFTC jurisdiction plus the ability to make a public-interest determination — not to leave the question with the states.

"So they said that and then the Congress said, okay, well let's let the CFTC hand, not the states." 56:22

He adds a detail that cuts against the standard use of the colloquy: Senator Lincoln, with Senator Chambliss and others, later filed an amicus brief in the Third Circuit case against New Jersey, stating that what Congress meant to do in Dodd-Frank was cover a wide range of instruments and ensure uniform federal regulation. If the argument is that we should listen to what a senator said, the same senator has since said the opposite of what her 2010 statement is used to prove. 56:34–57:06 Charmani puts it more flatly: the colloquy "might have been how the senator wishes the CFTC acted, but that doesn't take it away from the CFTC." 57:56–58:15

The parlay question stress-tests the text, because a parlay is where sportsbook economics concentrate and where the hedging rationale is least plausible. Sterling's answer:

"I think the fundamental premise is a swap plus a swap is a swap." 59:09

The statutory basis is the combination-and-permutation clause. His broader point is that there is no motivation test in the Commodity Exchange Act — nothing requires a trader to have a hedging purpose, and no contract must be capable of providing a hedge to be lawfully listed. A market's value is not exhausted by the two people on either side of a trade; it also generates information useful to everyone watching. 59:42–60:41

📋 The implementing regulation says more than the statute does, and that gap is where the litigation lives

The special rule is short, and every word in it has been fought over.

CEA § 5c(c)(5)(C) authorises the Commission to determine that an event contract is contrary to the public interest if it involves any of a set of enumerated activities: activity unlawful under federal or state law, terrorism, assassination, war, gaming, or a similar activity designated by rule. On that determination the contract may not be listed by a DCM or cleared by a derivatives clearing organisation. (63:19–63:42, 123:22–124:08)

Brad Bourque, policy counsel at the Hyperliquid Policy Center, identifies three features of the text that the debate routinely skips.

"The statute says that the CFTC may determine. So it's a discretionary authority. It's not some sort of like de facto ban." 124:14

The second is temporal: review is authorised "in connection with the listing" of a contract, so the Commission cannot make a blanket advance determination that a category is off limits — a DCM lists, and only then can review begin. The third is that both operative terms, "involve" and "gaming," are left undefined. 124:24–124:55

Then comes the regulation. In 2011 the CFTC adopted Regulation 40.11 to implement the special rule, and it differs from the statute materially.

🔍 Source Verified✅ 17 C.F.R. § 40.11(a) provides that a registered entity "shall not list for trading or accept for clearing on or through the registered entity" any agreement, contract, transaction or swap based upon an excluded commodity that "involves, relates to, or references terrorism, assassination, war, gaming, or an activity that is unlawful under any State or Federal law," or a similar activity the Commission determines to be contrary to the public interest. Paragraph (c) sets a 90-day review process during which the Commission requests that the registered entity suspend listing or trading, with an order due no later than 90 days after review commences. Source: law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/40.11.

Two differences. The regulation is written as a command to the exchange rather than an authority reserved to the Commission, and it triples the trigger: "involves, relates to, or references" instead of "involves." A contract that merely references gaming is caught, whether or not gaming is what settles it.

💡 Key InsightThat drafting choice inverted who bears the burden. Under the statute a contract is listable until the Commission determines otherwise through a public-interest review; under the regulation as written, a contract referencing an enumerated category is unlistable from the start and the review reads as confirmation rather than adjudication. The host observes that the expansion "might have been an extension of the law without congressional approval" — carefully phrased, but the concern is a real administrative-law one about a regulation exceeding its enabling statute. 68:02–68:16

This is the states' and tribes' central argument, and Bourque states it from their side without strawmanning: the CFTC, through 40.11, prohibited gaming contracts; a DCM listing sports event contracts is therefore listing illegally; and the CFTC, by declining to enforce its own rule, is abdicating a statutory duty. 130:16–130:46

The rule has been invoked three times in fifteen years, and each use narrowed the next.

In 2012 the exchange Nadex listed political election contracts. The Commission reviewed them under the special rule and found they involved gaming, using a method that looked at the act of trading the contract and compared it with state-law definitions of gambling — the staking of value on an uncertain future outcome. Having found gaming, it found the contracts contrary to the public interest, reasoning that they had no hedging utility and no price-basing function and could have perverse effects on the political process. In 2020–21 ErisX self-certified three NFL futures contracts with restricted participation; the CFTC opened a 40.11 review and ErisX withdrew before an order issued, so no final determination exists, though commissioner statements indicated disapproval on the same theory. 125:38–127:52

🔍 Source Verified✅ The CFTC issued its order prohibiting Nadex's political event contracts on 2 April 2012, reasoning that the unpredictability of an election's specific economic consequences meant the contracts could not reasonably be expected to be used for hedging and had no price-basing utility. It commenced the ErisX review under Regulation 40.11(c) on 15 December 2020 and requested suspension of listing; ErisX withdrew its certification on 22 March 2021, one day before the 90-day period expired. Sources: cftc.gov, releases 6224-12 and 8345-20.

The third use produced the decision the modern industry stands on. In June 2023 Kalshi, already designated as a DCM, self-certified two contracts asking whether Republicans would control the Senate and the House after the 2024 elections. The CFTC opened a special-rule review and issued a final order prohibiting them; Kalshi sued under the Administrative Procedure Act rather than withdrawing. 142:36–143:24

🔍 Source Verified✅ Judge Jia Cobb of the D.D.C. granted Kalshi summary judgment and vacated the CFTC's order on 6 September 2024, holding that the congressional control contracts involved elections and politics rather than unlawful activity or gaming. The emergency stay application failed, Kalshi listed election contracts within days, and the November 2024 cycle produced hundreds of millions of dollars in volume.

The reasoning that carried it is a canon of construction, and Bourque explains why it matters far beyond elections. If "gaming" means staking something of value on an uncertain outcome, every contract referencing assassination or war is also gaming.

"It would be silly to have the rest of the categories if they were all prohibited under the gaming definition." 141:55

That is the surplusage canon — courts presume Congress does not enact provisions that do no work. A definition of gaming broad enough to swallow terrorism, assassination and war reads four of the five enumerated categories out of the statute, so it cannot be right. 141:51–142:19

📋 Protocol Cheat Sheet
  • Under CFTC Regulation 40.2 a DCM self-certifies a new product, filing a written certification of compliance with the Act and Commission regulations by the close of business on the day before initial listing.
  • Under Regulation 40.3 it may instead request Commission approval — a voluntary 45-day review, extendable for novel or complex products, available before, alongside or after a self-certification.
  • Self-certification is the ordinary route and is why exchanges list quickly. It is not a rubber stamp: certification is a representation of compliance, and the Commission retains its review powers.
  • For an event contract on an excluded commodity, Regulation 40.11(c) lets the Commission commence a special-rule review, requesting suspension of listing and trading, with an order due within 90 days.
  • The consequence is that a DCM can list a contested contract on one day's notice and then defend it — the posture Kalshi, Crypto.com and later Polymarket's US entity have all adopted.

🗺️ The states escalate through four instruments, and the appellate courts have now split

Josh Sterling put the number of live lawsuits touching prediction market jurisdiction alone at "over two dozen" as of recording, counting states that have sued multiple exchanges, exchanges that have sued multiple states, and the CFTC's own suits. 32:06–32:26

The motive is not obscure. The American Gaming Association estimates legal sports betting generated over $2 billion in state and local tax revenue in 2024, with New Jersey alone collecting over $115 million. Tribes in many states hold exclusive gaming rights in exchange for substantial revenue-sharing payments, which a federally licensed national venue erodes without replacing. Nevada's position is the most extreme: state officials told the Ninth Circuit at oral argument that gaming revenue accounts for roughly a third of the state general fund. (72:58–73:30, 121:26–121:38)

⚙️ Market structure, not the duck test, is what actually separates a DCM from a sportsbook

The host treats this as the most important section of the episode, on the theory that people fear prediction markets largely because they do not know how they work. Grewal supplies the diagnosis.

"What I think is at the root of the anxiety or hostility to prediction markets, Jacob, is a sense that everything has become a game." 90:13

Much of that anxiety is irrational given the history, on his view, but the response has to be explanatory rather than dismissive: if people cannot distinguish an event contract on a CFTC-registered exchange from a casino, explain the difference and make the case for why it matters. 90:39–91:18

The mechanics state completely in a paragraph. Each contract is a yes-or-no proposition settling at one dollar. Price is probability: a contract at 62 cents means the market assigns roughly a 62% chance, and a correct call returns a dollar on that 62 cents. Positions trade at any time before resolution, so a holder whose side is winning late can sell at 95 cents rather than wait, and prices move continuously on supply and demand — a star player's injury before tip-off might take a contract from 62 to 32. 91:39–92:46

The structural point carries the legal argument. When you buy on Kalshi you are not buying from Kalshi. The platform does not take the other side; it matches buyers and sellers through a central order book and charges a small fee per trade, the way a stock exchange matches share transactions. Counterparties can be retail traders, institutions, or — most commonly in current practice — designated market makers, professional firms that quote both sides and earn the bid-ask spread. On Polymarket's offshore platform part of that function is performed by an automated market maker: a smart contract quoting prices from pooled funds by algorithm rather than by human traders. Either way the venue takes no directional position, and its fee does not depend on who wins. 92:46–94:06

Charmani's argument is that courts do not decide these questions by label.

"Sports book lines are quoted by the house while prediction markets prices are discovered through the interaction of willing buyers and sellers." 97:11

Her argument runs through three elements courts examine when assessing the economic reality of a transaction: counterparty risk, price formation, and the regulatory regime the venue operates under. At Caesars the customer bets against the house, which sets the odds, manages its own exposure and earns an embedded margin. On a DCM the customer trades against another participant on an open market, with an intermediary serving a clearing function and the price set by the market. 95:21–96:59

💡 Key InsightPrice formation is not a technicality — it is the entire policy rationale for protecting these markets under the Commodity Exchange Act. Exchange-traded prices work as price discovery signals that aggregate dispersed private information, which is the same justification that underpins federal protection of every other futures market. Charmani's argument is that a quoted sportsbook line cannot perform that function because it is not formed by the interaction of willing buyers and sellers, so the two venues are not substitutes for the purpose the statute cares about. 97:23–97:55

She adds the regulatory asymmetry: DCMs operate under position limits, daily trading information that must be published, financial integrity requirements, customer protection rules and insider trading surveillance. None of that exists in the sportsbook context. 98:15–98:45

🌐 Consensus CheckThose obligations are not discretionary undertakings but the Commodity Exchange Act's statutory core principles for designated contract markets — market surveillance and prevention of manipulation, position limits or accountability, daily publication of trading information, financial integrity of transactions, protection of market participants, and enforcement of the exchange's own rules. The host summarises the same set later, noting DCMs must surveil their markets, prevent manipulation, enforce trading rules and report data to the CFTC. 167:01–167:25

The revenue question is where the structural claim becomes checkable, because the fee schedule is public and formulaic. Kalshi charges a probability-weighted fee: current price times seven per cent, times one minus the price, rounded up to the nearest cent. 99:36

🔍 Source Verified✅ The formula matches Kalshi's published schedule. The taker fee is calculated as 0.07 × C × P × (1 − P), where C is the number of contracts and P the price in dollars, rounded up to the next cent; the maximum is 1.75 cents per contract at a price of 50 cents. Maker fees are roughly a quarter of that. Source: kalshi.com fee schedule.
💡 Key InsightA fee proportional to P × (1 − P) is a fee proportional to the contract's statistical variance — cheapest where the outcome is nearly certain, most expensive at a coin flip, which is exactly where the venue's matching, surveillance and settlement services are worth most and where the information content of a trade is highest. The host's translation: Kalshi charges in proportion to how uncertain the contract is. 99:49–100:13

In practice that is roughly 2–4% of the amount staked on taker orders, against an implicit spread — the vig — of about 4.5% on a standard −110 sportsbook line. On the same outcome, the host concludes, the exchange is structurally cheaper. Limit orders that add liquidity pay less still, and Kalshi runs incentive programmes plus a tiered rebate returning a substantial share of fees to active participants. 100:13–101:04

Matt Kalish, who co-founded DraftKings and ran it as president until stepping down in March 2026, supplies the strongest counter-argument in the episode, and it is a microstructure argument rather than a legal one. His objection is to the peer-to-peer framing.

"Retail doesn't typically post liquidity, right? Retail typically takes." 84:21

The retail experience, as he describes it, is arriving at a screen, seeing odds, clicking and buying — and what you buy is liquidity resting on the book from a market maker. In a sportsbook you bet against the house; here you bet against a professional firm that must earn a return on committed capital, which he likens to payment for order flow.

"The app is like a styrofoam sword against a Valyrian steel API that Wall Street is beating your ass with as a recreational user in the app." 165:29

The complaints are concrete: professionals see order book activity first, post an edge into their quotes the way a book posts a line, and exploit angles that do not exist in a sportsbook, including delayed data feeds and in-game news. When a player is scratched they cancel and re-price instantly across every correlated market while the recreational user holds stale orders; in a sportsbook the market simply freezes for five seconds and the odds refresh. 165:44–166:57

Kalish is not an abolitionist, and the host flags that his public posts read more hostile than his actual position.

"There's a real utility around prediction markets in sports markets. And I would say it's mainly for professional gamblers and Wall Street hedge funds." 157:42

He points to Betfair's coexistence of sportsbook and exchange in the UK as precedent, characterising the exchange customer as a top one-or-two-per-cent audience in sophistication. His prediction: in a few years 98% of people will prefer a sportsbook and professionals will prefer the exchange. (153:45–155:37, 157:01)

🎯 Against ConsensusKalish and Bobby Denault make directly opposed claims about the same facts. Denault says Kalshi is the fairer product precisely because it is not the house, does not chase losing customers and has no stake in who wins. Kalish says the absence of a house is cosmetic, because the counterparty is a professional trading firm and the recreational user is systematically on the worse side of it. Both descriptions are accurate; they differ on whether trading against a professional at a competitive price beats trading against a book at a posted margin. (159:46–160:28, 165:44)

Sterling's legal answer to the coexistence question is that the statute does not force a choice. Section 2(e) requires certain contracts to be traded by retail persons only on a DCM, but that does not make a sports bet against a book one of those contracts, and the exclusive jurisdiction provision itself says state laws continue to apply outside the narrow category actually traded on a DCM. The CFTC and SEC also jointly adopted a 2012 rule providing that consumer and commercial transactions which are not tradable are not swaps — a sports bet against a book is not tradable. 151:47–153:15

🎯 Resolution and insider trading are the industry's two genuinely unsolved problems

Everything above concerns whether these contracts may be listed. The two hardest operational questions concern what happens after they are.

The first is settlement. On a sporting event the answer is usually obvious, but the venues list thousands of markets where it is not. The worked example is the Super Bowl halftime show and whether Cardi B would perform with Bad Bunny: she was seen dancing, she did not sing, Polymarket resolved yes, and Kalshi took a middle path, closing the market and settling at 26 cents for no and 74 for yes. 101:31–102:22

Dustin Gouker, whose Event Horizon newsletter is cited often enough to appear in court filings, is critical of both the outcome and the drafting, while fair about the difficulty.

"Kalshi's putting up thousands of markets — writing rules that are airtight is not easy." 103:20

The structural difference is that Kalshi uses a centralised resolution committee working from rules published in each market's terms and conditions, while Polymarket outsources disputed settlement to UMA, a decentralised oracle in which token holders vote. Jeff Amico, chief operating officer at Gensyn and a former a16z partner, explains why this is the hard case for an oracle — the service connecting real-world information to smart contracts — since a prediction market's core value proposition is expressiveness: fine-grained positions on a huge variety of events, many unstructured and requiring judgment. 108:23–109:55

The history explains the design. Polymarket originally created, settled and resolved its own markets, with an internal council handling disputes. After the CFTC action in 2022 it stopped serving US persons, having no DCM licence, and decentralised settlement to UMA. Most markets then resolve uncontested; any participant may dispute a proposed settlement, a second stage allows a further challenge, and if it persists the question goes to UMA token holders, who discuss it on forums and vote over four or five days, with the result binding. (109:55–111:07, 113:44–114:49)

The failure mode is the MicroStrategy market. Polymarket runs a recurring weekly market asking whether MicroStrategy sold Bitcoin during the week. For the last week of May the company had in fact sold 32 Bitcoin, but disclosed it in an 8-K filed on 1 June. Someone disputed the outcome, it went to UMA, and voters resolved it "no" on the basis that the announcement had not been made within the week — even though the vote itself stretched four or five days into June, with the SEC filing in front of them the entire time. 111:38–113:07

💡 Key InsightThe MicroStrategy dispute is not a close call about ambiguous facts; it is a case where a voting body overrode the plain text of the market's own criteria. Amico's framing is precise — on a plain reading of what the criteria said, the answer should have been yes — and there was roughly $80 million of volume on the line. That is a governance failure of a different kind from Kalshi's Cardi B drafting failure: one is a rule written badly, the other a clear rule not applied. Both are settlement-integrity problems, and settlement integrity is now an explicit factor in the CFTC's proposed public-interest test. (113:07–113:40, 137:30)

The second unsolved problem is insider trading, and here the episode is at its most legally interesting because the doctrine genuinely does not fit.

Start with what the venues already do. Bobby Denault, Kalshi's head of enforcement, describes three overlapping platform rules: a prohibition on insider trading, one on improper influence of markets, and a broader prohibition on any trading by a person able to directly or indirectly uniquely influence a contract's outcome. Contract-specific terms sit on top — for political markets, candidates, campaign staff and connected pollsters are barred. The catch-all exists because a candidate's advantage is control, not information: he can buy "no" on his own election and then withdraw, collecting a windfall, or buy "yes" knowing opposition research is about to land on a rival. Kalshi's response in 2026 was name matching against KYC identity, blocking a user from trading strikes matching their own name; it generated hits on three candidates, two Democrats and one Republican, who by Denault's account were largely contrite and paid fines. 12:54–15:29

🔗 ConnectionExchange discipline → the regulatory gap it fills. Denault places these actions in a space that "may not rise to the level of illegality" and may not warrant CFTC attention, but which an exchange that cares about market integrity has to police anyway — self-regulation in the DCM core-principle sense, and commercially mandatory, because a market visibly full of insiders loses its customers. 15:29–16:07

The doctrinal problem Sam Enzer identifies is deeper than enforcement resourcing.

"It seems like a simple concept but when you drill down on whether something was an illegal versus a legal trade on information or alpha it is actually quite difficult." 20:48

He lays out three novelties. First, the instrument: the government says the asset traded is a swap while states say the same activity is gambling requiring a licence, and that threshold disagreement is unresolved. Second, the theory of liability. Classical insider trading law, in its misappropriation form, criminalises stealing information from its source and trading on it. Prediction markets open a category that does not fit.

"But if you are the source, if you are using information that you are the very source of, that doesn't really fit in the insider trading rubric." 22:46

His example is the reported allegation that former Congressman Santos profited from a market on whether he would attend the State of the Union. Trading on a fact you personally control misappropriates nobody's information. Is it insider trading? Market manipulation? Enzer's answer is that the question is genuinely open. Third is extraterritoriality: in the Google case the trader was a foreign citizen and the conduct occurred offshore, raising whether the Commodity Exchange Act, the wire fraud statute and the money laundering laws reach it, and whether SDNY has venue — asserted on the basis that Polymarket is headquartered in New York. 22:12–23:45

The charging structure reflects all of it. There is no standalone federal insider trading statute that fits, so the government charged commodities fraud under Regulation 180.1 on a misappropriation theory, together with wire fraud and money laundering. 23:45–24:33

🔍 Source Verified✅ The defendant is Michele Spagnuolo, a Google staff information security engineer, charged in the Southern District of New York in May 2026 with commodities fraud, wire fraud and money laundering. The complaint alleges that between October and December 2025 he traded at least 23 Polymarket contracts tied to Google's 2025 Year in Search results with near-perfect accuracy, generating approximately $1.2 million under the handle "AlphaRaccoon." The CFTC brought a parallel civil action on a misappropriation theory, alleging breach of a duty of trust and confidence owed to Google. Sources: justice.gov, USAO-SDNY release; cftc.gov, release 9237-26.
🌐 Consensus CheckRegulation 180.1 is the hook because of how it was built. Dodd-Frank § 753 amended CEA § 6(c) to prohibit fraud and manipulation in connection with any swap or commodity transaction, and the Commission implemented it in 2011 expressly on the model of SEC Rule 10b-5 — but broader in two ways that matter here: its scienter standard reaches recklessness as well as intent, and it captures attempted as well as completed conduct. That is what allows a misappropriation case to be charged as commodities fraud with no dedicated insider trading statute. 23:53–24:44

Denault's practical defence is a claim about surveillance surface area, and it is more persuasive than it first sounds.

"The universe of information that's material to that contract is actually a significant amount tighter than the information that's material to the price of a stock moving up or down." 163:20

For a market on next month's Fed rate announcement, the material non-public information is narrow and the set of people holding it is small; for an oil company's share price, materiality spans production at a field, activity at a port and decisions inside government. A contract with a single specific settlement condition is easier to surveil than an equity, even though the platform lists far more contracts overall. He is equally firm about the line: someone outside a stadium who overhears a rehearsal is doing research, just as counting ships outside a Baltimore port is a lawful edge in commodities, and where an NDA exists it governs prediction market trading exactly as it governs the stock. (106:24–107:53, 162:12–164:23)

💡 Key InsightDenault's framing separates two things public discussion conflates: lawful information asymmetry, which every market runs on, and unlawful asymmetry, which requires a duty and a breach. Prediction markets do not create the second merely by making more kinds of information price-relevant. What they change is the surface — more contracts means more duties potentially in play, an enforcement problem of scale rather than a doctrinal novelty, except in the one case Enzer identified, where the trader is the source. 106:57–107:28

🔮 The June 2026 proposal answers the question the courts have been arguing about, while they are still arguing

The regulatory response arrived while the episode was being finished, giving its last third an unusual quality: the guests are reading a 260-plus-page proposal days old.

The sequencing matters. Stefan Schropp explains that Chairman Michael Selig arrived at the start of 2026 and in February issued an advance notice of proposed rulemaking spanning more than 300 questions across more than 300 pages; the June notice is one deliberately narrow outgrowth. Schropp reads the pair as a systemic review pursued sequentially, starting at first principles with what can be listed on a DCM — the right place to start, because every other question depends on the answer. 69:16–70:45

Michael Passalacqua, senior adviser to Chairman Selig and lead of the agency's Innovation Task Force, speaking only for himself and constrained by an open comment period, names the target.

"The core problem that the NPRM seeks to address is ambiguity." 71:54

The proposal, he says, sets out criteria for when a contract involves an enumerated activity, what those activities are, and the parameters of the public policy review — with the stated goal of a framework that encourages responsible innovation while upholding market integrity. 72:02–72:35

🔍 Source Verified✅ The CFTC issued the notice of proposed rulemaking on 10 June 2026, published in the Federal Register on 12 June 2026 as Prediction Markets; Public Interest Determinations. It proposes amendments to Regulation 40.11 and the addition of Appendix F to Part 40, with a 90-day comment period. Chairman Michael S. Selig's accompanying statement was that "the CFTC will protect the integrity of our regulated markets without standing in the way of responsible innovation." Sources: cftc.gov, release 9249-26; federalregister.gov, 2026-11854.

Bourque walks the framework in three steps, and the middle one is the substantive move. Step one is a threshold question: is this an event contract within the special rule's scope — one referencing an excluded commodity? Step two asks whether it "involves" an enumerated activity. Step three, only if it does, is the public interest review. 136:00–136:40

The new definition of "involve" is tied to the settlement trigger: a contract involves an enumerated activity only if its settlement is determined by an occurrence inside that activity. Bourque illustrates with the war category.

"If you had a contract that settles based on how many ships pass through the Strait of Hormuz in a given month, that contract would not involve war even though a war in the region could affect the number, because the settlement trigger is the number of ships." 134:43

Contrast a contract on whether a named country will launch an air strike by a date: there the settlement condition is the strike. 135:01–135:16

💡 Key InsightAnchoring "involve" to the settlement trigger is the doctrinal counterpart to Judge Cobb's surplusage reasoning. It rejects the theory that trading on an outcome is itself gaming — the CFTC's own approach in the Nadex order fourteen years earlier — and converts a question of characterisation into a question of drafting: look at what resolves the contract, not at what it is about. That is administrable in a way the old test was not, and it moves a large set of contracts outside the enumerated categories entirely. 133:36–134:00

The proposal also defines "gaming," and the definition does what the surplusage problem demanded. Gaming means the game — an activity typically pursued for recreation, governed by rules, with outcomes determined by luck, skill or athletic ability — not the act of wagering on it. Separating game from wager is what stops the gaming prong swallowing terrorism, assassination and war.

🌐 Consensus CheckThe proposal also removes "relates to" and "references" from Regulation 40.11 wherever they appear, leaving only the statutory word "involve," for consistency with the Nadex and Kalshi orders, both of which turned on whether a contract "involved" an enumerated activity. That single deletion closes the gap between statute and regulation that had been the states' central textual argument. Source: the June 2026 proposal's discussion of § 40.11.

Step three publishes the test. Three general factors apply to every reviewed contract, none determinative — a weighing exercise. First, informational or hedging utility, counting in favour of listing. Second, market integrity: susceptibility to manipulation given the subject matter, integrity of the settlement source, and whether the structure encourages insider trading. Third, whether the contract impairs the exchange's ability to meet its compliance obligations. Activity-specific factors follow, with the Commission required to weigh both directions, explain its reasoning, and square any decision with prior determinations on similar subject matter. 136:47–138:30

For gaming the guidance splits. Games of pure random chance — a contract on a roulette spin — are likely to be rejected as serving no legitimate purpose. Sports contracts get a two-sided analysis: those settling on aggregate, objective, independently verifiable outcomes such as final scores, win-loss records and season statistics are favoured where surveillance and coordination with sports bodies exist, while those tied to discrete in-game events — player props, injuries, officiating calls, youth sports or pure chance — are likely to be found contrary to the public interest, because they reduce the outcome to a handful of identifiable people and invite manipulation. (132:27–133:10, 140:36–141:19)

🌐 Consensus CheckThe coordination-with-sports-bodies point is concrete rather than aspirational. The CFTC signed a memorandum of understanding with Major League Baseball in March 2026 and with the NHL on 21 May 2026, each providing for designated representatives, regular communication and confidential information sharing to address insider trading, fraud and market abuse in event contracts referencing those sports. Source: cftc.gov, release 9235-26. The host's reference to an NHL memorandum is accurate. 132:41

Schropp's summary is that prior Commissions wrote an implementing rule that was, in his words, not a model of clarity, producing opposed good-faith readings in oral argument after oral argument; the new proposal puts the public interest determination front and centre and publishes the guard rails. This is not a liberalisation of everything — sports sit inside the enumerated activity of gaming and remain subject to determination. Bourque identifies the litigation consequence: a rule that defines the terms, restores the statutory trigger and publishes the framework undercuts the abdication argument at its root, and states that these contracts, sports-based ones included, fall within the Commission's exclusive jurisdiction. (129:56–131:28, 139:05–140:05)

If the Court takes a case first and holds that sports event contracts are not swaps, the rulemaking becomes irrelevant to sports because exclusive jurisdiction never attaches. As of publication the host expected the Third Circuit case to reach the docket first; the Ninth Circuit's August 2026 ruling has made review likelier from either direction. Status: developing.

The final argument comes from Denault, and it is about the counterfactual.

"It is actually quite rare that you get players in a new financial space that are raising their hand for some very strict and difficult regulations." 168:45

His point is that offshore prediction markets already exist, do not perform KYC, and let anyone trade any outcome with no way to know whether a counterparty is an insider, a sanctioned person or a money launderer. Phase 3: Specific Item Breakdown

🟣 CEA § 1a(47) — The Swap Definition

The Dodd-Frank provision that made prediction market contracts federal instruments by defining a swap to include any payment contingent on an event with financial, economic or commercial consequence.

What It Covers: Six prongs, of which two matter. Clause (A)(ii) captures any agreement providing for payment dependent on the occurrence, non-occurrence or extent of occurrence of an event or contingency associated with a potential financial, economic or commercial consequence. Clause (A)(v) captures any combination or permutation of, or option on, the preceding categories. The definition also reaches instruments that "now are, or in the future come to be, known to the trade as swaps."

Why It Matters: Before Dodd-Frank, "swap" was not a defined term in the Act at all. Adding it pulled event contracts inside the federal perimeter automatically. The Third Circuit held sports event contracts satisfy this definition; the Ninth Circuit held they do not.

🟣 CEA § 2(a)(1)(A) — Exclusive Jurisdiction

The provision that converts "this is a swap" into "no state may regulate it," provided it trades on a designated contract market.

What It Covers: It grants the CFTC exclusive jurisdiction over swaps traded on or subject to the rules of a designated contract market. Sterling reads "exclusive jurisdiction" to mean no one else has jurisdiction, with no magic word needed to establish preemption. The states read "exclusive" as directed at other federal agencies, and invoke the presumption against preemption in fields — gambling being the paradigm — traditionally regulated by states.

Why It Matters: This is the contested link. The provision carries its own limit: outside the narrow category actually traded on a DCM, state laws continue to apply — Sterling's answer to the claim that a Kalshi win would abolish sportsbooks.

🟣 CEA § 5c(c)(5)(C) — The Special Rule

The discretionary veto Congress gave the CFTC over event contracts, exercisable only at listing and only after a public interest determination.

What It Covers: The Commission may determine that an event contract is contrary to the public interest if it involves activity unlawful under state or federal law, terrorism, assassination, war, gaming, or a similar activity designated by rule. A contract so determined may not be listed by a DCM or cleared by a DCO.

Why It Matters: Three features do the analytical work: the authority is permissive, not mandatory; it attaches only in connection with a listing, so no blanket advance prohibition is authorised; and both "involve" and "gaming" are left undefined. The 2026 rulemaking exists to supply the definitions Congress omitted.

🟣 17 C.F.R. § 40.11 — The Implementing Regulation

The 2011 rule that turned a discretionary Commission power into a standing command to exchanges, and thereby handed the states their best argument.

What It Covers: Paragraph (a) provides that a registered entity shall not list or accept for clearing any contract based on an excluded commodity that "involves, relates to, or references" the enumerated activities. Paragraph (c) establishes a review during which the Commission requests suspension of listing and trading, with an order due within 90 days of commencement.

Why It Matters: The regulation is written in the imperative and expands the trigger from one verb to three. The June 2026 proposal would delete "relates to" and "references," restoring the statutory word and the sequencing Congress wrote.

🟣 17 C.F.R. § 180.1 — The Anti-Fraud Hook

The CFTC's Rule 10b-5 analogue, and the only viable federal charge for insider trading in event contracts.

What It Covers: Adopted in 2011 under CEA § 6(c)(1) as amended by Dodd-Frank § 753, it prohibits manipulative or deceptive devices in connection with any swap or commodity transaction, and is broader than its securities-law model: its scienter standard reaches recklessness, and it captures attempted as well as completed conduct.

Why It Matters: No standalone federal insider trading statute fits an event contract, so the government proceeds on a misappropriation theory charged as commodities fraud — the Spagnuolo prosecution being among the first major applications to prediction market trading.

🟣 KalshiEX LLC v. CFTC (D.D.C. 2024) — The Election Contracts Decision

The APA challenge that vacated a CFTC prohibition order and made regulated US election markets a reality within days.

What It Covers: Kalshi self-certified congressional control contracts in June 2023; the CFTC opened a special-rule review and issued a final order prohibiting them; Kalshi sued in the District Court for the District of Columbia. On 6 September 2024 Judge Jia Cobb granted summary judgment and vacated the order, holding the contracts involved elections and politics rather than unlawful activity or gaming. The emergency stay failed and listing began immediately.

Why It Matters: Its surplusage reasoning — that a definition of gaming broad enough to cover any staking of value on an uncertain outcome would render terrorism, assassination and war superfluous — is now the analytical core of the CFTC's own 2026 proposal.

🟣 KalshiEX LLC v. Flaherty (3d Cir. 2026) — Federal Primacy, By One Vote

The first federal appellate holding that the Commodity Exchange Act preempts state gambling law as applied to DCM-traded sports event contracts.

What It Covers: Decided 6 April 2026, 2–1, Judge Porter writing. The majority held Kalshi likely to succeed on both links: sports event contracts are "swaps" because the event need only be associated with a potential financial, economic or commercial consequence, which sports outcomes satisfy through sponsors, broadcasters and local economies; and the CEA preempts state gambling regulation of contracts traded on a DCM. Judge Roth dissented on the presumption against preemption and on the availability of dual compliance.

Why It Matters: It is the farthest-advanced case and the one New Jersey signalled it would take to the Supreme Court. The majority also noted the CFTC had not acted to review or prohibit any sports-related event contract — a point the June 2026 rulemaking directly addresses.

🟣 In re Blockratize (CFTC 2022) — Why Polymarket Left the United States

The $1.4 million order that established, as an agency matter, that Polymarket's event contracts were swaps requiring a registered venue.

What It Covers: Issued 3 January 2022 against Blockratize, Inc., trading as Polymarket.com. The Commission found that from approximately June 2020 Polymarket had operated an illegal unregistered facility for event-based binary options; that the contracts were swaps within CFTC jurisdiction; and that Polymarket had neither obtained DCM designation nor registered as a swap execution facility. The order imposed a $1.4 million penalty, reduced for cooperation, and required wind-down of non-compliant markets.

Why It Matters: It produced both of Polymarket's defining structural features — withdrawal from US users, and the decentralisation of settlement to UMA — and is why its offshore and onshore products behave differently today.
