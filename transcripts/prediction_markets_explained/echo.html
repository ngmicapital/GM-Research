Prediction Markets, Explained: The Misunderstood Billion-Dollar Industry

Source Law of Code · 2026-06-30 · 176 min
Domain Law, Policy & Crypto
Reliability Expert explainer — fourteen named practitioners on the record, including a serving CFTC senior adviser, a former CFTC chairman, counsel actively litigating on the industry's side, and two critics
Content Type Explainer
Full Mode

⚡ The Bottom Line

  • Every prediction-market lawsuit in America reduces to a two-link chain. Link one: is an event contract a "swap" under Commodity Exchange Act § 1a(47)? Link two: does § 2(a)(1)(A)'s grant of exclusive CFTC jurisdiction over swaps traded on a designated contract market displace state gambling law? Break either link and the states win.
  • The statute Congress wrote is permissive; the regulation the CFTC wrote is prohibitory, and that gap is the states' best weapon. CEA § 5c(c)(5)(C) says the Commission may determine an event contract is contrary to the public interest. Regulation 40.11 says a registered entity shall not list anything that "involves, relates to, or references" gaming. States argue the agency banned these contracts itself and is now refusing to enforce its own rule.
  • The June 2026 rulemaking is aimed squarely at that gap. The CFTC proposes to tie "involve" to the contract's settlement trigger, define "gaming" as the game rather than the wager, delete "relates to" and "references," and publish the public-interest factors it will weigh — converting a de facto ban into a case-by-case review.
  • Market microstructure, not the "duck test," is the honest distinction. A sportsbook quotes the line, takes the other side and carries the risk. A designated contract market runs a central limit order book, holds no directional position, and earns a variance-scaled fee whoever wins.
  • The industry's unsolved problems are resolution and disclosure, not existence. Kalshi and Polymarket resolved the same Super Bowl question differently; a Polymarket oracle vote overrode the plain text of its own market rules; and the strongest critic's complaint is that retail traders are told they face peers when they mostly face professional market makers.

🔑 Key Claims (Ranked)

1. Event contracts fall inside the statutory definition of a swap, and the text does most of the work ↳ Evidence: Josh Sterling (Milbank, former CFTC Division of Market Oversight director) reads § 1a(47) directly — payment "dependent on the occurrence, non-occurrence, or the extent of the occurrence of an event or contingency associated with a potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence." 57:06 ↳ Caveat: The Ninth Circuit rejected exactly this reading on 28 August 2026, two months after publication. ↳ Context: The Third Circuit accepted it on 6 April 2026, holding a sports outcome is "associated with" financial consequence through sponsors, broadcasters and local economies.

2. Parlays are swaps too, because the swap definition swallows combinations ↳ Evidence: § 1a(47) reaches "any combination or permutation of" the listed transactions; Sterling's formulation is "a swap plus a swap is a swap." 59:11 ↳ Caveat: The objection is that a parlay has no hedging use — but Sterling notes the statute imposes no motivation test on traders. ↳ Context: This matters commercially, because parlays are where sportsbook margin is concentrated.

3. The special rule is discretionary authority, exercisable only at listing — not a standing ban ↳ Evidence: Brad Bourque (Hyperliquid Policy Center) walks the text: "the statute says that the CFTC may determine," and the review power attaches only in connection with a listing. 124:14 ↳ Context: The Commission has invoked it three times in fifteen years — Nadex (2012), ErisX (2021, withdrawn), Kalshi (2023, vacated in court).

4. Regulation 40.11 broadened the statutory trigger from "involve" to "involves, relates to, or references" ↳ Evidence: The host reads the two texts side by side and notes the regulation "might have been an extension of the law without congressional approval." 68:11 ↳ Context: The June 2026 proposal would delete both added terms, restoring the statutory word.

5. Judge Cobb's 2024 decision is the hinge the whole modern industry turns on ↳ Evidence: Kalshi self-certified congressional-control contracts in June 2023, the CFTC prohibited them, Kalshi sued under the Administrative Procedure Act, and the D.D.C. vacated the order; the D.C. Circuit refused an emergency stay and listing began within days. 142:31 ↳ Context: The reasoning — that a broad reading of "gaming" would swallow terrorism, assassination and war and render them surplusage — is now the CFTC's own position in the 2026 proposal.

6. The states escalate through four distinct instruments, and taxation is the fallback ↳ Evidence: Cease-and-desist letters from at least fourteen states, civil suits by gaming commissions and attorneys general, criminal charges in Arizona, then prohibitive taxes; Kentucky's 14.25% levy on transaction fees drew a CFTC counter-suit. (73:30, 87:36) ↳ Caveat: Stefan Schropp (Paradigm) argues a tax designed to eliminate the activity fails for the same preemption reason a ban would.

7. The platform never takes the other side, and its fee is indifferent to the result ↳ Evidence: Kalshi's taker fee is 7% of price × (1 − price), rounded up to the cent — a charge proportional to the contract's variance, peaking at 50/50 and falling to nearly nothing at the extremes. 99:36 ↳ Context: That works out to roughly 2–4% of stake in practice, against about 4.5% implied in a standard −110 sportsbook line.

8. Insider trading on event contracts is prosecutable, but through commodities fraud, not an insider-trading statute ↳ Evidence: Sam Enzer (Cahill Gordon & Reindel) explains the SDNY charged the Google case as commodities fraud, wire fraud and money laundering. 17:53 ↳ Caveat: Enzer flags the genuinely novel problem — a trader who is the source of the information, such as a politician trading on his own attendance, does not fit the misappropriation theory at all. 22:46 ↳ Context: The commodities-fraud hook is CFTC Regulation 180.1, modelled on SEC Rule 10b-5 but reaching recklessness and attempts.

9. Resolution is the industry's weakest joint, and the two leading venues resolve differently ↳ Evidence: Dustin Gouker on the Super Bowl halftime market — Polymarket resolved "yes," Kalshi settled part-way at 26/74. 102:13 ↳ Context: Jeff Amico (Gensyn) describes an ~$80 million Polymarket market on a MicroStrategy Bitcoin sale where UMA token holders voted against the plain reading of the market's own criteria. 113:33

10. The strongest critique is about disclosure, not legality ↳ Evidence: Matt Kalish, DraftKings co-founder, argues the peer-to-peer framing is misleading because retail rarely posts liquidity — "retail typically takes." 84:21 ↳ Caveat: Kalish still says both venues should exist; his objection is to how the product is marketed to recreational users.
