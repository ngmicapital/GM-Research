"Team's Going To Exit": Paradex's Anand Gomes On The Token Launch Loophole Nobody Closes — And The Contract He Signed To Close It

Source When Shift Happens · Date n/a · 69 min
Domain Crypto & Markets
Duration 69 min
Date
Speakers
  • Anand Gomes — Co-founder and CEO of Paradigm (institutional crypto derivatives liquidity network) and Paradex (privacy-focused perpetuals DEX built as a Starknet appchain on Ethereum)
  • Kevin Follonier — Host, When Shift Happens
Full Mode

Executive Summary

  • The episode's one durable contribution is a mechanism, not an opinion: 80% of Paradex core-team tokens unlock only against milestones published in advance and assessed annually. Gomes frames it as the answer to "down only" post-launch charts — teams whose supply unlocks on a calendar have no reason to care what the calendar costs holders. Independent coverage of the DIME schedule confirms the 80/20 split and the public-milestone process. 57:45–59:11
  • He closed the loophole that makes most lockups theatre. Locked tokens are routinely monetised through OTC desks and derivative structures without a single token moving. Paradex's foundation grant agreement contains an explicit clause prohibiting hedging instruments, with violation voiding the grant — a contractual commitment rather than a promise. 59:53–61:15
  • "TGE" has two readings, and the industry's incentive structure rewards the wrong one. Token generation event, or team's going to exit — a joke Gomes credits to a community member named Lazarillo and then uses as the episode's diagnostic frame. His sharpest observation is that serial token launchers gain credibility in this market rather than losing it. 52:55–56:32
  • His bull case for perp DEXes is a total-addressable-market argument. A perp DEX is simultaneously a bank, clearing house, exchange, brokerage and custodian, composable in one account — so the market being addressed is the sum of five industries, and 1% of that is still enormous. 44:52–47:00
  • He refused to build on Solana in 2022–23 while his own engineers were pushing hard for it, and says the architectural argument is not what won. What actually persuaded the team was Solana's RPC congestion at the time. His conclusion about founders is unsentimental: most people decide from what is visible, and the founder's job is to decide from what is not. 37:05–41:11
  • Roughly the first third of the conversation is medical, and the host says it is a record for the show. Gomes has mast cell activation syndrome, describes two months in 2025 unable to work, and undertook a 25-day near-total fast without medical supervision — then recommends it on camera. It is the most human material in the batch and the most hazardous; this document flags the risk explicitly rather than reproducing the recommendation. 01:55–15:16
  • The recording sits immediately before the DIME launch, which shapes everything in it. Gomes declines to give revenue numbers, promises them "right after TGE," and concedes his volume forecast will "come off as over bullish." The tokenomics commitments are checkable now; the disclosure promises are not yet. 65:17–65:44

🧪 The Body As A System: An Incurable Diagnosis Met With A Founder's Framework

The episode opens on health and stays there for roughly fifteen minutes of substantive content, which the host later notes is close to thirty minutes of the conversation overall and "probably a record on this show" 68:16.

The clinical picture Gomes gives: mast cell activation syndrome, a condition in which mast cells — most densely concentrated in the gut — activate erroneously, treating incoming stimuli as threats. He describes his cells as "hyper reactive," and traces the state to sleep deprivation, stress and poor food choices leaving his gut in what he calls a hyper-vigilant condition 03:36–04:17. He is explicit that doctors told him there is no cure 12:29.

His response is the thing worth archiving, because it is the same reasoning he applies to Paradex:

"My body is a system at the end of the day. It has inputs and it has outputs."

The move he narrates is a problem-definition move 12:45–13:36. He went from "I have this thing" to "I think this could be solvable" by reframing an incurable diagnosis as a system with observable inputs — food, stress, sleep — and observable outputs. He then argues this is the real difference between entrepreneurs and everyone else: not optimism and not risk tolerance, but practice at putting frameworks around problems. Most people have never had to acquire that skill because "normal life doesn't demand that off you."

The intervention: a 25-day fast. Coconut water alone for the first ten days, then coconut water plus one small cup of clear vegetable broth daily for the balance — the broth added, he says, for salt and electrolytes after realising coconut water supplies none. Twenty pounds lost. His assessment: the first three days were the hardest and after that "it was a breeze," and he had not felt that degree of relief in two years (02:17–07:01, 14:47–15:16).

He is clear about the motivation and about the supervision. The motivation was desperation — two years of suffering during which, he says, he had forgotten what a good state felt like. The supervision was none: asked directly whether there was doctor supervision, he says no, and points instead to a year of prior shorter fasts and self-directed research into electrolytes, sodium and magnesium 05:25–06:12.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Condition accurately described; the protocol carries risks the episode does not mention. Mast cell activation syndrome is a recognised condition in which overactive mast cells produce repeated multi-system episodes, and there is no cure — Gomes's account of his own diagnosis is consistent with the clinical literature. However, prolonged water-only fasting is a supervised therapeutic protocol in the research setting precisely because of refeeding syndrome, whose documented complications include arrhythmia, heart failure, respiratory and renal failure, and increased mortality. A 25-day near-total fast undertaken without medical supervision is materially riskier than the episode conveys, and the on-camera recommendation to the host — "I would highly recommend it to you" 03:01 — should not be taken as generalisable advice. Source: Cleveland Clinic MCAS overview; peer-reviewed literature on prolonged water-only fasting and refeeding.
💡 Key InsightThe transferable idea is not the fast — it is the reframe from diagnosis to system. A diagnosis is a label you either have or do not have; a system is something with controllable inputs and measurable responses. That conversion is what let him act at all, and it is the same conversion that lets a founder work on an unsolved technical problem. The reframe generalises; the 25-day protocol does not. 13:00

The section ends on a point both men agree on and that shapes the rest of the interview. Follonier: if you solve or learn to cope with health issues, "you're literally unstoppable, because building a business compared to that is so easy" — not easy, but tractable, because business has frameworks and precedents while an incurable illness has neither 14:22–14:47. Gomes's coda is careful: "I've not solved my problem, but I'm way better than I was a year ago."

🔗 ConnectionHealth → hiring → tokenomics — all three run on the same logic. Gomes's response to an unfixable situation is to find the variable he can contractually control and bind himself to it: eliminate every input to the gut; state plainly in interviews that the job will be miserable; lock 80% of his own tokens behind milestones he publishes in advance. Each is a commitment device substituting for a guarantee he cannot otherwise give. (13:00 → 26:13 → 57:45)

🔁 Surviving Cycles Is Almost Entirely Showing Up

Follonier frames the question against a market that has "gone to [hell] for the hundredth time," and notes something genuinely puzzling: with each cycle you would expect more serious people to join and more to survive, and it does not feel that way 15:19–16:06.

Gomes's answer is deliberately anticlimactic 16:11–18:36. Distilled to one thing, it is showing up every day — and he pre-empts the objection, saying he is not trying to trivialise how hard building is. His argument is about what you actually have to work with when things go badly: you do not have the answer to the problem. What you have is two things — "unshakeable optimism in what your goal is," and the perseverance to show up toward that goal daily. The tide turns eventually, and when you look back you find a few pivotal decisions and mostly just attendance.

Follonier supplies the framework Gomes is describing: Brian Armstrong's line that action produces information. Gomes agrees it is exactly that — "the action is just showing up... pushing yourself in a particular direction when you don't have enough information and you're operating with basically asymmetric information."

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed, with one refinement. "Action produces information" is indeed a line Brian Armstrong has publicly named as a favourite lesson — his full version is that when unsure what to do, do anything, even the wrong thing, because it tells you what you should actually be doing. Armstrong himself credits it as a Paul Graham-ism rather than his own coinage. Follonier's attribution to Armstrong is right about who popularised it. Source: Armstrong's own public statements; contemporaneous coverage.

Pressed on how he shows up while sick, Gomes reframes his "irrational optimism" as something more specific 18:58–20:59. The PC version, he says, is optimism. The actual version is a belief in a higher calling — building "this wealth creation engine for the world" that anyone can tap into for financial freedom. When he is sick, or markets are down, the thing he returns to is that the mission is unfinished: "in some sense we're still 0.1% of the way there."

The practical mechanism is smaller than the philosophy. On the worst days it comes down to getting out of bed and into the bathroom and the shower: "just that first step I feel is always the hardest."

🧵 Running ThesisGomes's stated engine across the entire episode is a single mission — permissionless access to a wealth creation engine — with everything else, including the perp DEX TAM argument, the Ethereum decision and the tokenomics, presented as instruments of it. Status: consistent throughout, but never tested: nothing in the interview asks what would falsify it or what he would stop doing if it were wrong.
⚡ TensionAt 17:53 Gomes endorses the view that action produces information — the case for moving before you have conviction. At 39:57 he describes the founder's job as the opposite: refusing the visible data points and deciding on first principles against internal consensus, which is precisely not letting new information change the direction. Both are defensible, and real operators do both — but the episode presents each as a general rule without reconciling when one applies over the other.

🪖 "Your Life Is Going To Suck": The Post-FTX Hiring Reset

Gomes calls his hiring approach "a complete 180 from when I started," and the story he tells about why is specific and unflattering to his earlier self 22:53–28:44.

The original error was location-based pattern matching. The company was founded in the US, and from roughly 2018 he hired for what he thought was Silicon Valley startup culture. His retrospective judgment is that the culture he was hiring for no longer existed: a long run of free money and rising tech stocks had made engineers comfortable, and "we were at least 10 years past" genuine hustle culture. He is blunt about his own position: "of course I didn't know anything."

FTX was the stress test that revealed it. The market collapsed, crypto went out of fashion, and Paradex itself "almost kind of went bankrupt." What he observed is the observation every founder eventually makes: when things go bad, a handful of people stay.

His reading of that is more precise than the usual character narrative 25:14–25:36: it is not really about character, it is that you learn what people's true motivation is. For some it is money, for some solving complex problems, for some impact — and none of it gets tested until something genuinely bad happens. The people who stayed are, in his words, the ones he will go to war for, because he knows they will do the same.

The deliberate change he made afterwards is the part worth extracting:

"Your life is going to suck. Because my life sucks."

Since 2022, Gomes personally conducts the final executive interview for every hire, and says this word for word 26:57–27:20. The full script: this is not an easy place to work, I am not an easy person to work for, this is my life's work and I approach it as sacred, there are no managers because the team is small, and if you want something I will probably be working on it with you — so there is nowhere to hide. The one promise he makes in exchange is "you will do your life's best work."

His argument for why this works is about the response it provokes rather than the information it conveys 27:47–28:24. It triggers a fight-or-flight reaction in real time. Some people visibly perk up and say this is what has been missing from their current job — he reads it in the change in body language. Others start asking about work-life balance and how much leeway there is, which he treats as its own answer.

🎯 Against ConsensusStandard recruiting practice is to sell the upside, and crypto's version is to sell it hardest — equity, token upside, the life-changing exit. Gomes does the inverse and does it at the final stage, after candidates are invested in the process. The design is a costly-signal filter: only candidates whose motivation survives an explicitly negative pitch make it through, which is a cheap way to screen for the exact failure mode FTX exposed. 26:13
💡 Key InsightThe thing that makes the script work is that it is falsifiable about the founder, not just the candidate. "My life sucks" and "I will probably be working on it with you" are commitments Gomes can be held to from day one of employment. A negative pitch nobody can verify is just a different kind of marketing. 27:20

⚔️ The Battle For The Soul Of Crypto, And Why That Meant Ethereum

Asked what crypto is for, Gomes describes an ongoing contest rather than a state 30:18–33:47.

His framing of the moment: the year began with crypto in fashion — regulatory environment improving, institutions arriving with tokenisation and Web3 language — and ended with crypto-native people feeling "there's this battle now for the soul of crypto." The concern is re-intermediation: institutions taking something built to be decentralised and rebuilding the intermediaries inside it.

He treats this as permanent rather than episodic. There will always be a force that takes the vision and moulds it into a shape that works for them, "and they will sell it to you as if it is the same thing as the thing you got to talk about, but it's just in my way, right? And they'll use nice marketing."

His answer for what holds the line is people, and he names one: he applauds Vitalik Buterin for staying true to the original ethos, citing a recent post to the effect that finance coming onchain is good but cannot come at the cost of decentralisation, censorship resistance or free speech. And he makes the connection to his own architecture explicit: "that's the reason I we built on Ethereum" — because Buterin's commitment makes Ethereum's direction predictable in a way he can build a decade of work on top of 32:28–32:49.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed as a fair characterisation. Buterin's public position through 2026 matches Gomes's summary closely: he declared 2026 "the year that we take back lost ground in terms of self-sovereignty and trustlessness," backed protocol-level censorship-resistance work including Fork-Choice Enforced Inclusion Lists scheduled for the Hegota upgrade, and stated that permissionlessness means "you are free to use Ethereum in whatever way you want, without caring about what I think." Gomes's paraphrase — finance onchain is good, but not at the cost of the principles — is accurate. Source: The Block, 16 January 2026; Decrypt coverage of FOCIL; Buterin's own posts.
⚡ TensionGomes's argument for Ethereum rests explicitly on one person's continued commitment — "unless he goes away or gives this up in some way, Ethereum will not change" 32:43. That is a key-person dependency, and it sits awkwardly against a section whose entire subject is why systems should not depend on any single controlling entity. He does not notice the contradiction, and it is the most interesting unexamined moment in the episode.

Pressed on what he wants crypto to be, Gomes reduces it to two principles: self-sovereign money and permissionless access, with permissionless read strictly — nobody can censor you, nobody gatekeeps you, and nobody needs to extract your information, which is where privacy enters 33:48–35:07. Paradex, in his framing, is "an app that sits on this foundational vision," which he attaches to both Ethereum and Bitcoin.

🔒 Three Layers Of Privacy — Paradex's Actual Differentiator

Asked to explain the privacy design, Gomes gives the clearest technical passage in the episode, and it is structured by where the data would otherwise leak 35:11–36:56.

He starts with the architecture that creates the problem. Paradex is an L2 appchain built on Ethereum: an actual blockchain that settles to L1. That produces two exposure surfaces — accounts are visible on the chain itself, and publishing state to Ethereum means positions become readable on Ethereum too.

The three layers of the response:

Layer one, Ethereum. Paradex encrypts positions before they are posted to L1. His claim: it is "the only exchange or DEX where we encrypt positions on Ethereum," so an observer either cannot see positions or sees ciphertext.

Layer two, the L2. Reading your position requires authenticated access. You can pull your own; without your credentials, nobody else can.

Layer three, orders. Orders live off-chain in the sequencer rather than on-chain, so they are not publicly visible at all.

The user-facing consequence is what he cares about: a trader on Paradex "doesn't have to worry about getting liquidation hunted or my positions known so I get squeezed."

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed on the architecture; ⚠️ the superlative is unbenchmarked. Paradex runs Paradex Chain, an L2 appchain on the Starknet SN stack — built with StarkWare and introduced as Starknet's first appchain in July 2023 — using CairoVM for computation and ZK proofs for state transitions. The privacy mechanism is independently documented: Paradex encrypts state update data before posting to Ethereum, with the ZK proof validating both the correctness of the transition and the integrity of the encryption, so observers cannot reconstruct balances or positions. The central limit order book runs off-chain on Paradex Cloud, matching Gomes's third layer. The one claim that cannot be checked is "the only DEX at scale" — "at scale" is undefined and no threshold is offered. Source: Messari research on Paradex; Starknet appchain documentation.
💡 Key InsightThe design inverts crypto's usual transparency default in exactly the place where transparency is a weapon. A public order book plus public positions on a leveraged venue is a standing invitation to liquidation hunting — the visible stop is the target. Privacy here is not an ideological feature bolted on; it is a microstructure fix for a specific predatory behaviour, which is why it is defensible even to people who do not care about privacy in the abstract. 36:42
📜 AnalogTraditional exchanges solved the same problem socially and legally rather than cryptographically — position reporting is aggregated and delayed, front-running your client is prohibited, and broker records are confidential by regulation. Paradex is reaching the same end state through encryption because a permissionless venue has no regulator to enforce the rules. 36:56

🧱 Why Ethereum And Not Solana — A Decision Made Against His Own Engineers

This is the episode's best case study in founder decision-making under maximum internal pressure, and Gomes tells it without smoothing the edges 37:05–41:11.

The context is 2022–23, Solana's ascent. He does not pretend price was irrelevant — the opposite: "anybody that tells you that price does not matter is either doesn't understand or has a very naive understanding of how this works. Price absolutely does matter." The conditions were a perfect storm: one ecosystem scaling with price rising, the other not scaling with price falling.

The pressure was internal. "All the engineers in our company were like, we need to build on Solana. The whole world is moving on to building on Solana. Why aren't we?"

His refusal rested on an architectural thesis rather than a market view: high-throughput, low-latency applications cannot be built directly on an L1, so the monolithic approach is wrong for applications like his and the modular approach is right. He says he is proud of holding the line.

Then the honest part, which is what makes the story useful 40:26–40:53. The architectural argument is not what persuaded the company. What persuaded them was Solana's RPC congestion at the time: "I think people less believed me for saying this was the right long-term technical choice, and they more believed the fact that, oh, this actually does have problems." The organisation was convinced by a visible defect, not by the thesis.

His generalisation follows directly: most people decide from what is visible right now, and "as a founder your job is to be almost like looking to the future" — extrapolating past the current data point to what might happen. But the anchor he keeps returning to is first principles: if we believed monolithic was the better way to scale, we would do that; we believe modular is better, so no matter how good monolithic looked at the time, it is not the right long-term trust.

The external pressure was worse than the internal. Gomes recalls essentially every investor conversation of 2023, and one specifically in New York with a Paradigm investor whose objection was straightforward: you have captive distribution on an L1 where people are already trading, and you want to build on something a year or eighteen months away — and Ethereum timelines, he notes drily, are "one plus you add another two." He describes those conversations as "pulling teeth," because the position had to be defended every time.

💡 Key InsightThe most useful line in this section is his rule about scrutiny. In an investor meeting you have no choice — the decision must be defended. Everywhere else, he declines: "why just open it up for scrutiny... it's just going to add stress to your life" 43:30–43:52. That is a discipline about where conviction gets debated, not whether it does — and it is a materially different practice from the industry habit of arguing every position in public. 43:40
🌐 Consensus CheckThe modular-versus-monolithic argument Gomes was defending in 2023 has since substantially settled in favour of application-specific chains for high-throughput venues — Paradex's own appchain, Hyperliquid's custom L1, and the broader appchain wave all embody it. Worth noting that the settlement went his way on the architecture while Solana also scaled successfully, so the correct reading is that both approaches proved viable, not that one was vindicated. 39:03

🏬 Perp DEXes As Financial Supercenters: Five Businesses In One Account

Asked why he is so bullish on perps specifically, Gomes rejects the vocabulary first 44:52–47:32. The word "perp DEX," he argues, "is almost like a disservice for what this thing really is and what it really can be."

His actual claim:

"PerpDexes are really the financial supercenters of the internet economy... for the first time you have one entity that has a bank, a clearinghouse, an exchange, a brokerage, a custodian, all in one."

The argument that follows is a TAM argument, not a trading argument. In legacy finance those are five separate regulated businesses, and the friction is the movement between them: JP Morgan to Fidelity to a retirement account, with a custodian somewhere in the chain. A perp DEX collapses them into one unified account with "instant permissionless access to any financial opportunity and asset in the world." So the addressable market is the combined TAM of every bank, brokerage, clearing house and custodian — trillions — and "even if you say I am 1% of this market, that is still an insane amount."

The second half of his answer is about positioning, and he is frank that it is part of the motivation: he believes Paradex is one of perhaps five entities in the running for a meaningful slice, and that being in that position at all is a privilege. "How many founders are lucky enough to get up and say, man, I have a shot at this?"

🎯 Against ConsensusThe market classifies perp DEXes as a derivatives trading category and values them on volume and fee capture. Gomes is arguing they are a balance sheet and custody category that happens to start with derivatives, and should be valued against banking and brokerage TAM. That is a much larger claim, and it is the same structural argument Hyperliquid makes from the other side — evidence that the strongest operators in the sector share a thesis the market has not fully priced. 45:14
🔗 ConnectionFive businesses in one → the privacy stack. If a single venue really does become bank, broker, custodian and clearing house for a user, then everything about that user's financial life is concentrated in one observable place. Under a fully transparent design that is a surveillance surface, not a convenience. The supercenter thesis is what makes Paradex's privacy work load-bearing rather than optional — a link the episode never makes explicitly. (45:14 → 36:42)
🌐 Consensus CheckThe framing omits the obvious counter. Five separate regulated entities also means five separate points of failure containment, and the separation of custody from execution exists precisely because history says combining them ends badly — a lesson crypto relearned with FTX, an event Gomes himself cites elsewhere in this interview as nearly bankrupting his company. Collapsing the five is a genuine efficiency gain and a genuine concentration of risk; the episode counts only the first. 46:20

🥈 Metals Were The Proof That Onchain Venues Can Lead Price Discovery

Gomes's evidence that the thesis is already working is the 2026 metals run, and his reading of it is specific 49:57–52:50.

The claim: metals broke out not just in crypto but in traditional finance, and for the first time metals traded in large size on decentralised venues — with the decentralised venues leading. What that demonstrates, in his framing, is threefold: onchain venues can tokenise real-world assets, they can bootstrap liquidity for them very quickly, and a market already exists to trade them. So the vision of a permissionless, self-custodial alternative financial system with deep immediate liquidity "is not something in the future that you're talking about. It's right here."

He also connects it to a reputational shift: this is not "a casino on Mars for some magic tokens that we've created."

Follonier supplies the sharpest formulation of the competitive dynamic 51:42–52:14. The old understanding of DEX advantage was that decentralised venues could list the latest trendy shitcoin faster than a centralised exchange. The new understanding is structural: whenever anything moves fast, they will list it first, and people will go there to trade it. Gomes agrees, and calls the metals episode a likely "aha moment" for traditional finance people — real-world assets, trading in significant size, on decentralised rails.

Then comes the forecast, which he flags himself 51:05–51:42. He predicts that by the end of the year Paradex will be not merely 10% of centralised-exchange crypto volume but, for the assets it lists in FX and commodities specifically, "at least 10% of the global CeFi market" aggregated across all exchanges. His own caveat: "this is probably going to come off as being super super bullish or over bullish."

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ The category claim holds; the company-level implication does not follow. Onchain metals volume in early 2026 was real and substantial — permissionless perp markets hit multi-billion-dollar daily volumes during the metals frenzy, with open interest peaking above $1bn. But the dominant metals markets ran on Hyperliquid's HIP-3 permissionless-deployment system, primarily through the deployer TradeXYZ, not on Paradex. Public estimates put those markets at roughly 1% of COMEX volume — meaningful for a months-old market, and a long way from 10% of aggregate global CeFi. Gomes's evidence is category-level proof that onchain venues can lead in real assets; it is not evidence about Paradex's own share. Source: The Block reporting on HIP-3 metals volume; market analyses of onchain versus COMEX volume.
🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Scale figures are plausible but partly unverifiable from the episode. Gomes says Paradex holds "$250 million in our contract" 22:42 — consistent with independent TVL reporting, which put Paradex between roughly $160m and $300m across early 2026. Paradex has generated over $250bn in cumulative volume since its February 2024 mainnet launch, with roughly $550m open interest in late February 2026, over 70,000 registered users, and a ranking around sixth among decentralised derivatives venues by 24-hour volume. Separately, the episode's opening credits cite "over $1 trillion in lifetime volume" for Paradigm; Paradigm redacts its lifetime volume figure on its own site, and independent reporting has cited figures in the hundreds of billions alongside roughly 35–40% share of listed crypto options and 700–1,000+ institutional counterparties. Treat the $1trn as an unverified producer claim. Source: DefiLlama; Messari; Paradigm's own site.

🚪 Two Kinds Of TGE — And The Contract Gomes Signed To Prove Which One He Is

This is the section the episode is named for, and it is the most substantive tokenomics discussion in the batch.

Follonier opens with the frame: there are two types of TGE — token generation event, and team's going to exit. Gomes immediately credits the joke to a community member, Lazarillo, rather than taking it 52:55–53:48.

He defines the honest version first. A TGE is when a team launches a token and it becomes freely tradable — "the equivalent of what an IPO is in some sense, but for projects with tokens, because we don't have equity." It is a real milestone: you have been building, you bootstrapped a product and a community, and now the market prices it.

Then the two paths 54:31–55:32. The good path uses the liquidity event to accelerate the mission. The bad path — "what most teams we've seen in crypto" do — is to launch at a high valuation, find a back-channel route to dump or cash in, and disappear. The signature is a chart that goes down for twelve to twenty-four months, after which the founders shut shop and start another project.

Follonier isolates the structural difference from equity markets: in traditional startups you have to build a product and find product-market fit to reach an IPO, whereas "in crypto you're able to make crazy money as a team without even building a serious product" 55:33–55:57. Gomes adds the two aggravating factors — no regulation and no real accountability for scamming someone — and then the observation that is genuinely his own 56:06–56:32:

"These repeat founders launching like their third token, and somehow people think that's bullish, which to me is crazy."

His response is a framework he calls transparency, alignment and governance, with four principles 57:07–63:25:

One, team-community alignment. Eighty per cent of the team's tokens are tied to performance milestones. Each year post-TGE, the foundation and board publish that year's milestones. If the team misses them, the team cannot sell. His contrast is with time-only unlocks: "a year passed, cool, you can sell now — but it doesn't really look at how much value did you create for the community." He reaches for Elon Musk's pay package as the conceptual precedent, tying unlocks to revenue or token price or both, announced to the community in advance.

Two, investor-community alignment. Paradex investors are locked for a year past TGE, on the IPO logic that private investors hold more information and more sophistication and therefore should unlock after the public market, not before.

Three, radical transparency. A commitment to publish sources and uses of cash in income-statement form — revenue generated, foundation costs, marketing spend. His stated motive is partly strategic: if the behaviour is associated with a token that does well, other teams adopt it and it becomes a market standard.

Four, value accrual. Named as a principle but, notably, never explained in the episode.

The loophole question is where the section earns its place. Follonier — noting that Gomes runs an options business and therefore understands the instruments — asks how the community can be sure the team will not simply hedge instead of selling 59:41–59:53. His answer is contractual: one of the conditions attached to the foundation's grants is an attestation that the team cannot use derivative instruments or hedging, and doing so puts them in violation of the agreement, which can then be voided 60:26–61:15.

🎯 Against ConsensusStandard crypto team vesting is a time cliff followed by linear unlocks, with no conditionality whatsoever — the calendar is the only gate. Making 80% of team supply contingent on published, assessed milestones and then contractually banning the hedging workaround is materially outside current practice, and Gomes's claim that no team has done this voluntarily is not obviously wrong. 57:45
💡 Key InsightThe enforcement question is the one the episode does not press. The milestones are assessed by the Paradigm board — the same corporate family as the team being assessed — so the commitment is contractual and reputational rather than independently adjudicated. That is still far stronger than a time cliff, because the criteria are published in advance and the failure is publicly observable. But the design's real test is the first year the team misses and the unlock is actually withheld. Nothing before that moment distinguishes it from a well-written promise. 59:11

🏆 "Winning Means Being Number One"

The closing section is short and unhedged, and it is useful precisely because it names the metrics 65:50–68:07.

Asked what the loudest voice in his head says, Gomes answers: "We want to win." If he is not winning, the nightly interrogation is structured — why are you not winning, is the plan wrong, is the product wrong, or are you not executing correctly?

He is equally direct about the definition. Winning means being number one, and the metrics are volume, revenue, users and open interest. His reference point is Binance: number one by volume and also number one by revenue, because the two tend to be highly correlated, with daily active users and open interest as the secondary set.

The question he audits himself against every day: "why are we not number one? Why haven't we built a brand that lives in the hearts and minds of people yet?"

And the justification for the cost, which lands as the honest version of the earlier hiring pitch: "if we're not here to win, then why am I putting myself through this, eating glass and crawling through mud and dirt?"

🔗 Connection"Winning means being number one" → the milestone tokenomics. The two are the same commitment expressed twice. A founder whose self-definition is a ranking on volume, revenue, users and open interest has already chosen the metrics a performance-gated unlock schedule would use — which is why the tokenomics design reads as native to him rather than as investor relations. Phase 3: Specific Item Breakdown

🟣 The 80/20 Milestone Lock — The Episode's One Verifiable Commitment

Eighty per cent of Paradex core-team tokens unlock only if the team hits milestones published in advance and assessed annually, which is the strongest anti-dump structure currently in the market.

What It Includes: Core contributors hold 25.1% of DIME supply. Of the team's allocation, 80% is performance-gated and 20% time-based, with the time portion unlocking 25% one year after listing and monthly over 36 months thereafter. Milestones are published before assessment and reviewed annually by the Paradigm board.

The Evidence: Gomes's framing is that time-only unlocks measure nothing about value created: "a year passed, cool, you can sell now" 58:23. His stated precedent is Elon Musk's performance-linked pay package, tied to a combination of price and revenue. The structure is confirmed by independent tokenomics coverage rather than only by the founder. The open question is enforcement: the assessing board sits inside the same corporate family, so the first genuinely withheld unlock is the real proof.

🟣 The Anti-Hedging Clause — Closing The Loophole Almost Nobody Closes

A grant condition that prohibits the team from using derivatives or hedges against locked tokens, with breach voiding the agreement.

What It Includes: An attestation attached to the foundation grants under which team members accept that using derivative instruments or hedging against their locked holdings constitutes a violation of the agreement, making it voidable.

The Evidence: This exists because Follonier asked the right question — Gomes runs an institutional options business, so nobody is better placed to know how a locked position gets monetised without selling it. Gomes concedes the practice outright: "people can still find a way to back channel, or some sort of derivative with some sort of OTC desk. People do this all the time" 60:15. His argument is that the lock and the clause together are what make the commitment real, since a lock alone is visible on-chain but circumventable off it.

🟣 The TAG Framework — Transparency, Alignment, Governance

Paradex's four-principle design rubric for token mechanics: team-community alignment, investor-community alignment, radical transparency, and value accrual.

What It Includes: Principle one is the 80/20 milestone lock. Principle two is a one-year post-TGE investor lockup on IPO logic — private holders have more information and more sophistication, so they should unlock after the public market. Principle three is publishing sources and uses of cash in income-statement form. Principle four is value accrual.

The Evidence: Gomes's stated strategic motive for principle three is worth noting because it is not altruistic 63:04–63:25: if the behaviour is publicly associated with a token that performs, other teams copy it and it becomes a market standard. The weakness is principle four — value accrual is named and never explained anywhere in the episode, which is conspicuous in a conversation otherwise dense with mechanism.

🟣 Paradex Chain — A Starknet Appchain Built To Be Private

An L2 appchain on the Starknet stack, settling to Ethereum, architected so that positions are encrypted before they ever reach L1.

What It Includes: Paradex Chain runs a private instance of the Starknet SN stack — CairoVM for computation, ZK proofs for state transitions — settling to Ethereum. The central limit order book runs off-chain on Paradex infrastructure. Three privacy layers: encrypted state diffs posted to L1, authenticated access at the L2, orders off-chain in the sequencer.

The Evidence: Gomes explains it by exposure surface rather than by feature list 35:22–36:45, which is the right way to reason about it: an L2 leaks position data in two places — its own chain and the state it publishes to L1 — and the design plugs both. Independently documented: Paradex encrypts state update data before posting to Ethereum, with the ZK proof validating both transition correctness and encryption integrity. Launched as Starknet's first appchain in July 2023, built with StarkWare; public mainnet February 2024.

🟣 The Perp DEX As Financial Supercenter

Gomes's reframe of the category — five regulated businesses collapsed into one composable account, so the addressable market is banking plus brokerage plus custody plus clearing, not derivatives.

What It Includes: Bank, clearing house, exchange, brokerage and custodian functions in a single unified account with permissionless access to any asset. The claimed consequence is a TAM in the trillions, where even 1% is a large business.

The Evidence: The friction example is concrete and familiar: JP Morgan to Fidelity to a retirement account, with custody handled elsewhere 45:52–46:08. Gomes says he first put the argument in a tweet — that DeFi takes over the world but perp DEXes specifically eat all of finance. What the episode does not weigh is that the five entities are separate partly by design: separation of custody from execution is a containment mechanism, and its absence is what made FTX possible — an event Gomes cites elsewhere in the same interview as nearly bankrupting his company.

🟣 Paradigm — The Institutional Options Business Underneath

Gomes's first company and the reason his answers on derivatives loopholes are credible: an institutional liquidity network for crypto options with a large share of the listed market.

What It Includes: A wholesale marketplace where institutional counterparties — hedge funds, OTC desks, market makers, structured product issuers, family offices — negotiate large derivatives trades off-exchange and settle on venues such as Deribit.

The Evidence: The episode's opening credits cite "over $1 trillion in lifetime volume," which should be treated as an unverified producer claim: Paradigm redacts its lifetime volume on its own site, and independent reporting has cited figures in the hundreds of billions alongside roughly 35–40% share of listed crypto options, average daily volume in the hundreds of millions, and 700–1,000+ institutional counterparties. The relevance to this interview is not the number — it is that the person designing anti-hedging clauses for his own token spends his day job running the venue where that hedging would be executed.
