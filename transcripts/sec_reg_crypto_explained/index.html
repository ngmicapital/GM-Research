Reliability Expert explainer — the host is a securities regulatory lawyer reading from the proposing release, with six practitioners who are cited in its footnotes and are filing comment letters on it.
Content Type Regulatory Explainer
Full Mode

⚡ The Bottom Line

  • Reg Crypto is the SEC's first affirmative instruction manual for selling tokens, and its genuinely new component is the exit. Two registration exemptions and a crypto-native disclosure schedule matter, but nothing before this told a project how an investment contract ends. Rule 400 does.
  • The exit test is contract performance, not decentralisation. Did you complete or permanently cease the essential managerial efforts you promised, and did you file a transition report saying so? Control, unilateral power and sufficient decentralisation appear only if the project itself promised them.
  • The architecture is Reg CF and Reg A+ with the disclosure content replaced. Subpart B is $5 million once over four years on a self-filed notice; Subpart C is Reg A's exact dollar architecture — $20 million and $75 million tiers, 10% investor cap, 30% and $6 million/$22.5 million affiliate caps — with token disclosure instead of a balance sheet.
  • Every practitioner backs the direction; all six objections are about the perimeter. A moral hazard that rewards silence, a perverse incentive toward minimal promises, third parties who cannot verify a transition report, and a federal exit that does not carry a state exit with it.
  • Nothing is law, and the legislative backstop has since failed. Comments closed 20 October 2026, adoption realistically lands in 2027 — and the CLARITY Act cloture vote the speakers were counting on failed 49–50 on 15 September 2026.

🔑 Key Claims (Ranked)

1. Rule 400 lets a token leave federal securities law by certifying promise-performance, and it is available even to projects that never used either exemption ↳ Evidence: Rule 400(a) requires completed or permanently ceased essential managerial efforts with no new promises; Rule 400(b) requires a Form TR analysis written for a reasonable investor 48:14–49:43. ↳ Caveat: The Commission can take the position after the fact that the contract never ceased, notwithstanding attempted reliance 50:26. ↳ Context: This is the retroactive relief channel for every token launched between 2017 and 2026 — they can file out without ever having used Subpart B or C.

2. Maintaining and improving an already-functional network does not count as essential managerial efforts ↳ Evidence: The release states that services to secure, maintain, improve or enhance a functional network, or to facilitate network effects, would not constitute essential managerial efforts 53:10. ↳ Context: Sarah Brennan notes nobody was sure Ethereum cleared the old bar because of continual upgrades; she now calls the test manageable and "probably too permissive" 54:13.

3. The startup exemption, not the fundraising exemption, is what lawyers expect to be used ↳ Evidence: $5 million over four years, one-time, Form NOR notice, no SEC qualification, no audited financials, no intermediary, retail eligible, no resale restrictions, and stackable with a Reg D placement under Rule 101(a) (18:08, 47:07). ↳ Caveat: One-time use per asset, and the four-year clock does not stop because the network is not finished 29:19. ↳ Context: Regulation CF failed because issuers did not want thousands of holders; for a token that is the product, which is the strongest argument Subpart B outperforms its ancestor.

4. Subpart C inherits Reg A+'s economics, and Reg A+ was almost never used ↳ Evidence: Gabriel Shapiro notes even conventional equity startups skip Reg A+ because it is not different enough from a full IPO to justify the cost 46:00; Brennan puts crypto Reg A costs at $300–500k historically 44:22. ↳ Caveat: Blockstack's 2019 Stacks offering — the only SEC-qualified token offering — took ten months and $2.8 million. ↳ Context: Shapiro's economic objection is that anyone who can raise $75 million from retail can raise it from VCs under Reg D with no qualification and no audit.

5. The promises-based trigger creates a moral hazard that rewards reputation over disclosure ↳ Evidence: Shapiro argues a highly credentialed team can say nothing, still sell the token, and sit outside the disclosure regime entirely 24:24. ↳ Context: Rodrigo Seira names the mirror-image failure at the exit — minimal promises, fast completion, fast escape 58:48 — so the rule's viability depends on reading "representations" broadly, which reintroduces the vagueness Lewis Cohen objects to elsewhere.

6. Form TR gives issuers a bright line and gives third parties nothing ↳ Evidence: Cohen supports the Commission's ability to disbelieve a transition report, but notes exchanges, market makers and custodians who relied on the filing have no protection if it is later disbelieved 51:31–52:11. ↳ Caveat: His proposed fix was congressional, and that route closed on 15 September. ↳ Context: He is also filing for an objective-criteria safe harbour for non-affiliated third parties dealing with the asset.

7. State law is the unpatched seam on both sides of the exit ↳ Evidence: Subpart E preempts state registration while the covered investment contract lives; Brennan notes there is no certainty of state preemption after the exit, naming the Martin Act 80:14. ↳ Context: Andrew Hinkes goes further — there is no federal private contract law, and no state law makes a token transfer an assignment of contractual rights, so the premise that rights ride with the token may not be supportable 77:04.

8. The proposal never says when a crypto asset is a security ↳ Evidence: Cohen: it applies only to crypto assets that are not securities, and the antecedent question is left where it was 62:34. ↳ Context: The March 2026 joint SEC–CFTC interpretation supplies the taxonomy and the representation factors, but the perimeter remains a facts-and-circumstances judgement.

Executive Summary

  • Reg Crypto is the SEC's first affirmative "here is how to do it" rulebook for selling tokens, and it does three separable things. It creates two registration exemptions for token distributions, a crypto-specific disclosure playbook, and — for the first time — a formal procedure by which a token stops being subject to federal securities law. Jacob Robinson frames the third as the genuinely new one: nothing in the prior regime told a project how the investment contract ends. 01:15–02:14
  • The architecture is borrowed wholesale from the traditional exempt-offering shelf, then re-plumbed for tokens. Subpart B is Regulation Crowdfunding in shape ($5 million, file-and-go, retail eligible); Subpart C is Regulation A+ in shape ($20 million and $75 million tiers, SEC qualification, audited financials at the top tier). What is not borrowed is the disclosure content, which is rewritten around token economics, code, governance and allocations rather than a balance sheet. 10:26–12:15
  • The exit test is a contract-performance test, not a decentralisation test. Rule 400 asks whether the issuer has completed or permanently ceased the essential managerial efforts it promised, and whether it has filed a transition report saying so. Sufficient decentralisation, control, and unilateral power — the vocabulary of the last eight years — are conspicuously absent unless the project itself promised them. 48:14–49:43
  • Every lawyer on the episode supports the direction; the disagreements are all about the perimeter. Gabriel Shapiro flags a moral hazard that rewards reputationally strong teams for saying nothing. Rodrigo Seira flags the mirror-image perverse incentive: minimal promises, fast completion, fast exit. Lewis Cohen flags third parties — exchanges, market makers, custodians — who can be caught out when an issuer's own transition report is later disbelieved. (24:24, 58:45, 64:22)
  • State law is the unpatched seam. Subpart E preempts state registration while the covered investment contract is alive, but Sarah Brennan's point is that the federal exit does not carry a state exit with it — and a permissive federal test can leave an issuer outside federal law while still looking like a security under, for example, New York's Martin Act. Andrew Hinkes adds the deeper problem: there is no federal private contract law, and no state law says a token transfer effects an assignment of contractual rights. (79:29–80:26, 75:57–78:11)
  • Nothing here is law yet, and the legislative backstop just failed. Comments were due 60 days from Federal Register publication; the realistic adoption window is 2027. Jacob and Lewis both look to the CLARITY Act's September 15 Senate vote as the event that would reshape the rulemaking — that cloture vote failed 49–50 on 15 September 2026, leaving Reg Crypto as the only live market-structure vehicle. 80:37–84:25

⚖️ Reg Crypto Exists Because Howey Never Had an Off-Ramp

The whole proposal is legible once you see the two specific problems Jacob Robinson says it was drafted against.

"How can I raise capital to develop a crypto asset while I am still working to develop the network where it will be used?" 00:20 — Chair Atkins, framing the question the proposal answers.

The first problem is the one every crypto lawyer has lived with: the Howey investment-contract test was built for orange groves and has no native concept of an asset that starts inside a securities relationship and later leaves it. Robinson notes the SEC's own admission that applying Howey to crypto is "challenging" because of the varying degrees of control, the diversity of asset types, and the evolving nature of the technology 06:52. The March 2026 interpretation had already confirmed the two-way street — a non-security crypto asset can become subject to an investment contract through the representations and promises made around it 07:02 — but confirming that a door swings both ways is not the same as building a handle on the inside.

The second problem is the middle. Robinson puts it precisely: you have the distribution at one end and the scheme falling away at the other, and in between the asset is subject to securities laws and "a host of rules that might apply" that were never written for this asset class 09:33–09:57. A team that accepted its token sale was a securities offering and wanted to comply faced exactly two doors: full registration — "think the full IPO process, wildly expensive, built for companies issuing stock" — or an exemption built for private company stock 10:06–10:21.

💡 Key InsightThe reason Reg Crypto reads as three rules stapled together is that it is answering three different questions that the existing regime answered badly or not at all: how do I sell, what do I say while I hold the relationship open, and how do I get out. The third has no analogue anywhere else in securities law, because equity never stops being equity. 01:41
🔍 Source Verified✅ SEC v. W.J. Howey Co., 328 U.S. 293 (1946), supplies the four-element test — an investment of money, in a common enterprise, with a reasonable expectation of profits, derived from the efforts of others. Nothing in Howey or its progeny addresses termination, because in the orange-grove facts the contractual relationship simply ran its course. Reg Crypto's Subpart D is an administrative answer to a question the case law never had to ask.
🌐 Consensus CheckThe March 2026 document Robinson refers to was a joint SEC–CFTC interpretation issued 17 March 2026. It set out a taxonomy of crypto assets — digital commodities, digital collectibles, digital tools, stablecoins and digital securities — and addressed how a non-security crypto asset may become subject to, and cease to be subject to, an investment contract. It also covered airdrops, protocol mining, protocol staking and wrapping. Reg Crypto is the rulemaking that operationalises it. 06:33

🏛️ The Proposal Is the Last Link in a Nineteen-Month Policy Chain

Robinson is careful to place Reg Crypto in sequence, because the sequence explains the content.

January 2025: then-Acting Chairman Mark Uyeda establishes the Crypto Task Force under Commissioner Hester Peirce with a mandate to develop a comprehensive framework 04:28. Two days later the President signs an executive order titled Strengthening American Leadership in Digital Financial Technology, creating the President's Working Group on Digital Asset Markets and ordering it to report back 04:42. Six months later it does, and its recommendations to the SEC are, in Robinson's words, exactly what the Commission went on to do: a fit-for-purpose exemption from registration for digital asset distributions; a time-limited safe harbour for tokens on networks not yet functional or decentralised; and a safe harbour for airdrops 05:08–05:24.

One day after that report, Chair Atkins announces Project Crypto and directs staff to build the proposals.

"For those crypto asset transactions that are subject to these securities laws, I've asked the staff to propose purpose fit disclosures, exemptions, and safe harbors, including for so-called initial coin offerings, airdrops, and network rewards." 05:47 — Atkins, 31 July 2025.

Robinson's structural point is that Reg Crypto maps one-to-one onto the working group's three recommendations, and he closes the episode by reading them back against what was proposed 85:53. That is not a coincidence to be admired; it is a signal about how much of the substance was settled before drafting began, and therefore how much of it is likely to survive the comment file.

Robinson points out that comment letters from the SEC's 2025 request are cited throughout the proposal's footnotes, and that the guests on this episode are among the cited authors (02:53, 81:08). The practical implication is that the comment file is not a formality here — the Commission has demonstrably been building from it, which is why every practitioner in the episode is filing again.

🧱 The Architecture: Five Subparts, Three Borrowed Ancestors

Before walking the rules, Robinson does the useful thing and explains the three traditional exemptions Reg Crypto is built from, because the whole proposal is a re-cut of them 10:26.

Regulation A — the "mini IPO." Up to $75 million per year from the general public, using a streamlined disclosure document called an offering circular, which the SEC must qualify before any sales occur 10:41. Regulation Crowdfunding — the smallest companies, up to $5 million per year from the general public, on a file-and-go model where the issuer posts disclosures and starts selling without waiting for SEC approval 10:56. Regulation D — the private placement exemption: unlimited capital, generally only from accredited investors, and the securities are restricted and cannot be freely resold 11:11.

Reg Crypto then divides into five subparts 12:15:

  • Subpart A — general rules, definitions, and the new disclosure playbook.
  • Subpart B — the startup exemption, up to $5 million over four years.
  • Subpart C — the fundraising exemption, up to $75 million per year, modelled on Reg A.
  • Subpart D — the investment contract safe harbour: the off-ramp.
  • Subpart E — preemption of state securities registration.
💡 Key InsightRead the lineage and the politics fall out of it. Subpart B inherits Reg CF's mechanics (notice, no qualification, retail eligible) but not its $5 million-per-year cadence — it is $5 million once, over four years. Subpart C inherits Reg A's dollar architecture exactly — the tiers, the audit line, the investor cap, the affiliate resale caps — while replacing Reg A's financial-statement-centric content with token disclosure. The SEC borrowed the plumbing it could defend and rewrote only the part that was demonstrably broken for this asset class. 11:34
🔍 Source Verified✅ Regulation A's numbers are exactly as ported. Tier 1 is capped at $20 million in a 12-month period, including no more than $6 million on behalf of selling securityholders who are affiliates; Tier 2 is capped at $75 million, including no more than $22.5 million for affiliate selling securityholders. Sales by all selling securityholders are limited to 30% of the aggregate offering price in an issuer's first Reg A offering and in any subsequent offering within the following 12 months. Non-accredited natural persons in a Tier 2 offering are limited by Rule 251(d)(2)(i)(C) to 10% of the greater of annual income or net worth. Source: sec.gov Regulation A guidance for issuers.
🌐 Consensus CheckOne mechanic Robinson does not mention is worth holding onto when comparing Subpart B to its ancestor: Regulation CF cannot be run by the issuer alone. Every Reg CF offering must be conducted through a registered funding portal or broker-dealer intermediary. The startup exemption has no such gatekeeper — the issuer files its own notice and publishes its own disclosures. That makes Subpart B meaningfully lighter than the rule it resembles, and it is a large part of why the practitioners here expect it to be the one that gets used. 10:56

🚀 Subpart B: The Startup Exemption Is the One Lawyers Expect Projects to Use

The startup exemption begins at page 70 of the proposal 15:50 and its purpose, in Robinson's framing, is to provide "a regulatory runway" — a way to fulfil the representations you made about what you would build 15:57.

He counts six conditions 18:08:

  1. Up to $5 million raised.
  2. One-time use — you cannot have already relied on it, and you cannot re-enter.
  3. A four-year window for sales and distributions.
  4. The clock starts on filing a simple notice of reliance with the SEC.
  5. Ongoing disclosures under Rule 103, published free of charge on the project's own website and kept current.
  6. The general conditions that apply across all of Reg Crypto — the bad actor disqualification, and the requirement that everything disclosed is honest (the anti-fraud provisions, which apply regardless).

The exemption ends at four years, or earlier if the project declares it is no longer conducting essential managerial efforts by filing a transition report. That form requires the project to tick a box certifying it has completed or ceased all the essential managerial efforts it said it would perform when distributing the assets, and that it will not make new promises to engage in similar efforts 19:06–19:25. Robinson's gloss is exact: "So, you either did it or you've given up."

Rule 103 is where the disclosure content lives, and it is the part that most rewards close reading. It requires the representations that create the investment contract — a business plan with detailed milestones, a timeline, information about personnel, sources of funding and other resources needed to meet those milestones, and an explanation of how holders of the non-security crypto asset will profit from the team's efforts. It also requires token allocation and recipients, token economics including total supply and distribution methods, code questions such as whether the code is open source and whether it has been audited, and information about the team 19:38–20:24.

Two structural features matter more than they sound. First, the exemption covers distributions, not merely sales — Robinson flags the word choice explicitly, and the definition expressly includes airdrops, distributions related to staking, governance and gas fees, rewards for securing the network, and payments for testing 28:58–29:17. Second, because retail can buy, there are no resale restrictions: tokens issued this way circulate freely from day one 30:04.

"One's a 5 million startup exemption and that one is very lightweight." 14:16 — Gabriel Shapiro on why Subpart B is the one he is confident will be used.

Shapiro's reading of who uses it is expansive, and deliberately so. He thinks the exemption is suited to centralised and decentralised projects alike, and gives a Telegram-shaped hypothetical: a messaging company announcing it is building a blockchain, that the token will have functions inside the centralised app, that it will be built over four years, and that a token sale finances it. "Even though there is some centralization to it… that should be totally allowed" 16:41–17:11. He also notes the stacking point — a Reg D private placement to VCs can run alongside a Subpart B distribution.

📜 AnalogShapiro's Telegram hypothetical is not a neutral example. SEC v. Telegram Group Inc. is the case where Judge P. Kevin Castel, SDNY, granted the SEC a preliminary injunction on 24 March 2020 blocking delivery of Gram tokens. Under Subpart B, the same fact pattern — centralised company, four-year build, token sold to finance it — becomes a filing rather than a lawsuit, provided the promises are written down and performed. That is the measure of how far the perimeter has moved. 16:41
🔗 ConnectionRule 101(a) → capital stack design. Robinson flags that Rule 101(a) makes the exemptions non-exclusive, so a Reg D private placement to VCs can occur alongside a startup-exemption distribution 47:07. Combined with Lewis Cohen's observation that most practitioners expect "a startup exemption and then a Reg D offering sort of going hand in hand" 34:12, the emerging default structure is visible: equity or SAFT-style instruments to accredited investors under Reg D, retail token distribution under Subpart B, both live at once.
💡 Key InsightThe one-time-use condition is the sharpest constraint in Subpart B and the least discussed. A project gets one $5 million, four-year runway per crypto asset, with no re-entry. If the network is not functional at the end of four years, Robinson lists the remaining options: move to the fundraising exemption, use another exemption such as Reg D, register fully, or simply stop distributing while the work finishes 29:19–29:44. Critically, the safe harbour itself has no deadline — only the distribution exemption does.

🏦 Subpart C: Reg A+ With Crypto Disclosure — and Reg A+'s History Is the Warning

The fundraising exemption is explicitly modelled on Regulation A, with the same dollar limits and different requirements 34:17. Tier 1 permits up to $20 million in a 12-month period; Tier 2 up to $75 million. What separates them is cost, and specifically the audit: both tiers must include financial statements in their offering documents, but Tier 1's carry no assurance requirement, while Tier 2's must be audited 34:40–35:02.

The process has four steps 36:44: file an offering statement with the SEC on a new crypto-specific form, which Robinson describes as a lightweight version of an IPO prospectus built around Rule 103; go through review, which means back-and-forth until staff is satisfied and qualifies the statement; sell; then file ongoing reports — annual, semi-annual, and a current report for material events between them.

Two investor-protection features are ported directly. A purchaser who is not accredited is limited to 10% of the greater of annual income or net worth, in both tiers 37:56–38:14. And insider selling is capped: existing holders cannot make up more than 30% of sales in the issuer's first year, with affiliates capped on an ongoing basis at $6 million in Tier 1 and $22.5 million in Tier 2 41:33–41:49.

Shapiro's scepticism is the sharpest thing in this section and it is an economic argument, not a legal one. "If you can raise 75 million from unaccredited investors you could probably also raise 75 million from VCs" 15:22 — so why accept audited GAAP financials, PCAOB standards and SEC qualification to reach a retail pool you do not need?

Sarah Brennan supplies the historical evidence for that scepticism. Reg CF failed, she says, "because nobody wants that many people on their cap table. It's hard to administer. You only do that if you really couldn't raise with a Reg D" 42:24–42:37. Reg A failed for a different reason: internal controls, overhead, a later-stage offering profile, and significant sunk cost with no certainty you raise what you intended 43:01–43:20. Shapiro's version is blunter: even ordinary equity startups do not use Reg A+, because it is "not different enough from the expense and time of a full IPO to be worth it" 46:00–46:14.

💡 Key InsightThe market's revealed preference is the whole argument. Both Brennan and Shapiro are saying the same thing from different angles — capital flows to the lowest-friction exemption that reaches the capital it actually needs, and for a decade that has been Reg D: uncapped, accredited-only, cheap, no qualification. Subpart C will be used only where retail distribution is itself the point, not where capital is the point. 43:46
🌐 Consensus CheckBrennan's cost estimates — $150–200k pre-COVID for a conventional Reg A, $300–500k for a crypto Reg A, and now plausibly the high end plus a legal opinion 44:06–45:06 — are, if anything, conservative against the one real datapoint. Blockstack's Stacks token offering, qualified by the SEC on 10 July 2019 as the first Reg A+ token offering, raised $28 million and by the company's own account took ten months and $2.8 million to complete. That is an order of magnitude above Brennan's figure, on a rule that had no crypto-specific disclosure regime to comply with. Sources: blog.blockstack.org; Jones Day, "SEC Qualifies First Issuance of Digital Token." 45:34
🎯 Against ConsensusLewis Cohen dissents mildly from his own colleagues on Subpart C's prospects. He agrees the startup-exemption-plus-Reg-D path is the more likely of the two directions, but insists there will be and should be fundraising exemption users: well-developed projects with a high degree of functionality already built, relatively mature, often internally or self-funded — the ones that invested the time and "the wherewithal" before they needed outside money 35:32–36:10. The implicit claim is that Subpart C is priced for maturity, not for bootstrapping.

Shapiro's other concern is procedural rather than substantive: qualification gives staff a lever. "The public fundraising exemption has to be SEC qualified, which gives them a lot of opportunities during the comment phase to sort of subtly insert KYC requirements" 39:05. He describes this from M&A practice as "shadow requirements… created through course of conduct" — things not in the rules that you do anyway because otherwise the filing does not clear 39:36–39:47. His proposed answer is zero-knowledge verification of the income and net worth test, of the kind MetaLeX already uses via ZK Passport from the Aztec team, so that a purchaser generates a proof locally and the issuer ingests the proof rather than the underlying documents 40:19–40:53. Without it, he warns, the 10% test manufactures "data honeypots of people's proof of residency and proof of income" 40:59.

🔗 ConnectionInvestor caps → compliance architecture. The 10% income-or-net-worth limit is a straight port from Reg A, where it is verified by self-certification through a broker-dealer or transfer agent. Ported into a token offering settled on public infrastructure, the same test either forces an off-chain KYC chokepoint or demands a cryptographic primitive that did not exist when Reg A was written. Shapiro's comment is the rare one that asks the Commission to specify a technology-shaped compliance path rather than a paperwork one. 40:19

🚪 Subpart D and Rule 400: A Promises Test, Not a Decentralisation Test

This is the part Robinson calls "one of the most important parts of all of Reg Crypto" 49:43, and he is right that it is the genuinely novel one.

Rule 400 imposes two conditions. Under Rule 400(a), the issuer has completed or otherwise permanently ceased all the essential managerial efforts it represented or promised under the covered investment contract, and is not making and does not intend to make any new representations or promises to engage in such efforts 48:39. Under Rule 400(b), the issuer files a transition report, which requires an analysis supporting that certification written so a reasonable investor can understand it — "a public on the record argument for why the investment contract is over" 49:10–49:28.

Robinson's characterisation of the shift is the load-bearing sentence of the episode: instead of sufficient decentralisation or lack of control, "this becomes more of a contract law question. What did you promise and did you do it?" 49:28.

The second surprise is the maintenance carve-out. The SEC is explicit that continuing to work on a network you have already built does not keep you inside the regime.

"Once such network or application is functional, it is our view that services to secure, maintain, improve or enhance such a network or application or its functionality or to facilitate network effects, whether through sponsoring or funding development projects or other similar activities would not constitute essential managerial efforts." 53:10 — the proposing release, read out by Robinson.

The third surprise is who can use it. The safe harbour is available to projects that relied on the startup exemption, on the fundraising exemption, or on neither — a project that never complied with either exemption can file a transition report certifying that it did what it promised, and take its tokens outside federal securities law for the Commission's purposes 49:51–50:12.

💡 Key InsightThat third feature is the retroactive amnesty the industry has wanted for eight years, and it is doing far more work than its placement suggests. Every token that launched between 2017 and 2026 is, on this design, eligible to file its way out — without ever having used the new exemptions. Brennan calls it "transitional relief through you affirmatively doing this filing and opting in and getting comfort" 54:26–54:46. It is also why she and others say the exit is "very manageable" and possibly "too permissive."
"I think that bar is very manageable, almost probably too permissive, but it should provide a lot of relief." 54:20 — Sarah Brennan on the essential-managerial-efforts exit test.

Brennan's specific reading is that the exit test resolves to "functionality plus promises fulfilled" 54:13, and that this is a very different bar from the one the industry assumed. "We used to think essential managerial efforts meant you had to like abandon the project" — nobody was even sure Ethereum cleared it, because of upgrades and continual work 54:13–54:19. Her residual worry is the reverse direction: certainty that you will not be pulled back in. Ecosystem development support and funding are clearly fine; "material substantial network upgrades" she is less sure about 55:44–55:55. She wants a clean exit precisely because a regime you can pop in and out of is not administrable.

⚡ TensionBrennan holds two positions at once and says so — the exit bar is "probably too permissive," and the exit is "a little less clear since the exit's so early" (54:20, 55:21). Those are not contradictory so much as a description of a poorly-shaped door: easy to walk through, hard to know whether you are still through it a year later. She wants the Commission to fix the second without tightening the first.
🔍 Source Verified✅ The safe harbour and its conditions are Rule 400 of proposed Regulation Crypto Assets, Release No. 33-11434, File No. S7-2026-27, proposed 18 August 2026 and published in the Federal Register on 21 August 2026. The transition report is filed on new Form TR; the startup exemption's notice of reliance is Form NOR; the fundraising exemption's offering statement is Form 1-CRYPTO. Sources: sec.gov/rules-regulations/2026/08/s7-2026-27; Sullivan & Cromwell, "SEC Proposes Regulation Crypto Assets."

Robinson also walks the second, less interesting exit from Subpart C's ongoing reporting: the Reg A-derived route where an issuer suspends reporting once its holder count falls below 300 record holders of the class — "probably going to be irrelevant" for a token, as he says — versus Rule 305(d)(2), under which satisfying Rule 400 or the covered investment contract otherwise ceasing to exist terminates reporting immediately on filing Form TR 47:43–48:10.

🌐 Consensus CheckThe 300-holder route is a genuine Regulation A mechanic, not an invention — Rule 257 lets a Tier 2 issuer suspend its ongoing reporting where the class is held of record by fewer than 300 persons, offers under the qualified statement are no longer ongoing, and the issuer has filed all required reports, by filing Part II of Form 1-Z. Robinson's judgement that it is irrelevant here is sound: a token distributed to retail with no resale restrictions will essentially never have fewer than 300 holders. Source: sec.gov Regulation A guidance for issuers. 47:48

🕳️ The Hole in the Bright Line: Third Parties and the Rebuttable Transition Report

Lewis Cohen's objection is the most technically precise argument in the episode, and it is not an objection to the safe harbour — it is an objection to who bears its uncertainty.

He reads the proposing release against itself, from page 167: if an issuer files a Form TR and represents, intentionally or otherwise, that it has satisfied Rule 400(a), the Commission may take the position that notwithstanding the attempted reliance, the covered investment contract has not ceased to exist, and the reporting, registration and other requirements continue to apply 50:26–51:07.

Cohen's response to that sentence is unambiguous: "That is completely reasonable and appropriate. I don't have any beef with that whatsoever" 51:07. The problem is downstream.

"It gives a bright line. It's like a bright line with a little hole in it." 63:17 — Lewis Cohen on the transition report.

The point of Form TR, he argues, is not only to tell the issuer when it has stopped engaging in securities activity — it is to tell third parties. A dealer, market maker, exchange, marketplace or custodian sees the Form TR filed and concludes it is clear. If the issuer misrepresented, intentionally or inadvertently, the Commission may later say the covered investment contract never ceased — and those third parties have been operating on a representation they had no way to test 51:31–52:11. Cohen is careful about the scope of his complaint: "I'm fine imposing all of that indeterminacy on the person or entity that did the fundraising. Where my concern is is the third parties" 64:24–64:37.

He also notes the layering problem underneath everything: the proposed regulation applies only to crypto assets that are not securities, but never says when a crypto asset is a security.

"The proposed regulation only applies to crypto assets that are not securities, but it never says when a crypto asset is a security." 62:34 — Lewis Cohen on the indeterminacy the proposal does not remove.

Cohen's own comment letter, filed on behalf of a client, asks for the safe harbour to be extended a further step: objective criteria under which non-affiliated third parties dealing with the asset are told their transactions are not securities transactions, leaving the issuer's obligations untouched 30:25–31:42. His fix for the transition report's hole is Congress, and specifically the CLARITY Act — "that's the thing Congress can address and I'm really hopeful… on September 15th" 52:11–52:16.

🌐 Consensus CheckThat hope did not survive. On 15 September 2026 the Senate's cloture vote on the motion to proceed to H.R. 3633, the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act, failed 49–50, far short of the 60 votes required; the House had passed the bill 294–134 on 17 July 2025. Reporting attributes the failure to unresolved ethics provisions on officials' crypto holdings rather than the SEC/CFTC jurisdictional split itself, with the Democratic negotiators who were expected to supply the margin voting no. The practical consequence for this episode: the statutory patch Cohen is relying on is not coming in 2026, and the third-party problem now has to be solved inside the rulemaking or not at all. Sources: CoinDesk, "Crypto Clarity Act flames out in failed U.S. Senate vote" (15 Sep 2026); NPR (15 Sep 2026). 52:19
🧵 Running ThesisReg Crypto solves the issuer's problem and leaves the market's problem open. Every exemption, form and safe-harbour condition is addressed to the issuer; none of them tells an exchange, custodian or market maker what it is holding. Status: developing — the Commission asked for comment on secondary activity, and Cohen, Brennan and Hinkes are all filing on it from different directions.

Andrew Hinkes describes the same gap from the remedies side. His sense — and he is careful that these are not the SEC's exact words — is that the Commission expects a secondary purchaser of a token covered by an investment contract to be entitled to rights and remedies under the securities laws, which is "very different than saying that they take the investment contract as if they were the counterparty" 67:24–67:51. He works it through with an Ethereum hypothetical: if you sell your ETH before the world computer goes live, do you as an original purchaser still have an interest in enforcing the promises? Sometimes yes — with hybrid instruments, yield-bearing tokens, or governance rights — at which point two parties may each have claims on the same investment contract, and you arrive at a "really wild place where maybe Ethereum owes securities like duties… to everyone that's ever bought an ETH" 69:44–69:55.

🗺️ Subpart E: Preemption Fixes Fifty Rulebooks and Stops at the Exit Door

Robinson calls state preemption "under-discussed and equally important" 75:14, and the mechanic is simple: covered investment contracts become covered securities, preempted from state registration requirements, for both the primary offering and the secondary trading that follows, provided the issuer stays current on disclosure. Without it, Robinson notes, whether you could legally resell a token could depend on each state's secondary trading rules 12:58–13:12.

Andrew Hinkes then raises the problem underneath the problem.

"Here we have a regulator saying a transaction of a crypto asset from one user to the other drags along with it the legal rights of a party to an investment contract." 75:40 — Andrew Hinkes on the assumption the whole framework rests on.

It is weird, he says, for two reasons. First, there is no federal private contract law — federal contract law exists for contracting with government entities, but otherwise contract law arises under state law, which is why terms of service name Delaware or New York or Montana 75:57–76:18. Second, an investment contract is a set of promises by an issuer to a purchaser, and those promises could be viewed as a contract. Moving a contract, under most states' law, requires either a novation or an assignment of rights — and assignments are generally accompanied by formalities, including a writing 76:30–77:04.

"The key thing is here that there is no state law that I'm aware of that says a mere token transaction accomplishes an assignment." 77:04 — Andrew Hinkes.

Most crypto transactions are not accompanied by writings, and a material share happen on decentralised exchanges where you do not know your counterparty, let alone document a transfer of legal rights 77:14–77:36. His alternative framing is real property: a servitude attached to land travels with the land, so a right of way survives the sale. Can you create servitudes on tokens? "Well, if there's a state law that says so, sure. But I'm not aware of a state law that says this" 77:36–78:11. In the absence of such a law, whether the assumption underlying all of this regulatory language is supportable under state law is, as he puts it, "a really interesting question for a court to try to resolve."

💡 Key InsightThis is the most consequential unglamorous point in the episode. The proposal's entire secondary-market design assumes that investment-contract rights ride along with the token. Federal securities law can define what counts as a security and preempt state registration; it cannot manufacture a state-law mechanism by which contractual rights transfer without a writing. If a court ever tests that assumption, the answer determines whether the secondary-market rights the SEC contemplates exist at all. 78:11

Sarah Brennan adds the timing dimension. Form TR is genuinely useful — you do not have to have gone through the exempt pathways to use it — but there is no post-hoc amnesty for transactions up to that point, and there is no certainty of state preemption once you are out. "It just says federally you're good" 79:29–80:04.

"A real weakness of this is the test to exit is permissive where I think that you would likely constitute a security under state law." 80:14 — Sarah Brennan, naming the Martin Act as the live exposure.
🔗 ConnectionPermissive exit → state-law exposure → plaintiffs' bar. Brennan's chain is worth stating plainly: the easier the federal exit, the wider the gap between federal and state characterisation, and New York's Martin Act is the standing example because it reaches conduct federal law does not, with a far lower scienter requirement. The federal off-ramp does not deliver you to open road; it delivers you to fifty state on-ramps. 80:24

🎭 The Moral Hazard: Silence Is Still Cheaper Than Disclosure

Two of the guests identify the same structural defect from opposite ends, and it is the proposal's most serious design question.

Gabriel Shapiro's version: because the trigger for an investment contract is what the team said, a team that says nothing has no investment contract and therefore no disclosure obligation.

"I do think yes it sets a bit of a moral hazard and it's one that especially advantages highly credentialed teams." 24:24 — Gabriel Shapiro.

His illustration is pointed and directed at the host: if Jacob Robinson launched a token, people would trust him because they know him and know he is a lawyer — "you may say nothing about what you're going to do with it, but people will still buy it, and then you're going to be outside this disclosure regime" 24:40–25:09. Reputation substitutes for promises; the projects most able to raise without saying anything are precisely the ones best placed to escape the disclosure regime entirely.

Shapiro is honest that this is a return to an older view. The marketing-based approach — that an investment contract should depend specifically on what the team promised — was widely considered and then "became rejected by sort of rough social consensus of securities lawyers and crypto lawyers over time," principally because Howey and the securities laws are not that narrow 21:03–21:50. His case in point is LBRY.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Shapiro's recollection of LBRY is close but the emphasis is slightly off in a way that matters. In SEC v. LBRY, Inc., Judge Paul J. Barbadoro (D.N.H.) granted the SEC summary judgment on 7 November 2022, holding LBC an unregistered security under Sections 5(a) and 5(c) of the Securities Act. The court's reasoning relied heavily on LBRY's own statements to prospective purchasers — blog posts, tweets and public remarks that led buyers to expect LBC would appreciate as the company developed the network — rather than on incentive alignment alone, though the company's large retained holding featured in the economic-reality analysis. The court also rejected LBRY's fair-notice defence. The correction cuts in Reg Crypto's favour: a promises-and-representations test is closer to how the LBRY court actually reasoned than Shapiro's framing implies. Sources: Morrison Foerster, "Court Rules LBRY Token Is a Security"; Skadden client alert, Nov 2022. 22:01

Shapiro's own countervailing argument is that market structure will supply the pressure the rule does not: the exemptions cover issuer liability but say nothing about exchange liability or market maker liability, so an ecosystem will form around projects that have the disclosures and the SEC qualification, and non-participants will be disadvantaged because "people want the disclosures… they will want the legitimacy of it" 25:41–26:31. On balance he is bullish, and reads the proposal as a legitimised, legalised ICO era: "if you're in the ICO business and ICOs have suddenly been both legitimized and legalized, I don't see how it could not but be bullish" 26:56–27:20.

Rodrigo Seira identifies the mirror-image failure mode inside the exit rather than the entry.

"A lot of projects are going to look at that and say, that's easy. Then I'll make very limited promises and I'll say that they're completed." 58:48 — Rodrigo Seira.

Seira is complimentary about the disclosure work — it has been clear for a while that forcing crypto into existing securities disclosures was not going to solve the problem, because those documents did not surface the information crypto holders care about 57:46–58:17. His concern is that the same promises-and-representations concept that creates the investment contract also terminates it, so an issuer can engineer a fast exit with a minimal promise set. His proposed fix is interpretive breadth: the concept of promises and representations must encompass all of an issuer's statements and actions, not just what it put in the disclosure document, or the rule produces "less like projects disclosing less about their managerial efforts" 59:01–59:38.

💡 Key InsightShapiro and Seira are describing one defect from two ends of the same pipe. If the trigger is speech, then the cheapest compliance strategy at every stage is silence — silence at launch avoids the regime, and a minimal promise set at exit escapes it quickly. The proposal's answer has to be a broad reading of "representations," which is exactly the indeterminacy Cohen complains about elsewhere. Narrow the concept and you get the moral hazard; broaden it and you get the vagueness. 59:38

Miles Jennings, in the archival clip Robinson brings in, states the industry-level cost of that equilibrium.

"There's been a lack of transparency and that was because the regulatory framework basically incentivized people to be opaque." 60:47 — Miles Jennings, a16z crypto.

His conclusion is that this is not a problem the existing regulatory scheme can solve, and that no other startup sector has to operate under a rule that says be quiet about what you are building 61:12–61:31. Robinson's own summing-up is even-handed: the downside of Reg Crypto is that it could disincentivise promises and public representations by attaching securities law to them; the upside is that it gives projects that need to make public promises a lawful way to make them while distributing tokens 61:32–61:53.

🧭 Decentralisation Loses Its Legal Job and Keeps Its Economic One

One consequence of the promises-based test is that decentralisation is no longer the pivot of the securities analysis. Both Cohen and Shapiro think it stays central for other reasons.

Cohen's argument is a product argument. Decentralisation "needs to have a product market fit" — people value Bitcoin and Ethereum precisely for their foundational decentralisation, and as you move down the scale the trade-offs between usability, practicability, security and decentralisation have been a pain point 70:34–71:01. He wants builders pushing on it even though it is no longer the central securities-law question, "because if no people want to use the product, it doesn't matter if it's a security or not a security" 71:10–71:16. He adds a point about developer liability: developers will not do the work decentralisation requires "if they're terrified that they're going to be prosecuted" 71:37–71:43.

Shapiro's argument is a cycle argument. The current trend is toward what he calls "the fintech of crypto" — isolated areas of transparency and composability wrapped in a centralised user experience 72:09–72:38. He expects decentralisation to return for two reasons: politics, because the industry is operating under a friendly administration and "people are getting lazy" while a less friendly government sends everyone back to reducing their governmental attack surface 72:48–73:07; and composability, because an asset carrying freeze functions gets risk-mitigated by DeFi protocols — never whitelisted, or whitelisted into separate pools — so the real composability never arrives 73:11–73:53.

📜 AnalogShapiro grounds the second argument in Nick Szabo's essay "Money, Blockchains, and Social Scalability," which he calls one of the most important texts in crypto: the more you reduce trust assumptions, the more things scale 74:35–74:52. Applied here, the claim is that decentralisation is not a legal posture that regulation can make optional — it is a scaling property, and demand for scale will re-create demand for it regardless of what the securities test says.
🔗 ConnectionPromises test → decentralisation becomes contingent. Robinson makes the precise doctrinal point: giving up unilateral control or becoming sufficiently decentralised is not a necessary exit from securities laws — unless it was part of what the project promised, in which case it becomes a condition of that project's exit 56:52–57:24. Decentralisation moves from a regulatory threshold to a contractual term. Projects that promise it are bound to it; projects that never mention it never owe it.

Cohen's broader theoretical objection surfaces here too. The Howey orange grove had a natural economic reason to hold the asset: it produced income. A crypto asset does not. If a secondary market does not develop, if demand does not increase, if the network does not go from zero to one, the asset has no value 33:24–33:45.

"Secondary market here is not a nice to have. It's a must have." 33:47 — Lewis Cohen on why token offerings are structurally unlike private placements.
🔍 Source Verified✅ The "seed of a new type of thing that's unknown to anyone" Cohen refers to at 65:56 is his own scholarship — "the Parable of the Strowrange Seeds," a hypothetical in The Ineluctable Modality of Securities Law: Why Fungible Crypto Assets Are Not Securities (Cohen, Strong, Lewin & Chen, 2022), a 107-page paper analysing 276 federal appellate and Supreme Court investment-contract opinions. Its central claim is the one he presses throughout this episode: marketplace-based secondary transfers do not themselves create investment contract transactions. Source: SSRN abstract 4282385. 65:56
📜 AnalogCohen's Telegram reference is doing precise work. Judge Castel's March 2020 decision found that the "security" was neither the purchase agreements nor the Gram itself but the entire scheme, including the expectation that initial purchasers would resell into the public market. That is the doctrinal root of Cohen's distinction: in an ordinary private placement the initial purchaser has an economic reason to hold, whereas a token's initial purchaser is structurally a distributor. Source: SEC v. Telegram Group Inc., No. 19-cv-9439 (S.D.N.Y. 24 Mar 2020). 32:35

🗓️ What Happens Next: Comment File, Timeline, and a Failed Backstop

Robinson is explicit that none of this is law. Comments were due within 60 days of the announcement, anyone can comment, and the file number is S7-2026-27 80:51–81:06.

"They ask 175 questions that are numbered and there's economic analysis questions." 81:18 — Sarah Brennan on the scale of the comment request.

Brennan's observation is worth sitting with: the granularity is an invitation, and the responses will not converge. "You can tell every comment letter they received said something that was the exact opposite of it or it was like left field" 81:36–81:52.

Shapiro's headline comment is about the subject of the financial disclosures. Reg Crypto's financial reporting is about the issuer's balance sheet, but for a decentralised protocol the labs company is not what holders have rights to — and there may be three or four relevant entities. What a token holder should care about is the protocol's own financial performance: the quality of protocol revenues, related deals that may be artificially inflating TVL, and what happens when such a deal terminates 81:56–83:12. His proposal is a separate disclosure category treating the protocol as an entity at least as material as the issuer — "and it doesn't seem like that's currently reflected" 83:29.

💡 Key InsightShapiro's comment is the most structurally ambitious one in the episode. Every other critique asks the Commission to adjust a threshold or clarify a term. This one asks it to recognise a new reporting entity — the protocol — with no legal personality, no management to sign, and no auditor relationship. It is the natural endpoint of the SEC's own premise that traditional disclosures do not surface what crypto holders need, and it is the item most likely to be deferred to a later rulemaking.

The mechanics from here, per Robinson: the comment period closes, the Division of Corporation Finance and the Office of the General Counsel work through the letters, revise the rule text, and draft an adopting release responding to the major arguments. For a proposal of this size that takes months; the Commission then votes on a final rule. "Realistically, we're looking at a final rule sometime in 2027" 83:42–84:08.

Congress is the wild card, and Robinson's conditional was that if CLARITY passed while the rulemaking was pending, the SEC would likely conform the rules to the statute, because the two frameworks aim at similar problems in different ways 84:11–84:23. Chair Atkins addressed the same dynamic directly:

"By moving forward with our current statutory authority, we can help ensure that America remains the world's premier destination to innovate, to raise capital." 84:50 — Chair Atkins on proceeding while Congress works.
🌐 Consensus CheckAs of mid-September 2026, that conditional has resolved in the direction the episode did not price. The CLARITY Act's cloture vote failed on 15 September; a future attempt requires a new cloture motion, and reporting treats comprehensive Senate market-structure work as effectively finished for 2026. Two consequences follow for Reg Crypto. First, the Commission is now proceeding without the statutory conformity question hanging over the adopting release — a simplification. Second, the specific gaps the practitioners wanted Congress to fill (Cohen's third-party problem, Brennan's state preemption after exit, transitional amnesty for pre-rule transactions) have no legislative vehicle, so they must be solved inside the rulemaking or left open. Sources: CoinDesk and NPR, 15 September 2026. 84:11

Andrew Hinkes gets the closing word, and it is the fairest summary of what is actually new.

"This is the first proactive prospective forward view looking thing that we've seen out of the SEC which is a wonderful thing for the industry." 86:24 — Andrew Hinkes.

Previous SEC efforts, he says, documented the significance of past events, analysed conduct through the lens of "we believe this violated the securities laws," or produced frameworks describing how the securities laws would view conduct after the fact. This is the first document to his memory that says: if you want to sell crypto assets in a fundraising transaction, here is how to do it 86:26–87:04.

Phase 3: Specific Item Breakdown

🟣 Subpart A — General Rules and the Disclosure Playbook

The definitions, the cross-cutting conditions, and Rule 103 — the crypto-native disclosure schedule that both exemptions are built on.

What It Covers: Definitions (including "covered investment contract" — an investment contract in which a crypto asset that is not itself a security is the only asset subject to the contract), general provisions including Rule 101(a)'s non-exclusivity, inflation adjustment of the offering limits, bad-actor disqualification, and Rule 103's disclosure items. Rule 103 spans the representations creating the investment contract, the offering, the subject crypto asset, management and conflicts, the associated network and plan of development, security and source code, token economics and allocations, governance, ecosystem, and risk factors.

Why It Matters: Rule 103 is the substantive innovation. The exemptions are ported plumbing; the disclosure content is the part written from scratch because, as Rodrigo Seira puts it, existing securities disclosures "weren't surfacing the information that was important to crypto holders" 57:55. It is also the schedule an issuer must keep current on its own website for the whole four-year startup runway.

🟣 Subpart B — The Startup Exemption

$5 million, once, over four years, on a self-filed notice, with retail eligible and no resale restrictions.

What It Covers: A one-time exemption per crypto asset. File a notice of reliance on Form NOR to start a four-year clock; publish and maintain Rule 103 disclosures free of charge on the project's own website; observe the bad-actor and anti-fraud conditions; exit at four years or earlier by filing Form TR. Covers distributions as well as sales — airdrops, staking-related distributions, governance and gas-fee distributions, network-security rewards, and testing payments.

Why It Matters: This is the one practitioners expect to be used, because it is genuinely lightweight: no SEC qualification, no audited financials, no intermediary, no resale restrictions, and it stacks with a Reg D private placement. Shapiro: "that one I'm very confident will be good and will be used" 14:54.

🟣 Subpart C — The Fundraising Exemption

Regulation A+ re-cut for tokens — two tiers, SEC qualification required, audited financials above $20 million.

What It Covers: Tier 1 up to $20 million per 12 months (unaudited financial statements permitted); Tier 2 up to $75 million per 12 months (audited financials required). File an offering statement on Form 1-CRYPTO built around Rule 103; go through staff review until qualified; then file annual, semi-annual and current reports. Non-accredited purchasers capped at 10% of the greater of annual income or net worth. Selling securityholders limited to 30% of the offering in year one; affiliates capped at $6 million (Tier 1) and $22.5 million (Tier 2) thereafter. No time limit — the caps are 12-month rolling.

Why It Matters: It is the only path to large-scale retail token distribution, and it carries the entire historical risk of the episode: Reg A+ was almost never used by crypto or by conventional startups, for cost and friction reasons that Subpart C only partly relieves. Whether the SEC processes qualifications quickly — Shapiro's "are they fast at it" question 46:33 — will decide it.

🟣 Subpart D — The Investment Contract Safe Harbor (Rule 400)

The first formal exit from federal securities law for a token, gated on promise-performance rather than decentralisation.

What It Covers: Rule 400(a): the issuer has completed or permanently ceased all essential managerial efforts it represented or promised, and is making no new such promises. Rule 400(b): the issuer files Form TR with an analysis, written for a reasonable investor, supporting that certification. Available whether or not the issuer used either exemption. Maintenance, security, improvement and network-effect facilitation of an already-functional network expressly do not count as essential managerial efforts.

Why It Matters: It converts an eight-year-old ontological question — is this thing decentralised enough? — into a documentary one. It is also the retroactive relief channel for every token already in the market. Its defect is symmetrical: the Commission can disbelieve a Form TR after the fact, and third parties who relied on it have no protection.

🟣 Subpart E — State Preemption

Covered investment contracts become covered securities, preempted from state registration for primary and secondary transactions — while the contract lives.

What It Covers: Preemption of state securities registration requirements for offerings under Reg Crypto and the secondary trading that follows, conditioned on the issuer remaining current with its disclosure obligations. State antifraud authority and notice filings are unaffected.

Why It Matters: Without it, Robinson notes, resale legality could turn on fifty separate secondary-trading regimes 13:02. With it, the unresolved question is what happens after the federal exit — Brennan's Martin Act point — and whether state contract law even supports the transfer of investment-contract rights that the whole design assumes.

🟣 Regulation A — The Mini IPO

The $75 million public-offering exemption that Subpart C is copied from, and that almost nobody uses.

What It Covers: Tier 1 up to $20 million and Tier 2 up to $75 million per 12 months, on a Form 1-A offering circular the SEC must qualify. Tier 2 requires audited financials, caps non-accredited investment at 10% of the greater of income or net worth, preempts state registration, and carries ongoing Forms 1-K, 1-SA and 1-U, suspendable under Rule 257 via Form 1-Z below 300 record holders.

Why It Matters: Its failure rate is the episode's central empirical warning. Brennan and Shapiro agree it did not work for conventional equity either, because it is not different enough from an IPO to be worth the cost. Blockstack's 2019 Stacks offering — the first and effectively only SEC-qualified token offering — took ten months and $2.8 million.

🟣 Regulation Crowdfunding — The File-and-Go Exemption

$5 million a year from the general public with no SEC qualification, and the shape Subpart B borrows.

What It Covers: Up to $5 million per 12 months, disclosures filed and posted rather than qualified, retail eligible with per-investor limits — but conducted only through a registered funding portal or broker-dealer intermediary.

Why It Matters: Brennan's diagnosis of why it failed is the one to carry into Subpart B: "nobody wants that many people on their cap table. It's hard to administer" 42:27. Tokens are the one asset class for which that objection inverts — a large, dispersed holder base is the product, not a problem — which is the strongest structural argument that Subpart B will outperform its ancestor.

🟣 Regulation D — The Default Path

Uncapped, accredited-only, cheap, no qualification — the exemption the market actually uses, and Reg Crypto's real competitor.

What It Covers: Unlimited capital raised generally from accredited investors, on a Form D notice filing with no SEC review. Securities are restricted and cannot be freely resold. Preempted from state registration.

Why It Matters: Robinson's summary of the JOBS Act era is that the market went where friction was lowest, and that was Reg D 43:46. Rule 101(a) makes Reg Crypto non-exclusive, so Reg D does not go away — it sits alongside Subpart B in the expected default structure and remains the reason Shapiro doubts Subpart C's economics.
