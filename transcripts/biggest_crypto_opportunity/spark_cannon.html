The Stablecoin Neobank Bet: KAST's Founder Says Cards Are A Loss Leader And Most Rivals Die Within 12 Months

Source When Shift Happens · Date n/a · 105 min
Domain Crypto & Markets
Duration 105 min
Date
Speakers
  • Raagulan "Rags" Pathy — Founder & CEO, KAST (global stablecoin neobank); former Circle executive; based in Singapore
  • Kevin Follonier — Host, When Shift Happens
Full Mode

Executive Summary

  • Pathy left Circle because he concluded the stablecoin issuer is the wrong layer to own — issuer revenue is a hostage of the Fed funds rate, while the application layer above it is uncapped. He calls the consumer fintech built on stablecoin rails "the greatest TAM opportunity of our lifetime," and frames the current stampede of exchanges and wallets bolting cards onto their apps as belated confirmation of a thesis he acted on two years early. 41:03
  • His most falsifiable claim is a consolidation prediction with a date on it: within twelve months, most crypto neobanks will give up and shut down. The argument is unit economics, not scorn — "cards in themselves are not a profitable business," so anyone whose entire product is a card is funding a permanent loss with token incentives, and investors stop paying for that once the path to profitability is invisible. 44:46
  • KAST cancelled its own token and is converting community points into tokenized equity instead, at a conversion targeted for Q4 and priced off the last round. Pathy accepts this will be "misunderstood" for years, and answers the fairness complaint with an attack on the standard token structure: founders keep equity, take a token allocation, and control an unaudited foundation treasury, then exit through OTC desks while the community holds a chart that goes down 90–95%. 84:30
  • The business grew roughly 3x through a 50% Bitcoin drawdown, which he attributes to having quietly stopped being a crypto company. More than half of KAST's users are not crypto-native; he sizes crypto's addressable market at roughly $2.5 trillion against a US-dollar market perhaps twenty times larger, and says virtual USD and EUR accounts are what pulled in non-crypto users. 31:38
  • The real moat, on his account, is the unglamorous part: instant fiat payout rails into 50 countries, built through 20–30 providers by a team of about 100 people — and he refuses to white-label any of it. He describes it as a wall the company is still pushing through, and the reason he expects competitors to need "a year or two catching up" on infrastructure that never appears in a product screenshot. 46:00
  • Two credibility events sit inside this episode: a terms-of-service clause that said deposited assets became KAST's property, and the cancellation of a token that users had earned through 1.5–2% fees. Pathy calls the first a drafting failure now rewritten, and the second a hard call taken with investor dilution to fund it — but both cut against the trust he names as the one risk that could kill the company. 68:38

💵 Pathy Left Circle Because The Issuer Is The Wrong Layer To Own

The single most consequential decision in this interview happened before KAST existed. Pathy was an executive at Circle, the issuer of USDC, and walked away from the issuer side of the business on a structural judgment about where value accrues.

"My thesis, the reason why I left Circle was this is the greatest TAM opportunity of our lifetime and the biggest thing that you could do was not to build a stable coin."

His reasoning is precise and worth separating from the usual founder narrative. Stablecoin issuers make money principally on the reserves backing their float — which means, as he puts it, "their success is determined by the Fed rate" 41:44. USDC and USDT are enormous, but the revenue line is a derivative of monetary policy that no operator controls. The application sitting on top of those rails has no such ceiling: it can charge for cards, FX, payouts, credit, business accounts and wealth products, and it compounds with every additional product shipped.

💡 Key InsightPathy is making a layer-selection argument that applies well beyond stablecoins — when the revenue of a lower layer is set by an exogenous variable (here, interest rates), the durable business is usually the layer above it, where pricing power is endogenous. 41:36

Asked directly why every crypto company is suddenly building a neobank, he gives what he calls "the no BS version": people have realised that a great deal of crypto has not delivered value to ordinary users, but that "hundreds of millions of people, potentially billions of people could really do with a fintech that sits on top of crypto rails and works at fiat" 41:10. The framing is deliberately deflationary about crypto itself. The interesting thing is not the chain; it is that the chain makes a genuinely global consumer bank buildable for the first time.

That ambition is not rhetorical. He describes the original vision paper as "a global neo bank built on stable coins where you could move money between crypto rails and fiat rails and move them across, you know, 200 countries," and immediately concedes: "It's never ever been done before... It is a ridiculous ambition because no one's done it before" 12:40.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed. KAST announced an $80M Series A on 9 March 2026 at a $600M valuation, co-led by QED Investors and Left Lane Capital, with Peak XV Partners, HSG and DST Global Partners participating. The release confirms Pathy is a former Circle executive and dates the company's founding to July 2024. Sources: KAST press release via PR Newswire (9 Mar 2026); CoinDesk (9 Mar 2026).

On the show, Pathy declines to confirm the valuation directly — "we don't confirm or deny the valuation but it is what's reported" 62:58 — and then makes a more interesting claim: KAST reached that mark in "closer to like less than one and a half years," and is "worth more now" 63:02. The company's arithmetic on time-to-scale is the part he is proudest of: zero to roughly $100 million in annualised revenue inside two and a half years, against the four-to-five years he attributes to Revolut, Nubank and Wise 34:38.

📜 AnalogPathy repeatedly reaches for the 2013–2015 neobank cohort as the template — Nubank founded 2013, Revolut 2015 — and treats stablecoins as the equivalent enabling shift that mobile banking was then. The tell is his framing of the competition: not other crypto apps, but the previous generation of fintechs that already won. 55:46

A striking piece of humility sits underneath the ambition. Kevin pushes him on comparisons to crypto rivals and Pathy refuses the frame entirely, pointing out that Nubank and Revolut hold roughly one-hundredth of JP Morgan's customer assets — "so if they're 100th of a JP Morgan and we're 100th of them... what are we? Nothing, right?" 55:54. His conclusion: "we have decades ahead of work" 56:23.

🧱 The Moat Is Payout Plumbing Nobody Can See, And He Will Not Sell It

If there is one durable idea to take from this episode, it is Pathy's insistence that the defensible asset is the least photogenic part of the stack. Everything a user experiences — the card, the app, the concierge — he treats as replicable. What he thinks is not replicable is the machinery that moves fiat in and out of dozens of jurisdictions.

The scale he describes: direct instant payments into 50 countries, growing; 20 to 30 payment providers behind them; and roughly 100 people building "the tech, the compliance infrastructure, the the the banking partnerships" 48:07. He expects money movement alone to become 20–30% of KAST's revenue 47:56.

"We have to build so much infrastructure to get through this wall but once you get through the wall it's very hard for others to follow." 49:06

The texture of why it is hard is the best reporting in the interview. Pathy describes a provider chain that is rarely acknowledged publicly: KAST would prefer to work directly with a bank in each country, but in practice "we're working with a provider who's working with a bank but sometimes they're working with another provider who's working with a bank" 15:35. When a customer's payment fails, KAST takes full ownership of the failure while, behind the scenes, "we've got people running around... working with the partner or their partners" 15:49.

Kevin adds a detail from a KAST employee — that entire departments exist at the company purely to absorb other companies' problems 12:06 — and Pathy does not deny it. His summary of the industry's state is that everybody is learning simultaneously: "we're pushing these new providers right to the extreme in ways that they haven't seen before cuz they haven't seen that kind of volume before" 13:33.

🔗 ConnectionProvider fragility → the fee debate. Pathy's defence of KAST's non-cheapest pricing 19:23 only makes sense against this backdrop: a business whose failure modes come from four-deep counterparty chains needs margin to absorb them, and a zero-margin card cannot fund a team that chases other people's outages. 15:35

He is also unusually candid that year two was harder than year one. The first product — deposit stablecoins, spend on a card — "just works." Adding payment rails and further products "is when things get really into a grind. And I would say the last year has been a massive grind" 14:02.

The strategic conclusion is the part rivals should read carefully. Asked whether KAST will white-label the rails, he says no: "we think it's a secret sauce, it's very hard to build but once it's built it's a secret sauce and so instead of giving it to others we're going to keep it for ourselves and our customers because that is what will keep people sticky with us" 48:38. Instead, the infrastructure gets packaged into KAST for Business, which he positions as a global Ramp or Brex competitor 48:30.

📋 Protocol Cheat Sheet
  • Ask any stablecoin card company how many payout corridors are direct-to-bank versus provider-intermediated — the chain depth is the real risk metric.
  • Ask what share of revenue comes from interchange versus everything else; a card-only revenue line is the failure pattern Pathy describes.
  • Check whether the "non-custodial" claim means exportable keys or only on-chain visibility inside a smart contract you cannot exit.
  • Check for licensed third-party custodians by name and for a stated bankruptcy-remote structure.
  • Read the terms of service section on what happens to assets on deposit — the KAST episode proves it is not boilerplate.

💳 Cards Are A Loss Leader — Which Is Why He Expects Most Rivals Dead In 12 Months

This is the most quotable and most testable section of the episode. Pathy's position is that the entire wave of exchanges and wallets "slapping on a card" 42:23 is a business-model error that will resolve itself violently.

"Cards in themselves are not a profitable business. If you do just a card you'll never make money." 45:22

He breaks the difficulty into three parts most crypto teams underestimate. First, the physical product and its experience — manufacturing real cards, packaging, the design cycle. Second, concierge and customer support at a volume no wallet has ever handled. Third, and most damaging, the operational tail: "there's just a lot more things that go wrong. You know, decline payments, refunds, all this other stuff, which a lot of crypto companies are not ready to do" 43:50. Then fiat rails are added, and "your life gets infinitely more complex" 44:14.

The prediction is explicit and dated:

"My theory is is that in 12 months time the majority are going to give up on this and shut down and think this is way too hard and there's going to be a few big players." 44:46

Asked why they fail, the answer is not sentiment: "They can't make the economics work. It's as simple as that. They're just losing money and there's no way path that they can see to ever turning profitable" 44:59. The financing consequence follows — investors who cannot see a route to scale stop funding the loss, "and you can't run losses forever" 45:20.

🎯 Against ConsensusPathy calls the top of the crypto-card cycle while it is still being built. The mainstream 2026 view is that every exchange, wallet and chain should ship a card as a distribution play; his view is that distribution without ten adjacent revenue lines is a subsidy with an expiry date. 44:46
⚡ TensionAt 44:46 he forecasts that most of his named peers will shut down within a year; at 54:58 he says "I don't actually think of them as competitors... the pie is just so ridiculously big" and that treating other crypto companies as rivals makes you "an idiot." Both can be sincere — the market is huge and most entrants still fail — but the two framings are doing very different work in the same conversation.

His constructive rule is the one worth stealing. The secret of building any fintech, he says, is to find a niche, get very good at it, then "find at least 10 other things that are going to make you money" 47:11. He cites Coinbase and Robinhood as the proof: "if you go and dig into the statements of Coinbase, Robin Hood and others... both of them talk about having 11 other lines of business that make at least 100 million" in revenue 47:16.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed, and if anything understated. Coinbase's Q4 2025 shareholder letter (12 Feb 2026) reported 12 products generating more than $100M on an annualised basis — half above $250M and two above $1B. Robinhood reported 11 business lines above $100M annualised in Q4 2025 and 13 by its Q2 2026 report. Pathy's "11 each" was the correct figure a quarter or two before recording. Sources: Coinbase Q4'25 shareholder letter; Robinhood Q2 2026 reporting.

Applied to KAST, the same rule produces a concrete forecast: "at cast we anticipate next year more than 50% of our revenue will come from other things which are not cards" 45:33. Cards, in his framing, are "really just an entry point so that people can experience your product, but ultimately they're not the way that you can make money" 45:48.

Kevin presses on the obvious asymmetry — a trading venue like Jupiter starts with high margin and can add a card, while KAST started with the low-margin product and must climb. Pathy accepts the framing and answers with product surface area: KAST is building the full gamut of money movement, a complete business product, a lighter emerging-markets app, plus credit, trading and savings inside them 51:26. He is explicit that people calling KAST a card business "are absolutely wrong. There's no way — the card was not in my original vision paper" 49:21.

🧵 Running ThesisVelocity of shipping, not any single product, is the compounding asset. Pathy's internal mantra is "shut up and ship" 53:06, and his bet is that in one to two years KAST is simply too far ahead on product count to catch. Status: developing — it is unfalsifiable in the short run, and it is the same argument every fast-shipping startup makes.

🪙 KAST Killed Its Own Token — And Indicts The Entire Token Playbook On The Way Out

The hardest exchange in the episode is Kevin's, and he does not soften it. Users, himself included, spent heavily on KAST partly because cashback was paid in points explicitly framed as converting into a future token — cashback rates of "three five six 10% 12% depending on the tiers" against fees of 1.5–2%, which made the fee question a non-issue 83:52. Then, weeks before recording, KAST announced there would be no token.

"How can the community that trusted you and funded you not feel cheated here?" 84:47

Pathy's answer begins with a principle — "in business, you have to be willing to make hard decisions and you have to be willing to be misunderstood for perhaps a long time" 84:54 — and then moves to evidence. Effectively every comparable token, he says, is down more than 90–95%, including those of direct competitors 85:32. Launching one would force KAST into supporting the price with buybacks, "and therefore you starve the business. You're unable to raise more money, unable to build a bigger business" 86:38. The token tanks anyway, and the company takes the reputational hit twice: "you're going to cop it either way" 87:04.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed. KAST emailed users on 2 July that KAST Points — previously framed as converting one-to-one into a future token — would instead convert into tokenized equity, with details targeted for Q4 2026. Coverage notes the open questions Pathy does not fully resolve on air: conversion ratio, what equity rights attach, and what liquidity mechanism, if any, exists. Sources: Crypto Briefing; KuCoin news desk summary of the KAST announcement.

The replacement structure, as described on air: the notional dollar value of points converts to equity at the valuation prevailing at conversion, "sort of a one for one" 92:31, targeted for Q4 92:24. It will not be listed — "we don't want to list that tokenized equity because it makes fundraising very complex" 91:51 — with liquidity instead coming from periodic private buybacks "every 6 months or every 12 months," the pattern he attributes to Stripe and Revolut while private 91:56. He also floats card upgrades and spend credits as alternative redemption paths 92:19.

The detail that most supports his good faith is one he says has not been reported: "we all had to take a dilution... to create space so that this could be an outcome for the community" 88:26. Existing shareholders gave up percentage ownership to fund a community allocation that did not previously exist in the cap table.

Then comes the broadside. Pathy's critique of how tokens are actually done is more specific than most on-record criticism from someone inside the industry:

"The way that tokens are done is complete BS. So few of them have any linkage to the actual outcomes that the founders are getting." 89:10

He lays out three pockets. Founders keep a significant chunk of equity. They receive a token allocation whose relationship to that equity is undefined — "the token doesn't have any ownership usually in the equity" 89:30. And they control the foundation, "another pool of money which has no accountability. They're not publishing what they're doing with that money" 90:26. Exits then happen quietly: "the investors figure out a way to cash out through OTC deals... so do the founders. The foundations go and sell some, convert it to stable coins" 93:44.

The self-implicating version is what gives the passage force. Pathy notes that launching a token would have been "super easy out" for him personally — keep all equity, sell tokens and foundation allocations via OTC, "having a great life" 90:46. Instead, by his own account, he has never sold a single share, invested more than $5 million of his own money buying out early shareholders, and sat under a $12,000-a-month salary cap that applied to him along with everyone else 63:46.

🔗 ConnectionToken cancellation → fee tolerance. The cashback-in-points model is what made KAST's 1.5–2% fees acceptable to price-sensitive users in the first place. Removing the token removes the offset, which raises the stakes on the fee argument in the next section considerably. 84:00
🧵 Running Thesis"The harder path compounds." It appears in the token decision, the decision never to sell equity, the refusal to white-label infrastructure, and the choice to eat cost rather than cut FX revenue. Status: confirmed as a consistent stated philosophy; unresolved as an outcome, because the Q4 instrument does not exist yet.

🔐 The Deposit-Ownership Blow-Up And The Distinction Most "Non-Custodial" Cards Hide

Kevin raises the terms-of-service controversy without euphemism: people discovered that once money is deposited on KAST, "the money isn't theirs anymore" 69:03.

Pathy calls it a drafting failure. The terms are long, the company was moving fast, and the custodial reality — "you give the money to that platform and then it's a debt to the user" 69:36 — was, he says, always how KAST operated but "probably wasn't clear enough in our T's and C's which got pulled out by someone and made into a big deal" 69:48. His mitigation list: licensed custodians holding a large share of funds, bankruptcy-remote structures being clarified, a spend on external auditors, and a three-month due-diligence exercise by a Big Four firm during the Series A that reconciled anonymised transaction data line by line 69:55.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed, with detail Pathy leaves out. KAST's terms stated that transferring virtual assets to KAST was treated as a sale, that "ownership of the Virtual Assets transfers to KAST," and that in-app records were ledger entries in USD rather than account balances. The issue surfaced after a multi-day public exchange between Ether.fi CEO Mike Silagadze and Pathy that began over card fees; Pathy responded that funds were custodied with BitGo and Fireblocks, and KAST amended the terms to state users may redeem or withdraw unspent balances at any time. Sources: The Defiant; Crypto Briefing.
📜 AnalogPathy's own framing of the stakes, offered earlier in the episode, is Credit Suisse: a fintech "is really just a place where people put money... and they could all leave tomorrow, you know, and you'd have no business" 09:30. Credit Suisse was founded in 1856 and was absorbed by UBS in an emergency weekend rescue in March 2023 — 167 years of franchise value ended in about 72 hours. His stated conclusion is that trust, security and regulatory risk are the categories that can sink a company, as distinct from the ordinary risks you must take to move fast. 09:35

The genuinely educational part is the taxonomy he draws afterwards, and it is a test readers can apply to any crypto card on the market. There are three states, not two:

Custodial — the platform holds the assets and owes you a balance. This is where most of KAST sits today, and where Binance, Coinbase, Revolut and essentially every bank sit. Pathy estimates 98% of customers use custodial products, and notes the major exchanges are "20x the size" of self-custodial wallets 74:01.

Non-custodial — "they can see on chain where it is... the money is here. You can go and look it up on chain right but you don't have the keys" 77:52. Funds typically sit locked in a smart contract. If the company fails, "you'd have to go through some process" involving a third party to retrieve them 78:08.

Self-custodial — "you own the keys. You put the keys into another wallet and money's in that wallet" 78:15.

💡 Key InsightPathy's explanation of why competitors stop at non-custodial is the most useful mechanical detail in the episode: card authorisation creates a settlement gap. If a user holds exportable keys, they can move funds out between the swipe and settlement while the authorisation has already been approved against a balance that no longer exists 76:52. That is why genuinely self-custodial cards must impose spending limits — a constraint, not a design preference. 78:41

At recording, KAST used Privy wallets for its Earn product, making that portion non-custodial already, with a broader option promised "within the next couple of weeks" 72:46. Kevin presses the obvious follow-up — in a failure, does the customer still have to trust KAST's characterisation of which pot was whose? — and Pathy's answer is that full self-custody is the only structural escape: "then you hold the keys, right, there's nothing anyone can do" 76:24.

⚠️ Unverified — no primary source located for the general release of KAST's self-custodial option to all users. The claim was forward-looking at recording ("hopefully by the time this is released it'll be out there"), and no announcement confirming a full rollout was found. Treat as pending.

He is honest about the trade-off, which is more than most: self-custody means managing chains, gas, swapping and bridging yourself, and accepting that "if it's USDC, you can only take out USDC" 78:58. His estimate is that only about 10% of users want this 73:23, and his stated position is that they should have it anyway.

📈 3x Growth Through A 50% Drawdown — Because The Customer Stopped Being A Crypto Customer

Kevin sets the scene with real numbers: at their last conversation, roughly eight or nine months earlier, Bitcoin traded around $120K; at recording it was near $60K, down about 50% 31:38. Over that same window, KAST tripled.

Pathy first corrects the drawdown picture, and his correction is more brutal than the setup: "when you say Bitcoin is down 50%. That's one factor, but altcoins are down probably 80% and even strong ones like Solana are probably down 65%" 32:13. His summary: "we basically have gone from ultra bull to ultra bare" 32:23.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed at the time of recording, and his altcoin framing is accurate. Bitcoin's all-time high was $126,296 on 6 October 2025; by early August 2026 BTC traded near $64,350, roughly a 50% drawdown. Solana's January 2025 high was about $293; at $101 today that is a decline of roughly 65–66%, matching his estimate almost exactly. Sources: CoinGecko spot data (14 Sep 2026: BTC $77,568, SOL $101.06); market coverage of the 6 Oct 2025 high.

His explanation for growing through it is a TAM argument. "People look at the TAM of crypto. I look at the TAM of crypto, it's like two and a half trillion or something, but the TAM of the US dollar is like 20 times that" 32:45. Stablecoins, in this reading, are not a crypto product; they are a digitisation of the dollar, with euros and sterling following. Attaching fiat features — instant local payments, virtual USD and EUR accounts — "mean that it's much more useful than just for crypto people," and the result is that "more than half of our users are not native crypto people" 33:19.

The company numbers he offers on air: on track for $100 million in annualised revenue within the year, with a board meeting days away where he expects to confirm it; more than a million customers who have used the product; a couple of hundred employees plus roughly another hundred contractors after a 4x headcount increase over about six months; and a forecast that headcount stays roughly flat — maybe +25% — while revenue grows another 3–4x 34:21.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed against the company's own Series A release (9 Mar 2026), which reported over 1 million users, nearly $5 billion in annualised transaction volume, an expected 2026 revenue run-rate of $100 million, 15–20% monthly growth, revenue doubling since end-September 2025, and over 250 employees across 190+ countries. Pathy's on-air figures are consistent with the March release rather than inflated beyond it. Source: KAST press release via PR Newswire.

Where he is measurably wrong is the benchmark he uses to size the opportunity.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Materially overstated. Pathy says "Nubank's 20 billion roughly in revenue. Revolute's getting close to $10 billion in revenue" 55:31. Nu Holdings reported FY2025 revenue of $16.3 billion (up 45%) with $2.9 billion net income; Revolut reported FY2025 group revenue of $6.0 billion (up 46%) with $2.3 billion profit before tax. Nubank is ~20% below his figure; Revolut is ~40% below it. The direction of his argument — that the previous neobank generation dwarfs anything in crypto — survives intact, but the numbers do not. Sources: Nu Holdings FY2025 results (25 Feb 2026); Revolut Group Holdings Annual Report 2025.

The JP Morgan comparison he attaches to those numbers is the more defensible half: both neobanks hold a fraction of JP Morgan's customer assets, which is why he treats crypto-versus-crypto comparisons as a category error 55:42.

🌐 Consensus CheckThe "stablecoins are dollar infrastructure, not crypto" reframing is now close to consensus among payments operators in 2026, and it is the reason a card business could grow while token prices halved. What is still contested is whether the resulting users are durable customers or yield tourists who leave when incentives stop. 33:06

Two new products carry the next leg. KAST for Business — with roughly 3,000 companies on the waitlist and newly in beta 35:30 — and a lighter consumer app for African, Latin American and parts of Asian markets, which Pathy calls a "social money app" and which he expects to scale users much faster than the premium KAST product 35:35.

💸 The Deliberate Refusal To Be Cheapest, And The Cost Paranoia Behind It

Kevin makes the criticism directly: KAST does not have the cheapest fees, and "our generation, we're very fee sensitive" 19:27.

Pathy does not concede the point; he attacks its premise. "My job as a founder and CEO is to build a sustainable business. There's no point in going and building a business that has the cheapest fees and then it's gone tomorrow cuz you haven't ultimately solved anything if it's gone tomorrow" 19:42. He describes the structural reason new fintechs cannot be cheap: "when you start in fintech you have a really bad cost base. Basically everyone charges you through the roof" 20:06, and only scale and renegotiated contracts fix it.

He gives one concrete receipt: KAST recently took FX to zero on all local spending across Latin America, funded by renegotiated provider contracts — and adds that the company is "still, by the way, eating some cost to do that" 20:20.

The customer-selection argument underneath is the interesting part, and it is not a comfortable one to say out loud: "there's a certain type of customer frankly who's not loyal, who will take you for your lowest fees, and then if someone else comes across with an unsustainable business with lower fees, they'll jump to them" 21:28.

He extends that into a claim about finance in general that almost never gets said publicly: "the reality of finance is that the majority of customers lose you money... at least 80% you will take a loss on" 22:15. The profitable cohort is 5–10% of users. The reason banks and fintechs do not simply build a product for that cohort is a lifecycle argument — "the person that is in that top five or 10% was once in the bottom five or 10%" 22:45 — which is why KAST wants users from age 18–20 and intends to grow with them "for a lifetime hopefully" 23:46.

💡 Key InsightThis reframes the fee debate entirely. If 80% of customers are loss-making and the business is built on capturing them before they become profitable, then competing purely on price selects for the cohort that never converts — and does so while removing the margin needed to build the products that create conversion. 22:15

Against that, he runs an aggressive internal cost discipline that he traces to a failed company more than a decade ago 37:30. His most transferable example: a review found KAST paying for 80 separate pieces of software. "I just told them, cut 20. I didn't care which 20. Cut 20 and negotiate and bring these down" 40:15. The rationale is that individually trivial line items — one, two, three thousand dollars — aggregate into something that matters, and over six months the waste equalled the company's entire early funding 39:42.

🔗 ConnectionCost paranoia → founder psychology. Pathy explicitly ties the discipline to having launched KAST with about $400–500K and having run break-even on roughly $10 million for most of two years 59:14. The lean period is what makes the $80 million feel like something to protect rather than spend. 37:41

On the $80 million itself, the deployment is unromantic: acquiring companies, particularly for infrastructure and for licences, plus licensing work directly — "if you just do these three things, by the way, you can blow through a lot of money" 59:34. Phase 3: Specific Item Breakdown

🟣 KAST (core consumer product) — A Fintech That Happens To Settle On Stablecoins

A premium global neobank whose card is an acquisition channel, whose margin is meant to come from everything else, and whose stated ambition is to reach the scale of Revolut or Nubank rather than to beat other crypto apps.

What It Includes: Stablecoin deposits with card spending, virtual USD and EUR accounts, instant local payments into around 50 countries and growing, an Earn product routed through Privy wallets, concierge support, a wealth product with some trading planned but explicitly not a trading-first app, and a premium physical card line treated as a brand asset.

The Evidence: Over 1 million users and nearly $5 billion annualised transaction volume as of the March 2026 Series A; $100 million expected 2026 revenue run-rate; roughly 3x growth over the eight or nine months in which Bitcoin halved; more than half of users described as non-crypto-native. Zero-FX local spending across Latin America was funded from renegotiated provider contracts, with KAST absorbing some cost.

The Stated Risk: Pathy names regulatory failure, security failure and loss of trust as the categories that can sink the business — "one weekend they're gone," in the Credit Suisse comparison. Most of the platform remains custodial, and fees are deliberately not the lowest in the category, which leaves KAST exposed if the promised non-card products do not arrive on schedule.

🔵 KAST For Business — The Global Ramp/Brex Competitor Nobody Is Pricing

The product Pathy expects to convert KAST's hardest-won infrastructure into the largest non-card revenue line, targeting the corporate spend and payout market rather than consumers.

What It Includes: A business account product positioned against Ramp and Brex but global in scope, built on the same payout rails, plus a partnership with Deel for employer-of-record and payroll functionality. At recording it had roughly 3,000 companies on the waitlist and had just entered beta.

The Evidence: Pathy expects money movement infrastructure alone to represent 20–30% of revenue, and more than 50% of next year's total revenue to come from non-card products. The infrastructure behind it is a team of roughly 100 people spanning engineering, compliance and banking partnerships, and KAST has been acquiring companies — particularly licensed entities — with Series A capital to own more of it.

The Stated Risk: He explicitly refuses to white-label the rails, which means the entire infrastructure investment has to be monetised through KAST's own products or not at all. That concentrates the return on a single go-to-market, in a category where Ramp and Brex are entrenched and far better capitalised.

🟢 Kasto / "Castro" — The Emerging-Markets App Built To Out-Scale The Premium Product

A lighter "social money app" aimed at African, Latin American and parts of Asian markets, explicitly not premium, and expected to add users far faster than flagship KAST.

What It Includes: A stripped-down consumer money app for markets where the premium KAST proposition does not fit. Pathy describes it as "much more for the common person" in LATAM, Africa and certain parts of Asia, and expects it to scale to users "probably a lot faster than cast."

The Evidence: It is one of the two named products carrying the next growth leg alongside KAST for Business. It was pre-launch at recording, so there are no metrics — only the strategic logic that user acquisition in emerging markets requires a different price point and a different product shape.

The Stated Risk: Lower-ARPU users in markets with harder payment infrastructure is exactly the combination that has historically produced volume without margin. It is also the segment where local incumbents and mobile-money networks are strongest.

Note on naming: the auto-transcript renders it as "Castro." Given that it consistently renders KAST as "cast," the product is most likely spelled "Kasto." No announcement confirming the name or launch date was located.

🟠 KAST Points → Tokenized Equity — The Cancelled Token, And The Instrument Replacing It

Community points earned through card spending will convert into a tokenized equity instrument at the prevailing share price rather than into a liquid token, with details targeted for Q4 and liquidity limited to periodic private buybacks.

What It Includes: Points accrued as cashback of roughly 3–12% depending on tier, originally framed as converting into a future token. The replacement: an equity-linked instrument, converted roughly one-for-one on notional value at the then-current valuation, unlisted by design. Supplementary redemption paths floated on air include card tier upgrades and converting some points into spending credit.

The Evidence: Users were emailed on 2 July that points would convert to tokenized equity, with clarity targeted for Q4 2026. Pathy's justification is that comparable tokens are down 90–95%, that supporting a token requires buybacks which starve the operating business, and that existing shareholders accepted dilution to create the community allocation. His comparison case is Revolut's early users, who hold equity that compounded with the business.

The Stated Risk: Pathy concedes the decision "will be misunderstood" for years and that "some will dispute" the conversion mechanics. The unresolved items are the ones that determine whether this is generous or nominal: the conversion ratio, what rights the instrument carries, and whether buybacks every 6–12 months actually materialise. None existed at recording.

🟡 Earn / Privy Non-Custodial Wallets — The One Part Of KAST That Is Already Not Custodial

KAST's yield product already routes through Privy wallets, making that slice non-custodial today — but non-custodial in Pathy's own taxonomy still means you cannot export the keys.

What It Includes: Funds deposited into Earn sit in wallets provisioned through Privy rather than on KAST's balance sheet. Pathy commits to extending a non-custodial option, and then a self-custodial one, across the product.

The Evidence: Pathy states directly that when you put money into Earn "it's actually non-custodial," and identifies Privy as the wallet provider. He then draws the distinction that matters: non-custodial means the balance is visible on-chain, typically locked in a smart contract, with key export requiring a process and possibly a third party; self-custodial means the user holds exportable keys.

The Stated Risk: By his own explanation, non-custodial funds may not be "that easy to retrieve" if the company fails. The settlement-gap problem means genuinely self-custodial balances will likely carry spending limits, plus manual handling of chains, gas, swaps and bridging — real UX costs he estimates only about 10% of users will accept.

🔴 The Competitive Set — RedotPay, Ether.fi, Jupiter, Plasma, Tria — Named, Then Written Off

Pathy names the field, declines to call them competitors, and simultaneously predicts most of the category will be gone within a year on unit economics.

What It Includes: Asked who else is building this, he names RedotPay ("Red Pay" in the transcript), Ether.fi, Jupiter, Plasma and Tria, and adds that there are "infinite" others. Kevin notes that When Shift Happens itself has multiple partners launching cards or payments — a useful independent signal on how crowded the category has become.

The Evidence: His differentiation argument is product surface area rather than headcount: competitors are building "a card product with maybe some staking and euro and USD accounts," where KAST is building the full money-movement stack, a business product, an emerging-markets app, plus credit, trading and savings. He also notes that headcount no longer maps to output because of AI, pre-empting the comparison that Jupiter does more with fewer people.

The Stated Risk: The prediction is unhedged — most shut down inside 12 months because they cannot reach profitability and investors stop funding the gap. Against that, he insists the pie is large enough that treating them as rivals would make him "an idiot," a tension he does not resolve. Ether.fi is also the counterparty in the public fight that surfaced KAST's terms-of-service problem, which he does not mention when listing the field.

⚫ The Bitcoin Bottom Call — "Here Or Hereabouts," With $100K Pushed To Mid-2027

Made with BTC near $60K, Pathy called the low as roughly in place with downside toward $50K, declined the January 2027 target the prediction markets were pricing, and set mid-2027 for a return to $100K.

What It Includes: A cycle call with three components: the low is here or near; the four-year cycle is intact; and $100K returns by mid-2027 rather than January. He supports it with the observation that the market absorbed war, AI capital diversion and Saylor's position adjustments without breaking, and that apathy is the usual precondition for a rebound.

The Evidence: The peak reference — October 2025 — is exact; the all-time high was $126,296 on 6 October 2025. His drawdown figures for altcoins (~80%) and Solana (~65%) were accurate. Since recording, BTC has risen to roughly $77,568, about 20% above the level at which he called the low, and the $100K target sits nine months out from today.

The Stated Risk: He volunteers that he is "terrible at exact market timing," and explicitly allows for a move toward $50K on a bad shock. The load-bearing assumption — that the four-year cycle still governs — is the single most contested proposition in the 2026 market, and if it is wrong, the shape of the recovery is wrong too.
