⚡ The Bottom Line

  • "9 Figure Investor" is the host's framing, not the guest's claim. Asked directly, Flood answers "no comment on personal wealth… I'll always abstain. I think it's bad luck to put numbers out there" 48:15. Treat every wealth figure attached to this video as unverified.
  • The three-bucket capital stack is the whole episode and takes ninety seconds. Fund 6–12 months of living costs in T-bills and never touch it; run a long-only bucket as the control group that tells you whether you actually have alpha; concentrate only in the third. Everything else is commentary.
  • His theory of ruin is about the burn, not the trade. "Now your assets are also marked down, and your burn is constant, and it feels that much more painful" 26:16. The killer is committed fixed costs — mortgage, school fees, capital calls — that cannot be dialled down when you need them dialled down.
  • He says more money past ultra-high-net-worth changes essentially nothing, and gives one exception. $300M vs $50M: "It's not. It's not. It's not" — the only named difference is owning a plane instead of chartering one.
  • His only crypto position is Hyperliquid, and he refuses to give a price target for it. That refusal has aged well: HYPE traded roughly $41 at recording and $87.45 on 4 September 2026, up ~110%.

🔑 Key Claims (Ranked)

1. Segregate capital into three buckets before doing anything else.
↳ Evidence: burn in T-bills ("not even in equities"), a beta bucket to measure alpha honestly, an active bucket for concentration. Plus: never hold all assets on one exchange.
↳ Caveat: his own burn bucket is "T-bills and STRC" — a Strategy perpetual preferred yielding ~11.5–13.6%, i.e. credit risk on a Bitcoin balance sheet, not a Treasury substitute. Copy the doctrine he stated, not the one he runs.
↳ Consensus: Aligned — it is retail liability-driven investing, matching a known near-term liability with a duration-matched asset.

2. People blow up in the spending column, not the trading column.
↳ Evidence: "they weren't intentional, aware, or pragmatic about how they're spending money and how they're spending their time"; the flip from +3 to −3 with 2 of fixed burn while assets simultaneously mark down.
↳ Consensus: Aligned with the standard ruin-risk framing; the extension from institutional balance sheets to a 25-year-old's rent is his own and is under-argued elsewhere.

3. Money is an amplifier of existing disposition, not a personality change.
↳ Evidence: "If you were an [expletive] before money, you can be even more of one and people will put up with it because you pay them" 50:28 — because wealth removes the friction other people's tolerance used to impose.
↳ Consensus: Contrarian-adjacent. Piff et al. (2012, PNAS) found a uniform shift toward unethical behaviour with social class; replication is mixed. Flood's two-directional amplifier framing is the better-supported version.

4. Value an exchange on net new deposits, not trailing revenue.
↳ Evidence: existing trader capital is being consumed, so volume is downstream of new distribution. Observables: web traffic, product-adoption velocity, whether depositors immediately withdraw, and cross-venue deposit-after-loss behaviour — all free via the public API.
↳ Caveat: his premise number is wrong. He says 90–95% of retail traders lose; measured on-chain data on 10,000 Hyperliquid addresses gives 73.8% losing, 16.5% profitable, 9.7% flat. The model survives at 75%; the urgency doesn't.

5. Hedonic decay on material purchases is faster than anyone budgets for.
↳ Evidence: "It only made me happy for like 3 and 1/2 months, and now it's actually a pain in the ass" 22:42 — followed by the tyres, the wrap, the broken console, and "these are all problems that I have."
↳ Consensus: Aligned. Brickman/Coates/Janoff-Bulman (1978) found lottery winners no happier than controls within 18 months; modern revisions (Diener/Lucas/Scollon 2006) show adaptation to material purchases is specifically faster than to experiences — which is the distinction he draws by hand.

6. Compensation outside crypto is far larger than most people assume — but his numbers are wrong.
↳ Evidence: he claims mid-level Jane Street pays $5–10M and hedge fund PMs $5–20M.
↳ Caveat: reported 2026 data puts Jane Street median total comp near $257k, quant traders $190k–$1.5M; PMs at funds ≳$250M AUM around $500k–$3M. Off by roughly an order of magnitude at the level named. His broader point — that $3M/year incomes are common — stands.

7. Stay under-legible on purpose.
↳ Evidence: give a banal description of what you do and read what people project onto it; stay undoxxed so family can't be harassed; do philanthropy anonymously. One operating principle applied to three domains.
↳ Caveat: he demands public accountability from crypto commentators while operating an account nobody can grade against a name.

🧱 The Three-Bucket Capital Stack: The Only Actual Protocol in the Interview, Delivered as a Throwaway

The bucket structure exists so that no single event can be terminal; the person who got liquidated on a resting bid had no bucket structure, so one event was. His contempt for that trader is the doctrine's enforcement mechanism, and it is also the least attractive thirty seconds in the interview — it treats a structural failure as a character failure, which is exactly the move he warns against elsewhere when he says people "over weight [randomness] at times where they blame it for all their failures" 31:03.

📜 Analog: The stack is a retail restatement of the endowment / liability-matching model — match a known near-term liability (a year of living costs) with a duration-matched, low-volatility asset, and only then let the residual take risk. Institutions call it liability-driven investing; Flood calls it "you know you're good for a year" 16:21. He arrives at the correct structure without the vocabulary, which is a recurring pattern in this interview.

💥 "Brutal Convexity": Why People Blow Up in the Spending Column, Not the Trading Column

"They could feel the capital slipping away because it was being sucked into all of these different buckets." 25:29

rasmr asks the sharp question — is blowing up a failure of diligence or a single bad trade? 24:58 — and Flood rejects the framing of both.

The trade-level blow-up he dismisses as uninteresting: "there's one thing where it's like, 'I had a limit bid and I got filled on 10 10 and I blew up.' Like, you're just a [ __ ]" 25:18. The real mechanism is slower and structural:

  • Obligations accrete quietly. "They have capital calls and pre-negotiations, and they have debt or some sort of structure, whether that's a mortgage or family, you know, kids, private schools" 25:36.
  • Treading water is survivable. "'Oh, I'm making three a year and I'm burning two, and I'm a little bit net negative after tax, maybe'" 25:53.
  • The flip is not linear. "It's like, 'I went from making three to losing three, and I have two of burn, and now I'm [ __ ]'" 26:01.
  • And the assets reprice at the same time. "It's not that like, 'Oh, great, like I lost a few million dollars. Like, I can sell my assets at par and what I bought them at.' It's like, 'No, now your assets are also marked down, and your burn is constant, and it feels that much more painful'" 26:16.

He names the shape himself — "this brutal convexity in life" 26:09 — and the naming is the contribution. The point is that the P&L swing and the burn are not independent variables: the same market event that flips your income also marks down the assets you would have sold to cover the burn, while the burn itself is the only term in the equation that doesn't move.

💡 Key Insight

Key Insight — the actionable version of this is a fixed-cost rule, and he states it two minutes earlier without connecting the two. At 23:08: "preserving it also means not letting your lifestyle expenses encroach to such a point that it becomes like detrimental to your ability to invest or trade." Combined with the convexity mechanism, the operative rule is not "spend less" — it is keep the burn variable. Everything he lists at 25:36 as a blow-up vector (mortgage, private school, capital calls, debt structures) is a committed cost that cannot be dialled down in the quarter you need it dialled down. Rent, discretionary travel and staff can be. The distinction is doing all the work and neither speaker draws it explicitly.

🧵 Running Thesis — "you get paid for having liquidity when nobody else does." Stated at 23:56: "you get paid in markets by having liquidity when people don't… You get paid whether through luck, through skill, or through cunning to say, 'I have money, and the entire market is wiping out'" 24:02. Status: confirmed within the episode — it is the same claim as the three-bucket stack (16:07), the same claim as the anti-lifestyle-creep rule (23:08), and the same claim as his own 2013-onward "a lot of resets" survival story (10:51). Note that it is a conditional thesis: it pays only if the drawdown ends. He never states the failure mode, which is the allocator who stays liquid through a decade-long bull market and is right about nothing.

🌐 Consensus Check: The claim that ruin is usually a flow problem rather than a position problem is well aligned with the risk-management literature and with the practitioner canon on ruin risk — the standard framing is that leverage plus a fixed obligation stream converts a survivable drawdown into a forced liquidation, because the obligation does not scale down with the mark. Flood's "brutal convexity" is an informal restatement of exactly that. Where he is unusual is in extending it from institutional balance sheets to a 25-year-old's rent and car payments, which is a genuinely under-argued application.

🎢 The Hedonics Section: A Lamborghini That Worked for Three and a Half Months

"These material things make you happy, but they make you happy for less time than you think." 22:25

This is the emotionally significant core of the interview and it is delivered with almost no theatre. The argument runs:

  1. The purchase impulse is real and he does not mock it. "A lot of people will make a bunch of money and then like they're like, 'Dude, I need to buy a BMW M3'" 22:19. And elsewhere, of people who spend at or above their means: "they derive a lot of happiness from that" 21:38.
  2. The decay is faster than expected, and the decay itself is what hurts. "And then it's this reversion to your baseline happiness that actually leads to unhappiness" 22:32. Not the absence of the thing — the return to baseline while owning the thing.
  3. The specific accounting. "Why isn't this Lamborghini making me happy for a year? It only made me happy for like 3 and 1/2 months, and now it's actually a pain in the ass because I have to go get it wrapped, I have to put new tires on, it needs maintenance, oh the center console like doesn't turn on anymore" 22:39.
  4. And then the tell. "Like, these are all problems that I have" 22:53.

Point four is what makes the passage worth quoting. It is not a homily about materialism from someone who never bought anything. It is a maintenance schedule.

The same idea returns forty minutes later in its most articulate form, about net worth rather than objects: "For a while I really did index myself to the number on the screen. But you realize that like that's actually a very tough psychologically violent way to live because that number on the screen fluctuates all the time. And then it affects the real world" 38:07. And the conclusion: "you're sort of letting again like your stuff control you rather than like you deriving value from these things and inanimate objects that you own" 38:29.

🔍 Source Verified

✅ The mechanism he describes is the hedonic treadmill, and it is one of the better-replicated findings in the field. The framing originates with Brickman & Campbell (1971), "Hedonic Relativism and Planning the Good Society", and the landmark empirical test is Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman (1978), "Lottery Winners and Accident Victims: Is Happiness Relative?", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology — which found Illinois State Lottery winners of $50,000–$1,000,000 (1970s dollars) no happier than non-winners from under one month to eighteen months after the win. ⚠️ Refinement he doesn't mention, and it cuts in his favour: the strict set-point model has been substantially revised since — Diener, Lucas & Scollon (2006, American Psychologist) and Lyubomirsky's adaptation work show baselines do shift for some life events and that adaptation to material purchases specifically is faster and more complete than adaptation to experiences or relationships. That is precisely the distinction Flood draws by hand at 33:38, where the thing he says actually moves his baseline is "being able to help people around me and use my capital as an accelerant." His folk model is closer to the current literature than the 1978 version is.

🎯 Against Consensus — he refuses the "money doesn't buy happiness" script and the "more is better" script in the same passage. Asked flatly whether $300M beats $50M, he answers "It's not. It's not. It's not" 37:29 — but he does not moralise about it. He concedes the one real difference ("probably 50 versus 300 would say I own a plane instead of like charter" 37:43), grants that some people genuinely derive value from ownership ("that's cool, man. Like I'm happy that you do" 38:03), and locates his own position as a preference rather than a virtue: "I just don't personally really care about those things that much. For a while I really did" 38:07. That last clause is the honest one. He is describing a preference he acquired, which is a much weaker and much more credible claim than the one the genre usually makes.

⚡ Tension — the frugality is real, and the party contradicts the framing of it. "I may be just too frugal, like almost… my friends make fun of me to some extent" 21:40, and "I think people would be very surprised given like the AUM of the family office versus like my burn or lifestyle spend" 11:39. rasmr does not let it pass: "how humble can it be if you have the ability to have a DJ play in your—" 12:15 and then the real point, "you spend more money than me. So what you're revealing is your AUM is really a lot, it seems" 12:20. Flood's defence is specific and partly holds — the DJ "didn't charge us anything for that… he's just legitimately friends of ours" 12:05 — but rasmr's inference is the sound one: absolute frugality and relative frugality are different claims, and only the second one is being made.

💡 Key Insight — the anti-luxury argument he actually makes is an operations argument, not a moral one. Read 22:39 again. Every item on the list is a task: wrapping, tyres, maintenance, a broken console. He is not saying the Lamborghini failed to deliver status or joy. He is saying it generated work, and the work outlasted the joy by an order of magnitude. That reframes conspicuous consumption as a liability that pays a declining dividend and charges a constant coupon — which is, note, the identical shape as the burn convexity at 26:09. The same man is running the same model on his car and on his balance sheet.

🚀 Escape Velocity, Two Tiers: "You Could Do Anything You Want in Normieville"

"There's this concept of escape velocity that someone told me one time." 35:29

Asked which dollar figure actually changes a life, Flood refuses the premise — "it's not a money thing. It's actually being a position of steady state" 33:30 — and then supplies a two-tier model anyway, borrowed and credited.

Tier one — "escape velocity" in the ordinary sense. He describes it as a game-character inventory: "it's like a GTA character. Like for the really autistic people they'll get what I'm talking about. Like you have all your equipment" 35:34. Concretely: "'Okay, I got a Rolex and I got a nice car and I'm renting like a 10k a month apartment and I'm still pretty cash flow positive and I can show out… I could basically do anything I want in Normieville'" 35:41. The defining property is not consumption; it is the removal of constraint — "you're no longer bounded by like the reality that most people are bounded by… where they have to go to work on Monday. They have bills. They're like behind on their debt. They have student loans" 36:18. rasmr places himself and his friends exactly here 36:08.

Tier two — the real thing. "But like true escape velocity of like I can do anything and I can rent anything and I can fly private if I want to. Like I truly have a complete abundance of just any path in the game tree that even the most exceptionally wealthy people in the world live" 36:33. His price for that: "that takes like tens of millions of dollars of post-tax capital and a pretty healthy income" 36:54, and then a datum — "The cutoff used to be ultra high net worth was like 30 million plus of post-tax assets. I would say it's probably a bit higher now" 37:03.

🌐 Consensus Check

The $30M figure is the correct industry threshold, not an invented one — US$30 million in net assets is the standard definition of an ultra-high-net-worth individual used across wealth research (Knight Frank's Wealth Report, Wealth-X, Capgemini's "ultra-HNWI" band). Flood's addition — "probably a bit higher now" 37:09 — is the reasonable adjustment for inflation since the threshold was set, and it is a fair point that the definition has never been indexed. Where he departs from the standard is the modifier "post-tax": the industry number is investable net assets, which is already a post-tax concept, so his version is not more conservative than the standard, just differently phrased.

💡 Key Insight — the two tiers have completely different currencies, and that is the whole argument. Tier one is denominated in freedom from obligation — no Monday, no debt, no student loans 36:18. Tier two is denominated in freedom of access — rent anything, fly private, any path in the game tree 36:33. The gap between them costs tens of millions of dollars and, on his own testimony forty seconds later, buys almost nothing that matters: "It's not. It's not. It's not" 37:29. Taken together, the passage says the expensive tier is a status good with no consumption surplus — a genuinely strong claim, delivered by someone with standing to make it and no obvious incentive to.

⚡ Tension: At 33:30 the life-changing variable is "being a position of steady state" — a psychological state. At 36:54 the same interview attaches a dollar figure — "tens of millions of dollars of post-tax capital" — to the version he calls "true" escape velocity. Both cannot be the operative threshold. The reconciliation he does not make explicit is that steady state is the threshold that matters and tier-two escape velocity is the one people chase; he answers the question he was asked with the first and then spends ninety seconds describing the second.

🪞 "Money Accentuates Who You Were Always Supposed to Be" — and the Advice He Gives on Repeat

"If you were an [ __ ] before money, you can be even more of an [ __ ] and people will put up with it because you pay them." 50:28

This is the load-bearing sentence of the whole interview and it arrives late. The full claim: "money just really accentuates who you were always supposed to be… If you're generous, you can be even more generous. If you're greedy and you like [ __ ] people over and you're just kind of like a shiesty person, like you can be even more of it because like people are forced to transact with you to some extent" 50:24–50:48.

The mechanism he names is not psychological but structural: money removes the constraint that other people's tolerance used to impose. It does not change your disposition; it removes the friction that was suppressing it.

From that he derives the advice he says he gives repeatedly, and it is the second most actionable thing in the file after the buckets:

"This is what I always tell these crypto guys who make their money really fast is like dude, set it aside, pretend like you didn't even make it, stick some of it in Bitcoin, some of it equities, keep some of it in cash and just sit and just think about how it makes you feel and think about the type of person that you want to be." 50:50

And the standing he claims for it: "I have calls with people who make a ton of capital all the time. And I help them work through like what it's like cuz it happened to me very quickly and then I lost a lot of it. So I have both perspectives. And then I obviously made it back" 51:09.

The failure mode he warns about is the one people don't expect: "You can use capital to alleviate, you know, poor parts of yourself. You can use it to try and fix relationships. And it'll fix it at least temporarily, but if anything it'll compound the bad habits and the bad decisions that you're making" 51:41.

🌐 Consensus Check

"Money accentuates who you were always supposed to be" is a folk statement of a claim with real empirical support, though the literature is more specific and more contested than the aphorism. Piff et al. (2012), PNAS — "Higher social class predicts increased unethical behavior" — found across seven studies that upper-class individuals were more likely to break traffic laws, take valued goods, lie in negotiation and cheat to win a prize, with the effect partly mediated by more favourable attitudes toward greed. The direction of Flood's version is subtly different and arguably better supported: he claims wealth is an amplifier of existing disposition in both directions ("if you're generous, you can be even more generous" 50:35), whereas the headline research claims a uniform shift toward self-interest. Replication work on the Piff findings has been mixed, which makes the amplifier framing the safer of the two.

📜 Analog: "People are forced to transact with you" 50:47 is the principal-agent / soft-power problem stated in one clause — the same dynamic that produces yes-men around founders, unchecked chief executives, and the "nobody tells the emperor" failure in every organisational-behaviour textbook. Flood identifies it as a personal risk rather than a governance risk, which is the more uncomfortable framing, because there is no board to fix it.

💡 Key Insight — "pretend like you didn't even make it" is a deliberate lifestyle-ratchet block, and it pairs exactly with the burn convexity. At 26:16 the killer is a constant burn against a collapsing mark. At 50:50 the prescription is to not let the burn ratchet up in the first place, during the window when a windfall feels permanent. Those are the same rule stated ex-post and ex-ante. Everything in this interview about spending — the Lamborghini 22:39, the M3 22:19, the "pretty autistic about your spend" line at 24:30, the AI expense audit at 23:20 — is enforcement of a single principle: the burn is the only variable you control in a drawdown, so never let it become fixed.

⚡ Tension

Tension — he asks for a culture of accountability while operating an account nobody can hold accountable. But the man throwing it is pseudonymous by design 41:31, publishes no P&L, and declines to confirm any wealth figure 48:15. His implicit counter partly works — his positions and calls are public and time-stamped on X, which is all his argument strictly requires — but the asymmetry is real: he can grade other people's records against a face and a reputation; they can only grade his against a handle.

💡 Key Insight — the strongest version of his argument is not about ego, it is about a missing scoreboard. "The only way you really tell, you know, how someone looked is, you know, it is is time like time tells. Like you might be an idiot for selling a stock, you might be a genius because it craters 50%" 72:36. Financial media, sell-side research and regulated advice all carry some mechanism for retrospective grading — track records, performance disclosure, mandated disclaimers. Crypto commentary has none. Flood's demand for public "updates" is, functionally, an attempt to manufacture the missing scoreboard through social pressure. That it doubles as entertainment for his followers is the honest objection, and he concedes it at 72:02.

🎯 Against Consensus: "For me to make money, I'm taking it from someone else" 71:59 is a flat rejection of the positive-sum framing that dominates industry messaging — the "big like Kumbaya like we're all friends, we're all amazing, we're all in it together" he mocks at 71:42. He is correct about the specific venue he trades: perpetual futures are zero-sum before fees and negative-sum after them, since every long is matched by a short and the exchange takes a cut. Whether crypto as a whole is zero-sum is a different and much weaker claim, and he does not make it — he confines the statement to trading, which is the defensible version.

📊 The Hyperliquid Thesis: "Net New Deposits," Because the Average Retail Trader Loses

"Ex- like in exchange is always valued not just on the current revenues and not just the current volumes and not just growth but also on net new deposits." 75:37

The last ten minutes turn into an unplanned masterclass, prompted by rasmr asking for a price target on his own biggest position. Flood refuses the price target and gives the framework instead — and it is the most professionally useful passage in the file after the buckets.

The core claim. Volume and revenue are outputs of a stock of capital that is continuously depleting, because "the average retail trader loses" 75:51. Therefore: "where can you actually grow? How can you actually sustain exchange volumes? You need net new deposits. You need distribution to be increasing" 76:03.

The observable proxies he names, in order:

  • "You can look at website traffic" 76:13
  • "You can look at the velocity of different product offerings taking off. For example, like HIP 3" 76:15
  • "How quickly HIP 4 adoption… comes around" 76:27
  • "Do people who deposit there immediately withdraw?" 76:51

And the method, which is the giveaway. "This is all on chain. So you can get a ton of really interesting data if you're willing to [Claude Code] it — of like, hey, like what is the trading behavior pattern of Hyperliquid traders versus [Lighter] versus [Extended]… and be able to say, wow, Hyperliquid traders deposit on average more money and more times even when they lose versus [Lighter]. This is a pretty big [ __ ] spread" 76:53–77:19. He closes with the part most people skip: "what a blessing it is that all of the information and all of the data about Hyperliquid is accessible to anybody for free, right? Via API" 77:36.

On his own positioning, three admissions in quick succession: "we're not as detailed as we used to be. We're kind of along the ride for Hyperliquid" 77:21; "for now it's just Hyperliquid because I don't really think owning anything else makes that much sense. But eventually I'll slide it back to Bitcoin and have Bitcoin be a higher weighting" 78:31; and on price targets, "I'm never a price target guy where like if it goes to 70, I'll sell… what if revenue 10x's and it's at 70 bucks? Are you going to sell your whole position? No, if anything it's cheaper than it was" 78:48–79:12.

🔍 Source Verified

⚠️ The direction is right; the number is inflated by roughly twenty percentage points. Flood states "90, 95% of retail traders are net losing net negative" 75:58. Measured on-chain data from Hyperliquid itself is worse than the industry's PR and better than his number: an analysis of 10,000 Hyperliquid traders found 16.5% profitable, 73.8% losing, 9.7% breaking even, with 27.2% losing more than 85% of capital within 30 days; a separate read put roughly three-quarters of addresses net-negative. The same analysis reports profitability scaling with account size — the losing cohort is concentrated in small accounts. Why the correction doesn't break his model: at ~75% net-negative the depletion argument still holds and net new deposits are still the binding growth constraint. What changes is the rate, and therefore how much new distribution is needed just to hold volumes flat. He presents 90–95% as settled; it is not, and the true figure is checkable from the public API he himself points at thirty seconds earlier.

💡 Key Insight — he has described a business whose growth requires a permanently replenished losing cohort, and he says so without flinching. Strip the framing: the KPI he selects for valuing an exchange is the arrival rate of new capital that will predominantly be lost. That is an accurate description of the economics and an uncomfortable one, and it is stated by a man whose largest position is the equity-equivalent of that business. He does not moralise about it — consistent with 71:59, where he treats trading as explicitly extractive. This is one of the very few places in crypto commentary where the bull case and the social cost are stated by the same person in the same paragraph.

🎯 Against Consensus — "remove yourself from the price targets" is the opposite of what the medium rewards. "This is what the best traders, the best capital allocators are able to do is they're able to remove themselves from the price targets and say like, what is actually happening with the underlying business and where do I think it's going to go in the future based on like the data" 79:17. rasmr has asked twice for a number 75:08 77:46 and gets none. The refusal is the advice. Note the one concession he does make, which is not nothing: "I think you should have places where you take profit as a whole because it's good to pull money out of a trade" 78:52 — profit-taking as a portfolio operation, not as a price event.

🧵 Running Thesis — the concentration is real and he never softens it. "For now it's just Hyperliquid" 78:31, against his own advice at 16:07 to run three buckets and at 16:51 never to hold everything in one place. Status: consistent, once you read the buckets correctly. Bucket one (T-bills + STRC) is already funded 21:10; the Hyperliquid concentration lives in buckets two and three, where it is supposed to live. The doctrine never says "diversify" — it says "make sure the concentrated bucket cannot kill you." That is a meaningfully different and far more defensible position than the concentration alone implies, and it is the single most important thing for a reader to take from this file before copying his book.

⚡ Tension — one attribution is genuinely unresolved. At 75:17 rasmr says his HYPE position is "like 80% of my portfolio or something crazy." The very next line is "That's a lot. It's about 60% of my portfolio. So it's it's a lot. I'm going Jez mode, bro" 75:18–75:27. The caption gives no turn marker. 🧩 Phase 3: The Frameworks, One Card Each

🧱 The Three-Bucket Capital Stack — The Only Thing in This Interview a Reader Should Copy Verbatim

Fund your survival first in Treasuries, prove your edge second in beta, and only then let the third bucket try to kill itself.

What It Includes

Three segregated pools with different mandates and different venues.

The Practice (Step-by-Step)

  1. Bucket 1 — Burn. Six to twelve months of all living expenses. "Put that in T-bills. Put that not even in equities. Like maybe some blend of equities and T-bills" 16:13. Untouchable: "that's your bucket of capital that you don't touch and you know you're good for a year" 16:21.
  2. Bucket 2 — Beta and measurement. "Your long only or very, you know, safe-ish long short bucket where you can really identify if you have alpha or not" 16:25. The point is diagnostic: this bucket tells you whether you have an edge at all, because it is the control group.
  3. Bucket 3 — The book. "And then you have your actively traded bucket" 16:39. This is where concentration is permitted, which is why his own all-Hyperliquid posture 78:31 is not a contradiction.
  4. Split custody across venues. "You should never have all your assets on one single exchange because of the risk of user error and fat finger" 16:51.

The Evidence

He has run it through "80, 90% drawdowns" 15:03 and states his own bucket one is funded 21:10. The Caveat This File Adds: His bucket one is "in T-bills and in STRC" 21:17. STRC is not a Treasury substitute. If you copy the doctrine, copy the version he stated, not the version he runs.
