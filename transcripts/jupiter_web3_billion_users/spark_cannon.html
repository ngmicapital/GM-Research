Jupiter's Meow Takes Apart His Own Token Story: "I Confused Telling Stories With Communicating A Narrative"

Source When Shift Happens · Date n/a · 81 min
Domain Crypto & Markets
Duration 81 min
Date
Speakers
  • Meow (@weremeow) — Co-founder, Jupiter (Solana's dominant DEX aggregator and DeFi superapp); previously a co-founder of Wrapped Bitcoin and an adviser to Kyber Network, Instadapp/Fluid and Blockfolio
  • Kevin Follonier — Host, When Shift Happens
Full Mode

Executive Summary

  • This is a post-mortem disguised as a strategy interview, and the post-mortem is the valuable half. Meow returns from a two-month social media blackout and spends the last third of the episode itemising three failures behind JUP's collapse — an incoherent narrative, a DAO design that set contributors against token holders, and a 50%-of-revenue buyback that he discovered most people did not know existed. He assigns all three to himself by name. 63:21–78:53
  • His central lesson generalises beyond crypto: a correct mechanism that is not continuously explained is worth approximately zero. "We make this really big thing... give 50% to the token... it actually doesn't matter," he says, concluding that what anchors a token is "fundamental optimism" rather than cash flow mechanics. That is a founder publicly abandoning the cash-flow-accrual thesis that most of DeFi adopted in 2025. 72:49–73:17
  • "Web4" is the repackaging that the next Jupiter chapter hangs on, and it is a participation thesis, not a technology one. Web1 was read, web2 was create, web3 built internet money — and Meow's argument is that almost nobody creates, governs or provides liquidity for that money, they only trade it on centralised exchanges. Web4 is his name for the moment that changes. 29:01–32:07
  • Jupiter is deliberately refusing to be a protocol, and is vertically integrating instead. The internal frame is an "iPhone moment" for DeFi, executed via what Meow calls triple-S: full surface (web, mobile, APIs), full suite (swap, perps, lend, portfolio), full stack (own validator, custom VM work, own data layer, onchain identity). Every one of these is a bet against the compose-small-primitives orthodoxy. 36:00–42:45
  • The omnichain problem, in Meow's telling, is a tribalism problem wearing a technical costume. He points out that widely-held tokens bridged to Solana attract near-nothing in volume despite the bridges working, and concludes the binding constraint is social identity, not liquidity routing. JupNet, ADI passkeys and a modified VM are the answers he is building. 55:49–63:14
  • The decision-making method he describes is intuition, and he says so without hedging. Jupiter allocates capital by "energy" — whether the right people want to do it and the users want it — with periodic structured reflection as the only error-correction. He immediately volunteers that he is unsure whether that is bullish or bearish for JUP. 52:52–55:20
  • The episode is also an advertisement, and the document treats it that way. Jupiter is a multi-year media partner of the show, the partnership is discussed on camera, and every forward-looking claim here is a founder's unaudited account of his own product. The verification blocks below mark where the numbers hold and where they do not. 42:45–44:58

🪞 Crypto's Reputation Problem Is An Incentive Problem, Not A Manners Problem

The interview opens with both men agreeing that crypto's public image is bad — and then Meow supplies a mechanism for why, which is more useful than the usual complaint about tone.

"The more your project doesn't have substance, the more it makes sense for you to just give as much as possible to people to show it."

Follonier's framing is about lost opportunity: he argues the people who most needed crypto's wealth-building window either got wiped out on FTX and Celsius or lost money on memecoins, and the institutions arrived to buy the assets that retail was supposed to front-run 04:12–05:29. His diagnosis is that the industry looks unserious, and that the seriousness deficit is what keeps outsiders away.

Meow's addition is the causal loop 06:45–07:24. A project with real substance has less marginal budget to spend on promotion because it spends on engineering; a project with no substance has nothing but promotion to spend on. The people willing to promote empty projects are, by selection, the least credible people. So the industry's public face is systematically staffed by its worst operators — not by accident, not by culture, but by an arithmetic that favours whoever has the least to lose.

💡 Key InsightThe visible surface of an industry is selected for by promotional budget divided by substance, not by quality. Any sector where marketing spend is uncorrelated with product quality will show the same pathology. 06:45

Meow then turns the lens on the internal culture. He describes Crypto Twitter as "the most reflexive ecosystem I ever seen in my life" — valuable because the feedback loop is brutally fast and direct, dangerous because it pulls operators into short-horizon decisions 08:26–09:53. His admission is specific and, in light of the later token section, load-bearing: "I've been guilty of so many decisions that are driven not by what I think is the right strategy long term, but more like because I felt a lot [of] pressure."

Follonier's complementary point is about gatekeeping-by-jargon. He paraphrases the early-Bitcoin line — if you don't understand it, I don't have time to explain it — and argues the industry still runs on it 07:43–08:20. His conclusion is blunt: you do not bring people into a technology "by acting like a bunch of jerks."

🔗 ConnectionReputation problem → token problem — the same failure repeats at the end of the episode in miniature. Meow's complaint here is that good projects under-communicate while empty ones over-communicate; at 72:01 he reports friends telling him Jupiter "does this much and talks about this much" while most teams do the inverse. The industry-level critique and his personal one are the same error at two scales. (07:24 → 72:01)

Neither speaker claims a fix beyond doing better work in public. What makes the exchange worth archiving is that it sets up the episode's actual thesis: Jupiter's problem was never the product.

🎰 "Crypto Today Is A Giant Casino" — And Meow Argues That Is The Funding Mechanism

Follonier presses the hardest question in the first half, and does not let Meow dodge it: seen honestly, from outside and from inside, crypto is "this 24/7 gigantic casino accessible by anyone from anywhere on the planet" 18:31–19:05.

Meow does not deny it. He reaches first for Animal Spirits — the Akerlof and Shiller book about the emotional drivers of economic decisions — to argue that the impulse toward speculation is a human constant rather than a crypto defect 19:20–19:53. Then he gives the answer that makes the section worth keeping:

"A great mind is one that can hold two truths at once."

The two truths are: crypto today is a casino with limited use outside the casino, and the casino generates the volume, fees and attention that finance the construction of a different financial system 20:33–21:05. He is explicit that Jupiter tries to hold both — building better speculation venues while pointing the proceeds at rails.

🎯 Against ConsensusMost founders answering the casino charge either deny it ("look at stablecoin settlement volume") or apologise for it. Meow's position — concede it fully, then argue the speculation is the subsidy that funds the infrastructure — is rarer and considerably more defensible, because it does not require the listener to pretend the volume is something it is not. 20:33

The practical expression of the "two truths" posture is his description of Jupiter's product philosophy: build what users want now — which is trading — while building toward the future you want, which is usage 21:15–23:53. He lists the friction removal work as evidence: gas abstraction, gas swaps, a slippage estimator that reads token volatility and volume to set a level per trade, a crypto-send flow for recipients without a wallet, and running one of Solana's larger validators specifically to improve transaction landing.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Partially confirmed. Animal Spirits is real and correctly characterised: George Akerlof and Robert Shiller, Princeton University Press, 2009, arguing that emotional drivers shape economic outcomes. Meow's gloss — the human desire to be rich and well known — is a reasonable paraphrase, though the term itself originates with Keynes in the 1930s rather than with the book. Source: Princeton University Press; Wikipedia entry on Animal Spirits (book).
🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Partially confirmed on the validator claim. Meow says Jupiter runs "the third largest validator" on Solana 23:01. Jupiter does operate a validator with a large stake pool behind it — JupSOL, around 4.7m SOL — but JitoSOL alone carries roughly 14.3m SOL and Binance's bnSOL is also larger. Jupiter is a top-tier Solana staking operation; the specific ordinal "third largest" is not supported by public stake-pool tables and appears to be an offhand recollection rather than a current ranking. Source: Solana Compass stake-pool statistics; Sanctum LST rankings.
⚡ TensionAt 52:52 Meow says Jupiter's core allocation metric is "energy" — intuition about whether the right people want to build it. But at 17:32 he describes the original decision to build Jupiter as the product of months of deliberate analysis: "I took a cold hard look at what our team could do... at the fundamental economics of the chain... at the differences between the operating context." Both accounts are probably true — the analysis ran for months, the trigger was intuitive — but the episode presents intuition as the method when the record he gives describes intuition as the tiebreaker on top of analysis.

🪐 "Web4" Is A Participation Thesis Wearing A Version Number

This is the section the partnership with the show exists to promote, and it is worth separating the framing from the substance.

The framing is a version ladder: web1 was the internet, web2 was social media, web3 was internet money, web4 will be "social money" 29:20–29:49. Taken as a taxonomy this is marketing — there is no independent definition of web4, no metric attached to it anywhere in the episode, and the numbering does no analytical work.

The substance underneath it is sharper. Meow's actual claim is about the shape of participation 30:02–31:44. In web1, people read. The phase change to web2 came when the base technology got good enough and there was a clear economic reason for ordinary people to participate in creating content. His reading of crypto now: the base technology for creating money exists and works, but the number of people involved in creating, governing, providing liquidity for or actually using these monies is "very very low, extremely low." Most of web3, he argues, has happened on centralised exchanges — where users hold exposure but take "zero part at all in the creation of it and governing of it" 31:38–32:07.

💡 Key InsightThe testable version of web4 is a participation ratio, not a product category — the share of token holders who do anything other than hold and trade. Meow never states that ratio, and the episode would be considerably stronger if he did. It is also the metric by which the thesis can be falsified. 31:44

From there he builds the organising structure for the whole Jupiter roadmap: the "universe of monies" and its three universal principles 32:14–34:44.

The contrast he draws is with gated capital. NASDAQ, CME, a bank, even the US dollar are, in his description, gated pools — access is granted, not assumed. DeFi's distinguishing property is the absence of that gate: value can move between any two assets without an intermediary deciding who is allowed in. He puts it as a claim about equality of access — "whether you are American, whether you're European, whether you are African, South African, Nigeria or Alaska, who cares, no one cares, you have equal access" 25:44–26:04.

📜 AnalogMeow compares centralised exchanges and banks to AOL, and DeFi to the open web — "a very limited universe of things I can do" versus direct access to the whole network. It is the right analogy for the argument he is making, and it carries the same implicit prediction: the walled garden wins on usability first and loses on surface area eventually. 25:05
🧵 Running ThesisMeow's through-line across the whole episode is that crypto's binding constraint is access friction, not technology — gas, signing, bridging, onboarding, trust in a brand. Every product decision he describes, from slippage estimation to passkey identity, is downstream of that one belief. Status: developing — consistent across the episode, but never tested against a counter-explanation (that the constraint is demand, not friction).

Follonier presses for a timeline: how many years until the world uses the "Google of finance" daily? Meow refuses the question and substitutes three variables for it 27:24–28:51: how fast the team can make access easy, how fast it can build a brand trusted at the level of Google or Apple, and how fast it can make the DeFi world more useful than what a bank offers. "We don't engage in hypothetical questions," he says. "We just go."

📱 The iPhone Strategy: Vertical Integration Because Composability Never Shipped A Consumer Product

Asked why Jupiter is the team to carry this, Meow answers with an analogy rather than a credential, and the analogy is the most structurally interesting idea in the episode 36:05–38:35.

Smartphones existed before the iPhone — BlackBerry, Palm, Nokia, webOS. The technology was there and it was clunky. What the iPhone required was not one breakthrough but simultaneous competence across hardware, operating system, software, applications, support, distribution, retail reach, brand awareness — and, he stresses, the backend services almost nobody credits. "People always see software, hardware and the OS, but they don't really see the giant web of [work] that happens in the background."

His claim is that DeFi needs the same kind of moment, and that it will require the same kind of full-span competence. He is careful to hedge the arrogance — "I'm not that arrogant to say we will be the one" — before claiming Jupiter is "probably the only team that is really focused" on solving it across the whole stack.

The operational form of that is the internal "triple-S" framework 38:44–42:45:

Full surface — web, mobile and APIs, which he names as the three delivery channels for any financial service. Jupiter started mobile roughly a year before the recording; he claims the best mobile app in the category and the most widely integrated APIs in Solana DeFi.

Full suite — the product line: the Ultra swap system, Ultra terminal, screener, lending, portfolio, perps, and more in build. His argument for the portfolio product is the most revealing: as a standalone business it is "not interesting" and has little economic value, but inside a system with other monetisation surfaces it drives usage and distribution to a high-value user base.

Full stack — depth rather than breadth: contract engineering, the data layer, API and UI design, validator operations, in-house validator software, and now work on Jupiter's own VM. He ties it to Alan Kay's maxim about software and hardware.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed on attribution. The line Meow half-attributes to "Alan Kay or Steve Jobs" — if you are serious about software, build the hardware — is Alan Kay's, from a Creative Think seminar in July 1982. Steve Jobs quoted it in the 2007 iPhone keynote, which is almost certainly why the two names are entangled in Meow's memory. Both halves of his hedge are defensible. Source: Alan Kay, Wikiquote; contemporaneous accounts of the 2007 keynote.
🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed on the market-position claims that underpin this section. Jupiter has processed over $2 trillion in lifetime trading volume, commands roughly 95% of Solana's DEX aggregator volume and routes around 80% of the network's onchain perpetuals trading, with $2.6–3bn TVL across products. The "we're not just another protocol" framing is supported by the share numbers, not just by ambition. Source: DefiLlama protocol pages; Solana Compass project review.
⚡ TensionAt 43:06 Meow says humility is the primary trait Jupiter screens for in partners and that arrogance makes conversation impossible, because arrogant people are "so confident in their version of the future that no one knows about." But 38:44–42:00 is an unbroken chain of superlatives about Jupiter — the only team strong across web, mobile and APIs; the most widely integrated APIs; probably the best mobile app; probably the best portfolio product "in DeFi or probably in the world." He is arguing for epistemic humility while exercising almost none about his own product.
🌐 Consensus CheckThe DeFi orthodoxy since 2020 has been composability — build one primitive well, let others assemble. Jupiter's bet is the opposite: own everything from the validator to the brand, because the integration seams are where consumer experience dies. Hyperliquid made structurally the same bet on the perps side and was rewarded for it; the open question is whether it generalises to an aggregator whose original moat was routing across other people's venues. 38:44

🌐 The Omnichain Problem Is Tribal Before It Is Technical

Meow calls this his favourite problem, and his credentials for it are real rather than rhetorical 55:49–63:14.

He lays out the provenance first. He co-founded Wrapped Bitcoin, which he describes as the vehicle for using Bitcoin in decentralised finance. He was fascinated early by Polkadot and Cosmos as explicit attempts at the multi-chain problem. He advised Instadapp, which built the Avocado smart wallet as another angle on the same thing, and which is now Fluid. Through Jupiter he has integrated, in his words, effectively every bridging variant in production.

The philosophical origin is a genuine observation rather than a pitch. While watching Bitcoin maximalists insist only one chain matters, he was simultaneously senior adviser to Blockfolio, whose user base was the precise opposite — altcoin holders who wanted more chains and more coins 57:37–58:10. The dissonance produced the thesis:

"The Bitcoin maxis might insist that there's only one coin that matters, but the reality is that the world really wants more chains and more coins."

He extends it into a prediction about structure, not sentiment: chain proliferation runs in boom-bust waves, most chains die each cycle, and this repeats indefinitely — so the world is permanently multi-chain regardless of what any maximalist argues 58:19–59:12.

Then comes the part that separates this section from every other omnichain pitch. Meow says the hard constraint is not bridging technology. He gives a concrete case: take a token with a large, genuinely enthusiastic holder base, bridge it to Solana, and the volume is negligible. "You would think that there's a lot of people in [Solana] who want to buy SPX or hype. Nah, not really" 60:43–61:09. His conclusion: "it's not just technical, it's not just liquidity, not just financial — it's also a very strong tribalism element."

💡 Key InsightIf token demand is chain-tribal rather than asset-rational, then bridging infrastructure has been solving the wrong half of the problem for five years. The unsolved half is not "can this asset move" but "does anyone on the destination chain want it" — which is a distribution and identity problem, and cannot be fixed by a faster message-passing layer. 61:09

JupNet is the answer he describes: not a bridge but a network that makes navigating many chains tractable for users, projects and liquidity at once 61:10–63:14. The parts he names are ADI, a rethink of blockchain UX away from the Bitcoin-inherited private-key-and-wallet model toward onchain passkeys that let a user log in and sign without managing keys; and JupiterVM, a heavily modified virtual machine built to support the omnichain, identity and sequencing behaviour the design needs.

🔗 ConnectionOmnichain tribalism → the web4 thesis — the two halves lock together. If participation in creating and governing money is the goal, and if tribal identity gates which monies a user will touch, then an identity layer (ADI) is not a UX nicety but the load-bearing component of the whole plan. (61:09 → 31:44)

💔 The Token Post-Mortem: Three Failures Meow Puts On Himself

Follonier asks the question the audience came for, without cushioning it: the token has not performed since TGE, holders are unhappy, what is the plan 61:24–63:35. What follows is roughly fifteen minutes of unusually specific self-criticism, and it is the most substantive part of the episode.

Meow's opening move is to explain why he disappeared. He took two months off social media specifically to rethink how he had built the token narrative — and this podcast, he says explicitly, is where he is being "forced to articulate it in a somewhat comprehensible way" for the first time (63:37–64:04, 77:24–77:34). The reflection is being performed live, which accounts for both the rawness and the repetition.

Failure one: he could tell stories but could not hold a narrative.

"I confused being able to tell stories with being able to communicate a narrative coherently."

His self-diagnosis 67:42–69:11 is that he would talk about twenty things and not decide which one was the main thing that needed to land. The consequence, he says, is that people understood neither Jupiter nor the token — which, for a protocol with a nine-figure fee run rate and a dominant market share, is a communication failure rather than a business one.

Failure two: the DAO work-group design put contributors and token holders on opposite sides.

The structure sounded correct on paper: the DAO votes to fund work groups, which then carry legitimacy to represent and build for Jupiter 69:11–70:04. In practice, he says, there was never sufficient trust built between the work groups — "amazing people, by the way" — and token holders. The votes recurred, each one re-litigating the same grievance, and holders experienced them as dilution even where the sums were small relative to the protocol. His own verdict: "I should have been way more sensitive to that problem," and "that friction was not noticed by me until probably too late" 70:40–71:12. He attributes the blind spot to being caught up in the theoretical correctness of the system while neglecting what it produced in practice.

Failure three: Jupiter did the buyback and then failed to connect it to anything.

This is the one with the widest applicability. Jupiter routes 50% of protocol revenue into buying JUP, locked in the Litterbox. Meow describes it as "a very heavy investment... not earnings by [reference]" — real cash, not accounting 64:47–65:05. And then: "we actually went about asking quite a lot of people — they don't even know that we do that" 65:48–65:56.

His conclusion goes further than a communications complaint, and it is the line worth extracting from the whole episode 72:49–73:17: the buyback "actually doesn't matter, because ultimately what really anchors a token is... the fundamental optimism" — and he says the same holds for Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana and HYPE alike.

🎯 Against ConsensusThrough 2025, DeFi converged on the view that fee-accrual and buybacks would finally give tokens a defensible valuation anchor. Meow — operating one of the largest such programmes in the sector — is saying from inside it that the mechanism does not set the price, belief about the future does. That is a founder arguing against the thesis most favourable to his own token's mechanics, which is why it is worth taking seriously. 72:49

He also identifies the launch framing as a permanent anchor he cannot undo: JUP was presented to the community as a governance token, and "that narrative is still there today" 66:06–66:25. The second anchor was the voting mechanism itself, which repeatedly put allocation decisions in front of holders.

🔗 ConnectionFailure three → the web4 thesis — they are the same problem at different scales. Meow's answer to "why did the buyback fail to bid the token" is the story did not connect the mechanism to a future; his answer to "why will web4 happen" is a story about a future that connects everyone to the mechanism. The second is the corrective for the first, which is why the episode is structured the way it is. (73:17 → 29:20)

👥 "One Community" Was Three Communities All Along

The most transferable structural lesson in the episode arrives near the end, and Meow presents it as the root cause beneath the other three failures 75:33–78:53.

His prior model was, in his own word, naive: token holders, DAO participants and users were all "one community." A year of operating taught him they are not — "holy [expletive], very different. Actually very different."

The three constituencies he now separates:

  • Users — people who trade and transact on Jupiter. Their interest is execution quality, cost and reliability. They may hold no JUP at all.
  • Evangelists and contributors — people leading initiatives, building in the community, representing Jupiter. Their interest is legitimacy, resourcing and scope to act.
  • Token holders — people with balance-sheet exposure to JUP. Their interest is the token's value and anything that dilutes or supports it.

The DAO work-group design failed precisely because it forced constituencies two and three into the same voting room on a recurring basis, with directly opposed incentives on every proposal. Meow's fix — stated as intent rather than as a shipped mechanism — is to build "an extremely synergistic mental model between all three," which starts from accepting that they have different objectives and different desires rather than pretending they are one bloc.

💡 Key InsightA single governance surface for constituencies with opposed incentives does not produce alignment; it produces a recurring, scheduled fight. The design error was not the work groups or the budgets — it was routing a contributor-funding decision through a holder vote at all. Meow names the symptom accurately and stops one step short of naming that as the structural fix. 77:10

He closes the section with a commitment he attaches to himself: "I need to be brave" — meaning, in context, willing to make decisions that one of the three groups will dislike rather than seeking a settlement that satisfies all of them 76:20–76:26. And he insists the DAO asset itself is worth keeping: Jupiter built what he calls the largest and most vibrant DAO in crypto, and "now I need to make it effective" 74:27–74:42.

🌐 Consensus CheckMeow's "largest and most vibrant DAO in crypto" is a self-assessment with no metric attached and no independent corroboration in this document — DAO size can be measured by treasury, by voter count, by proposal throughput or by staked supply, and the episode specifies none. Treat it as a claim about participation enthusiasm, not a measured ranking. 74:31

🔋 Jupiter Allocates Capital By "Energy," And Says So Without Apology

Asked how Jupiter decides what to build, buy or partner on, Meow gives an answer he knows sounds bad and gives it anyway 52:39–55:20.

"The core decision-making metric I use is energy... it sounds voodoo, but I really think it comes down to energy."

He is not being mystical about it when he unpacks the term. "Energy" resolves into three concrete conditions: the right set of people who want to do the thing, genuine demand from users, and flow with the team's existing competence. His claim is that when those align, difficulty is irrelevant — "I don't care how difficult, it gets done." And the converse is the sharper half: you can assemble the technically perfect team for a project and it will still not ship if the team is not feeling it or the users are not there.

He is candid about the failure mode. Intuition "might be wrong," so the counterweight is scheduled reflection — regularly stepping back to ask which decisions were bad and where the mental model needs updating 55:05–55:20. The two-month social blackout that preceded this interview was that mechanism firing.

He also volunteers the consequence, which most founders would not: "I'm not sure it's bullish or bearish for JUP" 54:27.

🧵 Running ThesisMeow's decision architecture is intuition plus periodic structured audit, with no formal gate in between. It explains both Jupiter's speed across an unusually wide product surface and the specific failure he describes in the token section — the DAO design was theoretically elegant and intuitively appealing, and nothing in the process caught the incentive conflict until holders had been fighting about it for a year. Status: confirmed by his own account.

Immediately before this, he describes Jupiter as "probably one of the most misunderstood, least understood projects in the space, because we do so many things that people really admire, but we also occasionally do things that people really didn't like" 55:02–55:17. Read alongside the energy method, that is a fairly precise description of what a high-variance intuitive process produces.

🧱 "Stacking Wins" — The Third Path Between Consistency And Big Swings

The strategic framework Meow offers for careers and companies alike sits at 45:27–52:39, and it is the section with the widest non-crypto application.

His observation is that people are taught two approaches and only two. The first is consistency: repeated small wins that run horizontally, reliable but non-compounding — "they'll do the same thing again and again for a long time." The second is the big swing: the 10x or 100x attempt, where a few succeed and most are removed from the game, because going for the big win repeatedly makes it "quite easy to die."

The underrated third path is stacking — consistently accumulating wins that each raise the ceiling of what the next attempt can be. The mechanism is network effects on reputation: keep stacking and at some point you can do things you could not previously do at all 46:57–48:01.

He uses Follonier's podcast as the worked example, and Follonier confirms the exchange happened. Follonier's original framing of his own future was anchored to where he currently was; Meow told him to think much bigger, repeatedly (44:59–45:27, 48:03–48:31). Meow's refinement is the important part: the instruction is not simply to think bigger. It is to pair big thinking with a consistent strategic method of piling up wins until you reach the point where you can actually execute on the big thought.

📜 AnalogThe stacking model is compound interest applied to capability rather than capital — each win raises the principal that the next attempt compounds on. The reason it is underrated is that it looks identical to plain consistency for a long time, and only diverges after the point where reputation starts unlocking previously unavailable moves. 47:01

He then applies it directly to Jupiter 48:51–49:22: the company is currently stacking competencies, mindsets, skill sets and talent specifically to be able to execute what he calls Jupiter V2. That is the operational meaning of the hiring and product sprawl described in the triple-S section — stacking capability ahead of a plan that needs it.

Follonier supplies the term Meow has been circling: synergies of scale. Meow accepts it and distinguishes it from economies of scale 49:23–52:39. Classical economies of scale are about physical resourcing — ten car factories that share nothing are worse than ten independent ones. Crypto's capital requirements are low enough that the hardware side of that logic barely applies; a large business can run on very little infrastructure. So the scale advantage relocates to people: assembling teams with distinct mindsets, competencies and ambitions such that each one executing its own mandate makes every other team better.

His illustration is the Jupiter portfolio product, and it is honest about the economics. As a standalone product it is "not interesting" and lacks economic value. Placed inside a system with many other ways of creating value, it becomes a distribution and usage engine pointed at a high-value user base.

🔗 ConnectionSynergies of scale → the triple-S framework — this is the economic justification for vertical integration. If the scale advantage in crypto comes from people and cross-team leverage rather than capital equipment, then owning more of the stack is rational in a way it would not be in a capital-intensive industry. It is the strongest argument in the episode for Jupiter's strategy, and Meow never quite states it as the argument. (51:02 → 38:44)

🐈 The Cat Doctrine: Identity As Something To Shed, Not Defend

The episode opens on what looks like a bit and turns into a genuine operating philosophy 12:02–15:52.

Asked who he is, Meow answers: "I really think I'm a cat." The reasoning is about adaptive range rather than whimsy. Cats are flexible, agile and survive anything, and there is a cat for every terrain — jungle, ice mountains, alleyways, desert sand cats that live on insects and minimal food, Persian cats in palaces. The lesson he draws is that the goal is to be "the right cat for the right situation": a chill domestic cat in a peaceful team environment, a tiger when it is time to go to war.

"What I hold in the highest regard is being able to let go of any emotional baggage, identity or hangups or insecurities, and just be whatever the moment needs me to be."

He then makes it concrete as a critique of crypto's dominant work culture 15:00–15:42. American popular culture and crypto culture both push a 24/7 always-on, always-winning posture. He tried it — "that's actually how I did this for the first year of Jupiter" — and concluded it does not work. What replaces it is cycle mastery: recognising highs and lows and being effective through both rather than pretending the lows are not happening.

💡 Key InsightThe cat framing is a founder's argument for low ego as an operational asset rather than a virtue. If your identity is invested in a position, you cannot abandon it when conditions change; the cost of conviction is paid in slow pivots. Read against the token post-mortem — where he had to publicly abandon the DAO design he had championed — the doctrine is doing real work rather than decorating an interview. 14:20
🔗 ConnectionCat doctrine → the two-month social blackout → the token post-mortem. The three are one sequence: shed the identity, take the cycle low seriously enough to withdraw, come back with the position revised. The interview is the output of the method he describes in its first ten minutes. (14:20 → 63:37 → 74:42)

Phase 3: Specific Item Breakdown

🟣 JUP — The Token Meow Says He Mis-Framed From Day One

A governance token whose launch framing outlived its usefulness, whose cash-flow mechanism nobody noticed, and whose founder now argues that neither is what sets the price.

What It Includes: JUP launched via the January 2024 airdrop that distributed roughly 1 billion tokens across close to a million wallets, framed to the community as a governance instrument. Governance rights, staking and quarterly staking rewards followed; the 50%-of-revenue buyback was layered on in February 2025.

The Evidence: Meow identifies the governance framing as a permanent anchor — "that narrative is still there today" 66:06. He identifies the voting mechanism as the second anchor, because a large share of votes concerned allocating budget to teams 66:27–66:47. And he identifies the absence of any communicated link between Jupiter's roadmap and JUP's future as the third 66:51–67:17. Against that, ATH $2.00 within a week of launch; ~$0.22 by September 2026; ~89% drawdown while the business grew. The interview's implicit claim — that this is a narrative failure rather than a supply or mechanism failure — is the part a reader should hold loosely, because unlock schedules and a 10bn supply are never discussed.

🟣 The Litterbox Trust — A Real Buyback Nobody Priced In

Fifty per cent of protocol revenue converted to JUP and locked for three years, running since February 2025, and described by its own creator as having failed to matter.

What It Includes: Half of Jupiter's protocol revenue is used to buy JUP on the open market; purchased tokens are held in the Litterbox multisig under a three-year lock. The other half converts to SOL/USDC for the treasury. Balance had reached roughly 96m JUP by September 2025.

The Evidence: Meow is emphatic that it is real spending, not an accounting device — "a very heavy investment... not earnings by [reference]" 65:00. His surprise is that surveying people revealed they did not know it existed 65:48. Crucially, the scale was overstated on air: the episode computes $150m of annual buybacks from a "$300m revenue" figure that is actually the fee line. On DefiLlama's ~$92m trailing-year revenue, the implied buyback is closer to $45–55m. The mechanism is confirmed; the magnitude discussed in the episode is not.

🟣 JupNet — The Omnichain Network Built Against Tribalism, Not Latency

Jupiter's attempt at a holistic network for navigating a many-chain world, premised on the claim that the unsolved problem is demand-side tribalism rather than message passing.

What It Includes: A network layer intended to unify liquidity and identity so that users, projects and assets can move across chains without bridging, gas management or wallet juggling. Its components as described on air: a rethink of execution, the ADI identity mechanism, and JupiterVM.

The Evidence: Meow's case rests on an empirical observation rather than a technical roadmap — bridged tokens with large native followings attracting near-zero volume on the destination chain 60:43. He lists liquidity, UX, security and accounting problems, then puts tribalism on top of them as the binding constraint 61:04–61:12. Timeline discipline matters here: public testnet was targeted for early Q4 2025 and mainnet by end-2025, and this conversation still discusses the design as active work. Verify mainnet status and third-party volume before treating it as shipped.

🟣 ADI — Passkey Identity As The Load-Bearing Piece

An onchain identity mechanism that replaces the Bitcoin-inherited private-key-and-wallet model with passkeys, framed by Meow as the prerequisite for a billion users interacting with a million chains.

What It Includes: Onchain passkeys that allow login and transaction signing without conventional wallet key management, developed over roughly a year with, in Meow's description, a hand-picked group of engineers.

The Evidence: His framing of the problem is precise 61:44–62:24: blockchain UX "is still pretty much the same as Bitcoin — private key, public key based, that means you always need a wallet to sign the stuff." The design question he set was what it would have to look like for a billion people and a million chains. This is the single highest-leverage item in the Jupiter roadmap if the web4 participation thesis is correct, and the one with the least independent evidence presented in the episode.

🟣 Triple-S — Full Surface, Full Suite, Full Stack

Jupiter's internal vertical-integration doctrine: be strong across every delivery channel, every product line, and every layer of the stack, because the iPhone moment required all three at once.

What It Includes: Full surface — web, mobile, APIs. Full suite — Ultra swap and terminal, screener, lending, portfolio, perps, with more in build. Full stack — contracts, data layer, API and UI design, validator operations, in-house validator software, and now Jupiter's own VM.

The Evidence: The strategic justification is the iPhone analogy — smartphones existed and were clunky until one team assembled hardware, OS, software, apps, support, distribution, retail, brand and backend simultaneously 36:52–37:41. The economic justification arrives sixty seconds later in a different section: synergies of scale, where crypto's low capital requirements relocate the scale advantage from equipment to cross-team leverage 51:02. The measurable parts hold up — ~95% aggregator share, ~80% of Solana perps, $2trn lifetime volume. The component superlatives ("best mobile app," "best portfolio product in the world") are self-graded and should be read as ambition.

🟣 The Three Universals — Access, Creation, Exchange

Jupiter's stated goal compressed into three principles: anyone can access any money, anyone can participate in creating money, and any money can be exchanged for any other without friction.

What It Includes: Universal accessibility — any person anywhere reaching the whole universe of assets. Universal creation — participation in the creation of monies, which Meow stresses is not "pressing a button" but a process of assembling stakeholders, building trust, governing and earning the trust of other monies. Universal exchange — chain- and format-agnostic swapping at low friction and low cost.

The Evidence: The contrast he draws is with gated capital pools — NASDAQ, CME, banks, the dollar itself 32:34–32:44. His cost argument for universal exchange is the FX comparison: "if people knew how much fees are in forex they'd go crazy — all these [loyalty] points are funded by really crazy forex fees" 34:04–34:18. Note that the FX-fee claim is asserted, not sourced, and no figure is given; it is directionally uncontroversial but should not be cited from this episode.

🟣 The Jupiter DAO — The Structure Meow Says Broke Trust

A governance system that was, by its architect's own account, elegant on paper and corrosive in practice, because it routed contributor funding through holder votes.

What It Includes: Token-weighted voting, work groups funded by DAO vote and carrying delegated legitimacy to build for and represent Jupiter, plus quarterly staking rewards.

The Evidence: Meow's own verdict: the concept "was great on paper... in reality there wasn't sufficient trust built up between the work groups — amazing people — and the token holders," repeated across many votes until it became structural 69:36–70:27. The external record is blunter: voting was suspended in June 2025, with the team acknowledging "a breakdown in trust," no new DAO-funded work groups, existing ones continuing, staking rewards held at 50m JUP per quarter, and a reset promised for 2026. One material complaint from holders — the team's own voting weight — appears in the public record and not in this interview.
