🏦 "Not a Pure Acquisition of a New Business Line": What Franklin Actually Bought

"So we're actually — this is not a pure acquisition of a new business line. This is a merger of a fantastic existing business with the liquid business that we spun out of CoinFund. And this is just a doubling down." — Seth Ginns 14:00

Avi's question is the right blunt one: "Why did a multi-billion-dollar traditional fund buy a crypto fund?" 13:24 Ginns's answer reframes the premise three times.

First, on timing: Franklin did not arrive in 2026. "Franklin started getting involved with crypto at the end of last decade… 2017, 2018." Avi presses — "You're saying they've been in crypto for almost a decade" — and Ginns confirms. In that period Franklin "built out fantastic tokenization technology, asset management arm." 13:30 · 13:47

Second, on scale: the deal is a merge of two teams, not the hire of one. Eight people came across; fifteen-plus were already there. 13:53

Third, on motive: this is a downturn purchase. "What do you do if you have high conviction in an investment — you have high conviction in the TAM, in the team — and you're in a cyclical downturn? You double down." 14:17

🎯 The Trade Is the Disconnect: Institutional Intent vs. Price

"There's a really big disconnect between the fundamental momentum of this convergence, the fundamental interest across the sell side right now in getting engaged with crypto, and where prices are. That disconnect is something that Franklin felt was really attractive." — Seth Ginns 15:10

This is the actual investment thesis, and it is worth stating precisely because Ginns is careful about it. He is not claiming crypto is cheap on fundamentals. He is claiming that the rate of institutional engagement he can observe privately is running ahead of what price reflects — and that Franklin has a structural read on that rate because "we're talking to all of our peers… we're talking to the sell side." He calls it, in as many words, "a little bit of an unfair advantage." 14:37 · 14:46

He also states the client-behaviour constraint that shapes the product: Franklin needs to be ready to help clients access crypto "in a variety of ways that meet the way that they want to invest in the space, which is probably not up 400% down 80%." 15:40

🎯 Against Consensus The crypto-native consensus in a drawdown is that institutions are the late, trend-following money — they arrive at tops. Ginns argues the opposite from inside the building: institutional engagement is a slow-moving, contract-and-committee process whose lead time is measured in quarters, and which therefore decouples from price by construction. Whether that is insight or incumbency bias is unfalsifiable from the outside — but note the firm backed it with a balance-sheet acquisition in a downturn, which is a costlier signal than a podcast appearance. 15:10
🧵 Running Thesis "2026 is the year that crypto and traditional finance start to converge." Ginns dates the conviction to "the end of last year, beginning of this year" — the CoinFund spinout decision was itself downstream of it, taken with his partner Chris Perkins. Status: developing / partially confirmed. Confirmed on the plumbing (NYSE–OKX, NYSE–Securitize, Nasdaq–Kraken, the SEC's BENJI no-action letter); unconfirmed on the flows. 05:11 · 05:42
🌐 Consensus Check The "TradFi is coming" claim is now largely infrastructure-verifiable rather than a matter of opinion. During 2026: ICE (NYSE's parent) took a strategic stake in and partnered with OKX (tokenised NYSE-listed equities to OKX customers as early as H2 2026); NYSE tapped Securitize to design its tokenised-securities platform; Nasdaq announced an issuer-centric equity-token design plus a Kraken/Payward gateway, with DLT services targeted from H1 2027 and SEC approval of its tokenised-securities proposal. 🗳️ The CLARITY Act: "It Doesn't Really Matter If It Passes. What Matters Is That the Vote Happens."

"It doesn't really matter if CLARITY passes. What matters is that the vote happens." — Seth Ginns 16:39

Avi does the single most useful thing an interviewer can do here — he refuses the abstraction: "People often say 'we're having conversations' and I often say, like, what? What are the specifics? Maybe one or two concrete things for people to sink their teeth into." 16:10 What comes back is the best material in the episode.

1. Enforcement risk is already priced out. "Everyone understands that the administration is very forward-thinking with regard to crypto and the administration is not going to go after you if you're a big bank that wants to start leaning into crypto. In fact, you're helping their agenda" — tokenised equities, more stablecoin adoption. He adds the fiscal kicker: "stablecoins use treasuries as collateral and that creates a bid for US treasuries." 16:46 · 17:06

2. So the bill is not about permission — it is about preemption. If institutions give up on federal legislation and take the available path, they end up with "a New York BitLicense… state-by-state regulation" and no federal preemption. And the moment they do that, "you undermine the case, you undermine the momentum for getting CLARITY passed." 17:26 · 17:37

3. Hence the cost of not voting is the worst of the three outcomes. "The fact that it hasn't come to a vote is actually creating a little holding pattern." Desks have told Franklin "we're ready to build out our capital markets activities in crypto" — "these are all of the traditional primes" — "but they want CLARITY to come to a vote. If it passes, great — federal preemption goes super fast, we know exactly what that looks like. If it doesn't pass, okay, we're still going to move forward, it's just going to be a little slower. But until it comes to a vote, we're kind of waiting. We can't move forward aggressively." 17:40 · 18:13

💡 Key Insight This inverts the standard trader framing of legislation as a binary catalyst. In Ginns's telling the market is not pricing pass vs. fail — it is pricing resolution vs. limbo, and limbo is strictly the worst state because it freezes capital-allocation decisions inside institutions that cannot start building against an unknown regime. On that logic, a failed cloture vote is less damaging to build-out than an indefinitely postponed one. That is a genuinely non-obvious, tradeable distinction. 17:40
🔍 Source Verified ✅ The "holding pattern" was real and is still live as of this writing. The Senate opened the first procedural stage of the Digital Asset Market CLARITY Act in early August 2026; Majority Leader John Thune filed a cloture motion on the motion to proceed, setting a 15 September 2026 procedural vote — requiring 60 votes — after the Senate's return on 14 September. Open sticking points reported: government-ethics provisions, law-enforcement provisions, stablecoin yield/rewards, and whether state attorneys general get secondary enforcement power. With roughly 14 working days before an October election recess, multiple outlets report that passage in 2026 is widely considered unlikely. Ginns's structural point survives either outcome — but his "the vote hasn't happened" framing has a hard expiry date of 15 Sept 2026. (Sources: CoinDesk, 8 Aug 2026; CNBC, 1 Sep 2026; Latham & Watkins US Crypto Policy Tracker.)

Both can be true, but they imply different urgency: the first says build at leisure, the second says the window is roughly two years wide and closes on an election. 📈 "Not Necessarily in the Moment Today": Ginns's Valuation Framework

"Whether you're talking about crypto or whether you're talking about equities, it's about having amazing visionary founders, it's about having product-market fit, it's about seeing the opportunity for revenue down the road and seeing the path toward value capture down the road — either for the equity or the token. But it's not about necessarily having that in the moment today." — Seth Ginns 09:35

Avi sets up the standard objection: crypto assets "don't generate revenue… people say you're investing in vaporware." 06:03 Ginns's answer has two halves, and the second is the interesting one.

Half one — crypto is converging on equity-like value capture

In 2020, "there weren't that many fundamentally driven names. The exchange tokens were the only names that had a direct line from generating revenue to a buyback." Even then it was opaque: "some of them did it off of revenue, some of them did it off of earnings; if they did it off of earnings, you didn't know how much they were growing headcount, how much they were paying people. So you didn't have that certainty of value capture." 06:27 · 06:49

Today his exemplar is explicit: "Hyperliquid is winning because they have strong revenue growth. They have a great business, but they also have direct value capture in the token." And he describes a live imitation dynamic — "crypto is great at having protocols borrow from other protocols that have things that are working" — with founders now asking him "what do you think we should do with value capture? How should we think about ascribing value capture to the token?" 07:06 · 07:22

Half two — the premise that equities have clean value capture is itself wrong

"You have legal value capture in the equity, but a lot of times you have equities that do really well but are unprofitable. And a lot of times you have equities that do really well but don't have any revenue right now — they're almost like a narrative or memecoin." His examples of narrative-first sectors that later became real: nuclear and quantum. 07:53 · 08:15

The historical proof is two names from the Jennison book. Tesla (~2012): "when we invested they hadn't started shipping the Model S. So they were on the precipice potentially of being bankrupt if there was a problem with the Model S. Now, that was a decision tree that ended up taking the path that led to a highly profitable business." (The caption garbles a second name here as "Tesla Genesis"; marked [unclear].) 08:25 · 08:40

Amazon (2011–12): a consumer-investor friend told him "the retail business, I just can't get it to profitability… I don't understand how institutional investors, serious investors, can own this." Meanwhile Jennison's own analysts "were taking Amazon's revenue, putting Walmart's net margin on it, and then putting a higher multiple on it." Ginns's verdict on his own side: "they ended up being totally wrong." The profit engine was AWS and the ad platform, not retail margin. 08:51 · 09:21

💡 Key Insight The Amazon story is told against the analysts, not in praise of them — and that is what makes it useful. Jennison owned the right stock through the right decade on the wrong model. Ginns's takeaway is not "we called it"; it is that a valuation model is a placeholder for conviction about management and optionality, and being directionally right about the founder can survive being completely wrong about the mechanism. Applied to tokens, that is an argument for underwriting teams and adoption over building a DCF on emissions. 09:21
⚡ Tension Ginns holds two positions that sit uneasily together. At 07:06 Hyperliquid is praised precisely because it has revenue and direct token value capture — the fundamentals-first case. At 09:55 the framework says value capture today is explicitly not required. The reconciliation he implies but never states: value capture is the exit criterion, not the entry criterion — you underwrite the path, and demand the proof before the position becomes core.
🌐 Consensus Check The "tokens are converging on cash-flow assets" claim now has broad buy-side company — the 2024–26 shift toward fee-switch and buyback designs is real and visibly driven by imitation of the highest-revenue venue tokens, exactly as Ginns describes. The honest counter, which he does not address: most of that value capture remains discretionary and governance-revocable, unlike the legal claim attached to equity. He acknowledges the equity claim is "legal" and then argues equity's economics are often just as speculative — a fair point about cash flows, but not about enforceability.

🧠 Vetting Management: "Are They a Student of Their End Market?"

"You're really betting on management… That's the algorithm that's the same whether you're doing early-stage startup investing, public equities or crypto tokens." — Seth Ginns 10:33 · 11:24

Ginns argues the AI cycle has made management quality more decisive, not less: "with AI and with the cadence of innovation that you see in AI, you can't bet on cash-flow businesses, just cash cows that generate good earnings, consistent but without a lot of growth year in and year out. You can't bet on that continuing indefinitely with all of the change that's happening." 10:48 · 11:06

Asked for the actual process, his answer is unglamorous — "spending a lot of time with them" — but the checklist underneath is specific: 11:41

  • Curiosity, scoped. "How well read they are, how curious they are — not about everything… but are they a student of their end market? Are they a student of their competition? Are they a student of business in general? What's worked, what hasn't worked?" 11:54 · 12:07
  • Personal risk concentration as a signal. "If you're launching a business, you're making a very concentrated bet — you're putting a lot of your net worth and a lot of your future wealth in one name. So it's about how seriously they're taking that commitment… how much they're putting on the line." 12:15 · 12:37
  • Access as an asymmetry crypto still owns. "You could just send a message to most crypto founders and they would respond to you in the early days." Harder now at scale, "but they're still far more easy to get access to… than it is in the public markets, outside of earnings calls." 12:40 · 13:05
📋 Protocol Cheat Sheet
  • Management diligence, four questions (Ginns, 11:54–12:37): (1) student of their end market? (2) student of their competition? (3) student of business generally — what has worked and what hasn't? (4) how much personal net worth is concentrated in this one name?
  • Founder access, crypto: cold-message the founder directly — still a materially higher hit-rate than public-company IR. Public-equity analogue: earnings calls only.
  • Entry test for a token position: don't require value capture today; require (a) a visionary founder, (b) demonstrated product-market fit, (c) a specific named path to revenue, (d) a specific named path to token value capture. Underwrite (c) and (d) as dated milestones.
  • Career advice, verbatim (19:49): "Take measured risks. Learn as much as you can. Meet as many people as you can. Get as much advice as you can, but take shots on goal… You miss 100% of the shots on goal that you don't take. Learn what you're good at, learn where you have an edge, and then lean into that."
  • Calendar trigger: Senate CLARITY Act cloture vote, 15 September 2026 — the event Ginns says unfreezes prime-broker crypto build-outs either way.
  • Watch instead of headlines: NYSE/OKX tokenised-equity go-live (targeted H2 2026); Nasdaq DLT services (targeted H1 2027); tokenised-Treasury AUM and BENJI's share of it (rwa.xyz).

🕯️ Lehman, in the Building, on the Day: The Formative Scene

"I was in Lehman's headquarters the day they went under, meeting with the management. And the analyst that brought me in was like, 'I don't know if I'm going to have a job tomorrow. I don't know if this is still going to exist tomorrow.'" — Seth Ginns 00:00 · 22:39

The producers open the episode cold on this line, and they are right to. Everything Ginns says about institutional risk descends from it.

Asked what the craziest day of the GFC was for him personally, he does not reach for a price. He reaches for a room. He was at Lehman's headquarters, in a normal buy-side management meeting, on the day the firm failed — with an analyst host who did not know whether his employer would exist the following morning. 22:34 · 22:54

What makes the passage land is the doubling he describes: "on the one hand things were just functioning like they always do — like, we were meeting with managements — and then on the other hand you're looking at what stocks were doing and it was absolutely crazy." He gives a second scene from the same period: "I remember being in Rock Center talking to GE['s] CEO as they were weathering the crisis, because GE Capital had a lot of exposure." 23:00 · 23:16

And then the observation that is genuinely his own: the human system lagged the price system. "There was really an element of shock and disbelief on the human side of it that only kind of caught up to where prices were weeks or months afterwards." 23:36 · 23:48

💡 Key Insight That lag — prices repricing instantly, people repricing over weeks — is the exact structure of Ginns's 2026 trade, run in reverse. In 2008, price knew before the institutions did. His claim today is that institutions know before price does: the sell-side desks are already committed, the balance-sheet decisions already made, and price hasn't caught up. Same lag, opposite sign. He never draws the parallel out loud; it is the spine of the interview. 23:36 · 15:10
📜 Analog Ginns's route into crypto is itself a GFC artefact. "The GFC is how I got into crypto" — because being "exposed to macro in my day job" made him want "early-stage startups that are secular growth that are going to grow no matter what's happening in macro… so I don't have to bet on Bernanke." He describes the QE regime precisely: "he was doing these bursts of QE that were 250 billion — you would get a boost to the market and then he would pull it back and the market would come down." That search led to Y Combinator deal flow, and to Coinbase. 01:38 · 02:26

The Coinbase seed, and the Mt. Gox tax

The origin story is unusually honest about the cost of hesitation. Ginns read a New Yorker article about Bitcoin in 2011, thought "this is cool" — "but I have to buy the Bitcoin on Mt. Gox. Mt. Gox seemed a little shady. Turns out it was a little shady. So I didn't do anything." 02:39 · 02:50

The entry came later and sideways: via FundersClub, an accredited-investor crowdfunding platform that had itself been through Y Combinator and "started listing all their batchmates — and their batchmates were Coinbase and Instacart." He invested in the Coinbase seed round in 2012 and held it "all the way to IPO." Avi's response — "So what are you doing talking to me? Why aren't you on your yacht?" — gets the honest answer: "Too much fun, man." 02:52 · 03:29

🌍 "A Hyper-Levered Global Economy": Why Ginns Thinks It's Fine

"I think it's a problem to keep an eye on, but I also think it's totally going to be okay." — Seth Ginns 25:33

Ginns's explanation for elevated volatility across both asset classes is a single structural claim: "part of the volatility is tied to the fact that we have a hyper-levered global economy and we have massively disruptive technology right now." 25:10

His reason for optimism is mechanical, not sentimental. AI drives "massive productivity gains… massively disinflationary over time", which drives "huge nominal GDP growth" — and therefore "the path to growing our way out of our over-indebtedness is more clear today than it's ever been." Five years ago, he says, the only visible exit was "purely going to be a debasement trade" — printing. 25:41 · 26:13

Then he asks the question against his own book, which is the mark of a serious answer: if we now get real GDP growth and debasement together, does that dilute the case for Bitcoin? His answer is no, and the mechanism is the interesting part: "the pace of penetration of these new technologies is going to drive such fast productivity gains and such volatility in the way the economy is structured that we're probably going to have to print." He credits the framing to a recent Elon Musk podcast appearance — "if you speedrun all this big deflationary pressure, then what do you do to offset that? You have to print" — landing on strong real GDP growth, offset by deflationary pressure, requiring printing to keep nominal accelerating. 26:32 · 27:10

Avi's reaction is the honest one: "It kind of makes me feel like this is a simulation, because we nailed it perfectly — right as we're coming up on this big issue, right as inflation's kicking off, we're getting this massive deflationary shock and AI actually expanding its use cases and real GDP coming back." 27:11 · 27:28

🎯 Against Consensus The mainstream debasement trade says print → currency debasement → own hard assets. Ginns argues something more specific and more contrarian: AI-driven disinflation makes printing structurally necessary rather than politically optional, so the debasement bid persists even in a strong real-growth regime. That is a meaningfully different claim from "money printer go brrr" — it says the hard-asset bid survives good news, which is precisely the scenario most debasement bulls quietly fear. 26:32
⚡ Tension Ginns's two framings of the same fact are in tension and he presents both without choosing. Framing A (25:41–26:13): leverage is fine because AI bails us out — technology as exogenous good fortune. Framing B (27:33–28:55): leverage is fine because the system is designed that way — "the US system drives excess leverage, but it drives excess leverage in just enough of an intelligent, thoughtful way to drive an acceleration in technological innovation." A is luck; B is design. If B is right, the "problem to keep an eye on" isn't really a problem at all — and the hedge case weakens.

The multiple-discovery coda

Ginns closes on his most abstract point, and it is the philosophical basis for Framing B. Why, he asks, "did you have multiple people come up with calculus at the same time?" Why do so many discoveries — he names evolution as another — arrive simultaneously from "multiple people who were in different parts of the world who weren't directly communicating"? 28:57 · 29:25

The standard answer, which he adopts: "you had enough of the precursors." His extension: "the US is really good — our capitalist system is really good at finding those precursors and somewhat intelligently allocating capital, and again sometimes in an overlevered way, in order to move us to that next technological progression." He frames the destination loosely as "the singularity… recursive AI self-improvement" or "the convergence of all different technological paths — healthcare and industrial tech, so hard technology as well as software and AI." 28:20 · 29:48

🌐 Consensus Check Multiple discovery (simultaneous invention) is a well-established concept in the history and sociology of science, and Ginns's two examples are the textbook ones: Newton and Leibniz on calculus, Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace on natural selection. The "precursors" explanation he gives is the standard scholarly account — discovery becomes near-inevitable once the necessary prior conditions exist. His extension of it to capital allocation as a precursor-finding mechanism is his own analogy, not an established finding; read it as rhetoric, not evidence.
💡 Key Insight Read structurally, the multiple-discovery coda is the same argument as the CLARITY Act argument in a different domain. In both, the specific event is nearly irrelevant and the state of the preconditions is everything — CLARITY passing doesn't matter, the vote clearing the fog does; Newton didn't matter, the precursors did. That is a consistent worldview, and it makes his 2026 convergence call legible: he is not forecasting a catalyst, he is claiming the preconditions are already assembled. 16:39 · 29:28

🟢 BENJI / Franklin OnChain U.S. Government Money Fund (FOBXX) — SHIPPED, and the flagship

The first US-registered mutual fund to use a public blockchain as its system of record — and now, remarkably, a currency Franklin uses to pay for acquisitions.

What It Is

FOBXX is a US-registered government money market fund; BENJI is its on-chain share token. Blockchain records carry purchases, redemptions, dividend distributions, NAVs and trade dates; sensitive shareholder data stays in Franklin's internal records.

Chains

Eight public networks — Stellar, Polygon, Arbitrum, Aptos, Avalanche, Base, Solana, Ethereum — with the majority of BENJI assets on Stellar.

Size

rwa.xyz-derived reporting puts BENJI total asset value at ~$2.44B (July 2026); a separate Q1 2026 figure cites ~$828M for FOBXX specifically. ⚠️ These are not the same denominator — the BENJI token now spans more than one Franklin tokenised fund — so cite the one you mean.

Regulatory Status (the most important 2026 development)

On 12 August 2026 the SEC's Division of Investment Management issued a no-action letter clearing Franklin's registered mutual funds and ETFs to invest directly in BENJI for cash management. The relief exempts Franklin from paragraphs (b), (e) and (f) of Rule 17f-2 under the Investment Company Act of 1940 — provisions written for physical securities custody. The safeguard structure: Franklin's affiliated transfer agent (FTIS) retains full control of private keys, administrative functions and the official shareholder file, so records can be corrected or restored.

🔍 Source Verified ✅ The 40-Act / transfer-agent point is real and specific, and it is the answer to "how does a blockchain fund satisfy a 1940 statute?" — it doesn't, exactly; it gets targeted relief. The integrated model (internal book-entry plus Stellar transaction records, with the affiliated transfer agent holding the keys and the authoritative shareholder file) is what let SEC staff grant relief without abandoning the custody rule. Note the direction of travel: Franklin's traditional funds can now hold Franklin's tokenised fund — on-chain infrastructure moving inward into the conventional fund complex, not outward to crypto customers. (Sources: The Block, 12 Aug 2026; SEC Division of Investment Management no-action letter.)
