🖼️ NFTs Are In Winter, But The Collecting Impulse Moved Rather Than Died

Felman opens the substantive conversation by putting his own worst position on the table.

"I've seen a lot of people dunking on NFTs and at the same time collecting Pokémon cards, and I'm like — you do realise those are NFTs, right? Like, they're physical NFTs." 02:51

The position is an Ether Rock, bought for about $50,000, marked at one point at a $3 million position, now quoted around $100,000 — which Felman says he will probably never sell 02:18. The question he puts to gmoney is simply whether any of it comes back.

gmoney's answer does not defend NFT prices; it relocates the demand. The people dunking on JPEGs are often the same people bidding up graded Pokémon cards, and the 2020–21 sequence ran in that order — the physical card market went nuts first, then the NFT market did 02:56. His long-term thesis was never about art: a fifteen-year-old more digitally native than anyone older will eventually have money and want to buy something digital, because the psychology underneath a card and a PFP is identical 03:13.

The mechanism he names is status conversion. When traders want to lock in gains and show them, they buy watches, cars and houses; his 2021 bet was that people making money on chain would want to display it on chain, when ETH was around $500 04:27.

💡 Key InsightThe interesting part of gmoney's answer is that it is an argument about sequencing, not about NFTs. If the collecting impulse is stable and only the wrapper changes, then the physical card boom is the leading indicator and the NFT market is the levered expression of it — which makes "are NFTs back" a question about where the marginal collector's money is, not about whether the art was good. He holds the position he is describing: CryptoPunks as his profile picture, plus Chromie Squiggles and other Art Blocks work. (03:13, 05:04)

🧪 NFTs 2.0 Pays In Tokenized Stock, Not In Floor Price

The strongest section of the episode is gmoney's account of what he calls NFTs 2.0 — constructs that give an idle collectible a cash flow.

"We're going to see this massive race between all these issuers trying to dominate tokenized stocks, the way Circle and Tether have really dominated stablecoins." 09:24

The first is Quotrons, which he later names as his biggest on-chain position. His description: a one-way 404 — it starts as a liquid token, you can burn the token into an NFT, and the NFT cannot be converted back. Once burned, the NFT receives trading fees paid out in tokenized stock, so every trade in the pair pays holders in equities rather than in the project's own token 05:14. He calls it a yield play he does not want to sell, and notes the floor has risen since he bought 05:54.

The second is Fake World Assets. Gacha is a blind box — pack ripping, where you pay $25, rip, and find out whether the card inside is worth $10 or $1,000, with an instant sell-back at a 10–15% discount (27:33, 28:06). FWA applied that to NFTs with one addition that matters: you can play, or you can deposit, which means you can be the house 28:11. CryptoPunks and Bored Apes sit in the pool. gmoney's read is that the protocol layer is built and the remaining problem is distribution — spins drive daily revenue (29:44, 31:32).

🔍 Source Verified✅ Fake World Assets is an on-chain NFT gacha protocol built by TokenWorks, a two-person team of the developers gmoney names (Rhynotic) and tetonotsorry, using Chainlink's verifiable random function to settle draws. It relaunched on 20 July 2026 and moved extremely fast: roughly 90,000 transactions, 35,000 purchases and 2,000 ETH of volume, with peak daily fees near $1.53mn on 25 July and about $8.8mn of cumulative fees inside thirty days. It also had a violent drawdown that gmoney does not mention — after a dispute over launch-fee distribution the token fell to an all-time low of $0.0066 in early August, 78% below its 26 July peak, taking market capitalisation to roughly $8mn before TokenWorks raised the buyback allocation from 50% to 80% of protocol fees. The roughly $12mn market cap he quotes on air is a recovery off that low, not a fresh listing.
🌐 Consensus CheckThe tokenized-equity land-grab gmoney describes is already well underway, and the stablecoin analogy is closer than it sounds. Four issuers anchor the category: Backed Finance's xStocks, Dinari's dShares, Robinhood's EU-listed tokens on Robinhood Chain, and Kraken — which agreed in December 2025 to acquire Backed outright, taking ownership of the issuance standard. Kraken's xStocks are issued on Solana, Ethereum, TON and Ink, which is the bridge gmoney refers to at 38:07. Robinhood Chain's tokenized assets grew from about $11.9mn at its July launch to $149.4mn by 4 September, roughly 77% of it equities. Three days after this taping, Kraken launched xStocks vaults paying on-chain yield on tokenized S&P 500, Nasdaq and Nvidia exposure — the exact product shape gmoney is betting on. 09:24

The third construct is "memefied" tokens — memecoins paired with tokenized stocks, which gmoney notes was simply not possible before tokenized equities existed 08:06. He is watching rather than playing, and says why in a way that is more honest than most: he cannot hold conviction on a meme, and if he steps away from the desk when someone decides to sell, he was the exit liquidity 08:28.

His structural point is better than his trade: meme pairs are a demand sink that drags equities on chain. "Why do you need stocks on chain" gets the same answer as "why do you need dollars on chain" — once they are there, people build with them 09:04.

One precision, noted once. gmoney describes the Quotrons payout as "tokenized stock dividends" 05:14. The project's own documentation is explicit that distributions to burned — "hardwired" — terminals are promotional rewards under a rewards programme and are not dividends or investment income, and that the burn is irreversible. The mechanic is as he describes it; the legal characterisation deliberately is not.

🎰 Memecoins Are A Funnel, And Both Men Are Standing At Its Mouth

Neither speaker defends memecoins as investments. Both defend them as a distribution channel, which is a different and more defensible claim.

"I ran a trading desk for 10 plus years, and if somebody used me as exit liquidity, they'd be kicked off the desk." 11:45

Felman's framing: memecoins are onboarding points. It is easier to convince someone to open a wallet and fund it with a hundred dollars if they believe they can 30x it than for any other reason, and some share of those people stick around 09:40. His evidence is the previous week's guest — the founder of the trading app FOMO — who described running user-generated-content campaigns, short-form TikTok videos from small accounts, that brought in people who had never bought a stock or a coin in their lives 10:11. Felman is clear about what the instrument is: "this thing is just a lottery ticket at the end of the day" 10:43.

gmoney's objection is cultural rather than financial. He accepts that memecoin traders see the game as player-versus-player, a Fortnite frame in which getting someone to buy so you can sell into them is a badge of honour 12:07. What he rejects is the inversion of the desk norm he grew up with: information gets shared inside a circle because making money is hard, not to manufacture a counterparty 11:56. Felman's shorthand for the same distinction is PvP versus PvE — you want to be playing PvE 14:12.

💡 Key InsightThe disclosure problem in this segment is structural, and both men name it themselves without quite resolving it. Felman asks the obvious question — why would an investor host a show and give away alpha? — and answers that short-term trades do not matter once he is positioned, and long-term theses have room for everyone to win (14:30, 14:49). That is a real answer for the second category and a weaker one for the first, and it is the reason he says he deliberately does not publish his complete book: the trades change 16:57.

Felman's cautionary example is Luna, bought around 30 cents and ridden to roughly a 300x before selling somewhere around $20–40 (12:56, 13:10). The lesson is not "avoid scams" but that he did not uncover the fabricated Chai payment data until later, and got out when the mint-and-burn mechanism with UST became too large (13:22, 13:37). His surviving rule: the experiments that last are the ones with a narrative people genuinely believe — which is why, on his read, dogwifhat is dead 13:54.

🏛️ If AI Is National Defence, The Hike Is A Formality

The macro turn is the most quotable stretch on the tape, and gmoney has clearly been running the framework for a while.

"The market knows. The market sniffed them out. That's why gold caught a bid. That's why Bitcoin got a bid." 19:56

He sets it up as a year-old position: if you view AI as a matter of national defence, which he believes the US government now does, there is no slowing the train — Washington will spend whatever it takes for as long as it takes in order to beat China 18:47. Everything else follows from that. Any blip gets met, and he points at two from the previous month as proof of the reflex: the Japanese yen intervention, and Bessent announcing he would buy the long end of the curve and then scaling it up days later (19:08, 19:16).

The conclusion is a two-part call, and the second part is the falsifiable one. Warsh has to hike at the next meeting to maintain credibility 19:26. But it will not be a long cycle, because the government cannot afford one 20:07.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Both interventions happened as described. Japan's Ministry of Finance conducted a coordinated yen-buying operation with the US Treasury in early August 2026 — Bank of Japan data pointed to roughly $34bn deployed in a single session, a record monthly total — and the direct US participation, carried out under the joint finance ministers' statement of September 2025, was itself the unusual part. Separately, Treasury more than doubled debt buyback operations to at least $4bn each from 9 September after the 30-year Treasury hit a 19-year high, with Bessent saying afterwards they could go larger. Both are exactly the "why are you doing this near the highs" behaviour gmoney is pointing at.

Felman's own AI position is disclosed in the same breath. 💳 The Credit Objection, Raised And Dismissed

gmoney then argues against his own book, which is the most useful thing either of them does.

"I think that the CDS spreads are fake." 23:46

His devil's advocate case starts from how fast bailouts have compressed. From Countrywide going under to Lehman was about two years; Silicon Valley Bank took about two weeks for contagion to settle. If an AI capex contagion appears, he expects it handled inside a week, plausibly 48 hours (22:12, 22:36). He also flags what would change the answer — if leverage grew five or ten times from here it becomes a populist question, and the difference between Bear Stearns and Lehman was the public mood, not the balance sheets (23:02, 23:23).

Felman's response is the harder claim. Credit risk at Google, Microsoft and Meta is, in his view, basically equivalent to US government credit risk regardless of how much debt they raise; these are not financial institutions, they are not employing mass leverage, they cannot run a Lehman-style balance-sheet mismatch, and so a widening spread is something you know will come back in (23:53, 24:20). He is candid that people he has argued this with think he is nuts 23:53.

🌐 Consensus CheckThe market is repricing something narrower than "hyperscalers." Oracle is the outlier — its five-year CDS went from roughly 40bp in mid-2025 to a peak near 190bp in April 2026, settling around 180bp — while Meta sits closer to 75bp, which is much nearer Felman's read. The scale, though, is real: hyperscalers issued roughly $182bn of bonds in 2026, an order-of-magnitude jump on the prior year, and the group is on track to spend more on capex than it generates in free cash flow. Apollo published a warning on hyperscaler debt five days after this taping. Felman's distinction — that this is issuance against cash flow rather than levered maturity transformation — survives those numbers; his implication that the spread carries no information does not survive Oracle. 24:07

Nvidia is where both land. gmoney bought into the August earnings and posted the trade beforehand 25:02. His forward thesis is the more interesting half: because Nvidia financed much of the neocloud build-out, it now holds residual economics across that capacity — a percentage of revenues, capital-light, closer to a software business than a hardware one, and therefore deserving of a higher multiple (25:33, 25:48). He adds the technical: the stock had consolidated for roughly two years 26:04.

🧭 Frameworks Over Trades, And Why Not Investing Is Now A Policy Problem

The closing third is about behaviour, and it is where Felman makes his largest claim.

"Investing is becoming a national security issue for the United States." 33:06

The argument: the next decade produces an extraordinary amount of wealth at companies like Nvidia, Microsoft and Meta because of AI; the economy will be unrecognisable; anyone not invested is left far behind; and a large uninvested population becomes "incredibly discontent" and causes societal problems. The policy conclusion he draws is that successive administrations will need to get every American's money working somehow (33:26, 33:40).

gmoney arrives at a near-identical place from the opposite direction, crediting a frame he attributes to a previous 1000x guest: in a world of hyper-unemployment, everyone hyper-gambles, that behaviour gets encouraged by the powers that be, and the positioning conclusion is that you want to own the casinos (32:34, 32:45).

🔗 ConnectionThese two claims are the same trade wearing different clothes. Felman's version is that participation becomes a state interest and therefore gets subsidised; gmoney's is that gambling becomes the participation mechanism. Both end at owning the venue rather than the ticket — which is precisely why Felman spends the preceding segment describing FOMO's leaderboard as the thing actually driving volume, and predicting that everything gets "FOMO-ified," including casino table games. (32:23, 33:06)

The practical advice underneath is unusually concrete for this format. Felman: if you are gambling on memecoins or trading perps, make sure you also hold spot, so that a blow-up does not leave you with no exposure to what is coming — NASDAQ, Bitcoin, the future (34:00, 34:20). Keep leverage trading to 5–10% of the book 36:03. gmoney: if you are not actively trading, do not use leverage at all; and if you are, use stops. His example is a friend up 150% day trading for a month whose method was that the market goes down and then it goes up, and who does not use stops (34:34, 34:57).

Felman's own anecdote is the sharper one. At a wedding in Mystic, Connecticut, an older man asked him for investment advice and showed him a portfolio held entirely inside FOMO — memecoins plus some Bitcoin, and nothing else (35:19, 35:41). His reaction, "what am I looking at," is followed by the reason he says he does the show at all 35:57. He told the man to buy QQQ, and the man had at least heard of it 37:04.

📋 Protocol Cheat Sheet
  • Hold spot exposure behind any leveraged or memecoin book — Felman's floor is that a blow-up must not leave you with zero exposure to NASDAQ and Bitcoin.
  • Cap discretionary leverage at 5–10% of the portfolio.
  • If you use leverage, use stops; gmoney treats their absence as the single reliable predictor of a blow-up.
  • Re-evaluate the thesis when new information arrives, and accept that changing position publicly is the job rather than an embarrassment 16:07.
  • Judge a small-cap protocol on convexity: gmoney's stated test on Quotrons is a roughly $10mn market cap where the upside is large if right and the loss is tolerable if wrong 39:11.

🟩 Nvidia (NVDA) — Bought Into Earnings, Held For The Royalty

gmoney's disclosed AI core position, with a forward thesis about residual neocloud economics rather than chip volumes.

What It Covers: He bought into the August earnings and posted the view publicly beforehand, which Felman confirms on air (25:02, 25:12). He notes it had consolidated for roughly two years 26:04.

Why It Matters: It reframes the circular-financing criticism as the bull case. Nvidia reported $96.2bn of revenue for the quarter ended 26 July, up 106% year over year, and guided to $108bn.

Stated Risk: Named but not sized — the circular-financing objection he is inverting, and the capex-contagion scenario he spends the preceding segment arguing would be contained.

🔵 Intel (INTC) and SanDisk — Bought Into Abandonment

Felman's entry into chipmakers at the moment he judged the AI trade had been given up on.

What It Covers: "Once crypto started ripping… Bitcoin hit 80K like a week ago, Intel was trading at 87 bucks a share, SanDisk was down a ton. People had moved on completely from the AI trade" — and those are his favourite moments to enter (20:35, 20:44).

Why It Matters: A rotation call as much as a stock call: capital leaving semis for crypto is the condition he is buying. Five days later he was still long Intel, and it rose about 5% that day.

Stated Risk: Not stated here. The implicit risk is that abandonment was correct rather than early.

🟪 Micron and Nebius — The Long-Duration Half Of The Barbell

gmoney's slow AI exposure, explicitly held on a different clock from his on-chain positions.

What It Covers: "I'm bullish on Micron, Nvidia, Nebius — but I have long-term calls on them. Am I going to 10x that in a month? No" (26:23, 26:35). The barbell: large-cap AI for compounding, small on-chain market caps for multiples on a small stack.

Why It Matters: It is the clearest statement in the episode of how the two halves of the show connect — he says outright they are "probably kind of the same trade," expressed at opposite ends of the liquidity spectrum.

Stated Risk: Self-described — the large caps will not produce the multiple he needs quickly, which is why the small-cap sleeve exists rather than replacing it.

🟧 Quotrons — The Biggest On-Chain Bag

A one-way burn token that converts into an NFT which then earns tokenized equity from trading fees.

What It Covers: Phase one is the burn mechanic — liquid token to NFT, irreversibly, after which the NFT receives fees paid in tokenized stock 05:14. Phase two is bridging to Ink and creating tokenized-equity pools there, which is where gmoney expects fee generation to come from going forward (38:02, 38:07). He calls it his biggest on-chain position and cites roughly a $10mn market cap as the convexity argument (37:49, 39:11). He also flags, as intrigue rather than analysis, that Kraken's co-CEO has interacted with the project's account 38:22.

Why It Matters: The purest expression of the tokenized-equity thesis: not owning tokenized stock, but owning what distributes it. Kraken's xStocks are live on Ink, which makes the phase-two claim mechanically possible.

Stated Risk: Stated on tape: small market cap, "the risk isn't that terrible if you're wrong" 39:13. The unstated risk is that the payout is defined by the project as a promotional reward rather than a dividend, which is a different claim on future cash flows than the on-air description implies.

🟫 Fake World Assets (FWA) — Be The House

An on-chain gacha protocol that lets NFT holders earn yield on assets that had none.

What It Covers: Deposit NFTs into the randomised pool and earn from spins, or pay to spin and win one; CryptoPunks and Bored Apes sit in the pool (28:06, 28:16). gmoney's frame is that it becomes a liquidity sink for all gacha mechanics — the way a deep prediction-market book becomes the buyer of last resort — so physical-card platforms could lay off risk into it (28:41, 28:51). He says it hit top-three revenue generation on Ethereum for three or four consecutive days, at a roughly $12mn market cap (29:48, 30:18).

Why It Matters: It monetises the exact problem Felman describes with his Ether Rock: capital locked in collectibles that produce nothing. It also fits gmoney's casual-gaming segmentation — memecoins demand constant attention, gacha does not.

Stated Risk: He names execution directly: the protocol layer is built, and everything now depends on top-of-funnel distribution driving daily spins 31:32. Absent from the on-air account is the token's early-August collapse to an all-time low and the subsequent increase in fee-funded buybacks.

🟨 Gold and Bitcoin — The Intervention Trade

The assets both men read as already pricing the policy reflex they expect.

What It Covers: gmoney's claim is diagnostic rather than directional: gold and Bitcoin bid because of the yen intervention and the Treasury's long-end buying, not despite them — the market sniffed out what those actions imply (19:22, 19:56).

Why It Matters: It is the mechanism connecting the macro and crypto halves of the show, and it is the same argument Felman would make on the next episode in support of a hawkish Fed being bullish for hard assets. Bitcoin had risen roughly 24% in August and reclaimed $80,000 in the first week of September.

Stated Risk: None stated. This is offered as a read of the tape rather than a position with a level.

🟦 Spot Beta — QQQ, NASDAQ, Bitcoin

The only unambiguous recommendation either man makes, aimed at listeners rather than traders.

What It Covers: Felman's instruction is explicit: whatever you gamble, hold spot as well, so that a blow-up leaves you still invested in NASDAQ, Bitcoin and "the future" (34:00, 34:20). Cap the leveraged sleeve at 5–10% 36:03. The wedding anecdote is the argument — a portfolio held entirely inside one trading app, to which his advice was QQQ (35:41, 37:04).

Why It Matters: It is the practical counterweight to an episode otherwise full of $10mn market caps, and it is consistent with his national-security framing: the risk he is describing is non-participation, not volatility.

Stated Risk: Inverted — the risk he is managing is being out of the market, which he treats as the larger exposure.
