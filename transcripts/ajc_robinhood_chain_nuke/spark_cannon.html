3. The actual thesis is about the category, not either token. This is the strongest analytical passage in the interview and it is an argument about mispricing:

"Launchpads are one of the clearest examples of product-market fit that crypto has… they're doing a million dollars of revenue a day in like the DEXs of the bear market. 💡 Key Insight

his edge here is a sentiment-structure argument, not a valuation argument, and the distinction matters. He is not claiming launchpads are cheap on a DCF. He is claiming they are cheap because the marginal allocator is embarrassed by them — capital rotated toward "institutional" crypto narratives, leaving the highest-revenue businesses in the sector without a natural bid. That is a supply-of-capital thesis, and it has a testable expiry: it stops working the moment allocating to a launchpad becomes respectable. It is also, note, the same structural argument he made about Robinhood Chain — a neglected cohort being underserved. He runs one idea twice. 12:22–12:45 · 05:56

The one number he gives for how he valued PONS. "Pump.fun's FDV buyback multiple was like in the 10 to 15x range. When I took my position in PONS it was at like 2x. I think since then it's in like the 1 to 2x range" 13:10–13:23.

🌐 Consensus Check

the "buyback multiple" is a real and increasingly standard on-chain valuation metric, and his framing of it is orthodox. The metric is market cap (or FDV) divided by annualised buyback spend — effectively an inverted shareholder-yield ratio, borrowed wholesale from equity analysis. Applying it across launchpads was, by mid-2026, mainstream practice among on-chain research desks rather than a contrarian move. What is genuinely contrarian is the conclusion he draws: that a 5–7x relative discount to Pump.fun on the same metric was an entry rather than a warning about durability, at a moment when PONS had roughly six weeks of operating history against Pump.fun's two years. 🔁 The Horseshoe Theory: "Launch a Coin Day One or Never Launch a Coin at All"

"I'm like arriving to this horseshoe theory of launching coins, and it's like either you launch a coin the day you launch your product or you never launch a coin at all." 13:53–14:00

This is the interview's one coined concept and it deserves its own treatment, because it inverts what was consensus twelve months earlier.

The old rule he is overturning. "Previously people thought like, oh, you don't want to launch a coin for your launchpad right away because that puts an implicit ceiling on the coins" 14:05–14:11. The logic: a platform token competes with the assets on the platform for buyer attention and caps them.

Why it broke. "Because they've gotten beat over the head so many times with just shitty tokens that [screw] you over, if they can't see right away that there's alignment around some sort of token — that they know they're not going to get rugged on — they have no incentive to use your platform" 14:15–14:28. Retail's default assumption flipped from trust until proven otherwise to assume extraction unless the alignment is visible on day one.

The PONS case, and the detail most people get wrong. "PONS came out — actually, they weren't even the ones that launched the token. 💡 Key Insight

the alignment that made PONS work was not designed by the team, and that is the strangest fact in the file. The token was community-launched and CTO'd (community takeover); the operators then adopted it and routed 80% of revenue to buying it back. That means the horseshoe theory as stated ("launch day one or never") is descriptively wrong about its own best case — PONS did neither. 🧬 Stock-Paired Memecoins: The Real Thesis Is Plumbing, Not Narrative

"The power of AMM-based pools is that you can connect the value of two assets together." 18:45–18:49

This is the section a reader should actually take away, and it starts with a story about bottled water.

The origin of the idea — the Saratoga observation. "You remember the morning routine guy with the Saratoga water? A memecoin dropped from him and ran to like 20 million. But there was also like the Saratoga stock — it went up like 100%. But these two assets had no connection in the value between one another" 18:55–19:13. The fix: "if you have viral moments like that, now you could have launched his memecoin also paired to the stock. Those two assets are now directly tied to each other" 19:13–19:22.

The generalisation. "Let's pair memecoins with what makes sense to pair them to — what's actually the value driver. It doesn't make sense if you're launching an AI-related memecoin to pair it with something like ETH. ETH ain't going up because AI is going crazy. You're going to want to pair it to Nvidia — or I think Stock Fund also allows you to pair to pre-IPO shares like Anthropic or OpenAI" 19:30–19:53. And the framing sentence: "we can actually finally tie these cultural assets more closely to the asset that they're a proxy for" 19:57–20:05.

Why he thinks it raises ceilings. "It's no longer just pairing against SOL or ETH. It's you're going to pair it against the asset it makes the most sense to pair against" 20:16–20:22, and the demand argument: "look at how many people are trading SOL-based memecoins — like tons. They should start trading this, and then maybe the ceilings for these do go higher because now there's finally value accrual" 20:24–20:36.

💡 Key Insight

his bull case is not that memecoins get better; it is that they acquire a floor formed by someone else's balance sheet. A SOL-paired memecoin's downside is bounded by nothing. A HIMS-paired memecoin's downside is bounded, in a vacuum, by the equity of a real company with revenue, filings and a NYSE listing — because the pool's value is the pool's contents. That is the first structural argument in this archive for why a memecoin might be worth more than an equivalent one on Solana, and it is an argument about collateral quality, not about culture, community or attention. Whether the floor survives contact with panic selling is the question he answers honestly twelve minutes later at 25:00–25:13: it does not, mechanically, hold in both directions. 19:57 · 20:24

📜 Analog

this is a tracking-basket structure rebuilt out of AMM parts, and the closest precedent is the ETF creation/redemption mechanism. An authorised participant arbitrages an ETF back to NAV by creating or redeeming baskets; here, a whitelisted market maker mints or redeems the tokenised share when the on-chain price diverges from the exchange price 23:01–23:12. The mechanism is genuinely the same. The differences are the ones that generate all the risk in this file: the arb is capacity-constrained (thin on-chain float), schedule-constrained (US market hours only), and discretionary (a market maker may simply not show up). AJC names all three without naming the analogy.

🔍 Source Verified

⚙️ The Mechanism, Explained Properly: What Actually Happens When You Buy BONER With HIMS

threadguy: "I know we're not supposed to say this part out loud, but… what is actually happening when you buy like BONERcoin with HIMS stock? What is the mechanism?" 21:32–21:47

The clearest four minutes on the tape. AJC's walk-through, step by step and in his order:

  1. Stock tokens are permissioned at the edge. "Robinhood, they have official stock tokens, and to get those on chain it has to be like a whitelisted market maker or whatnot. I don't know all the ones off the top of my head. One of them I do know is like Rialto, which is like a prop AMM on Robinhood Chain" 21:48–22:04.
  2. The buy routes through the equity. "If someone buys BONER — let's say you buy a hundred bucks of BONER — you're also indirectly buying a hundred bucks of HIMS stock" 22:05–22:11.
  3. The full path. "Let's say you start in USDG, you buy $100 of BONER. What's actually happening is the $100 gets swapped for HIMS stock. That HIMS stock gets swapped into the BONER/HIMS liquidity pool, and then you're receiving a $100 equivalent worth of BONER coins" 22:13–22:31.
  4. The correction to the obvious reading. threadguy summarises it as "you're selling HIMS to buy BONER" 22:32–22:36; AJC corrects: "you're selling HIMS but like not really, because you're actually depositing HIMS" 22:38–22:44. The stock does not leave the system — it accumulates in the pool.
  5. The consequence, which is the whole thesis. "Pairing these coins is actually bringing supply on chain. I think like yesterday there was a 30% uptick in the value of onchain RWA on Robinhood Chain. So this is definitely working" 23:16–23:30.
  6. How the supply gets there. "The incentive for new supply to come on chain is basically just these arbs… in theory, one of these memecoins will push up the price like 0.5% above what its market [price] is, and brings it on" 23:01–23:14. The failure case he cites as precedent: "you saw this with CINEMA and AMC, where there's only like 40k of liquidity for AMC on chain and the price massively depegged" 22:47–22:57.

Then the correlation mechanics, which threadguy pushes him on properly — "why would HIMS getting cut in half 50% in a 15-minute candle make BONERcoin go down?" 24:13–24:19:

"In AMMs, the value of the one asset — call it BONER — is directly tied to the value of the pool. So if the value of the pool increases by 10%, if you assume there's no buys or sells, the value of the other asset also went up 10%. This is because of how AMMs [work] — simple x·y [= k]." 24:20–24:47

And the immediate, unprompted limitation:

"Obviously the issue is the buying and selling changes the ratio. So if HIMS goes up 50% but people for whatever reason panic BONERcoin and sell the [—] out of it, it might not necessarily go up 50%. But if you just assume there's no buys or sells, or it nets out — yes… in a vacuum it's one-to-one correlated." 24:52–25:16
💡 Key Insight

"one-to-one correlated in a vacuum" is doing enormous work, and it fails asymmetrically in exactly the scenario people will buy this for. The pairing transmits the stock's move to the memecoin only when memecoin flow is neutral. But the entire reason to buy a stock-paired memecoin is to express a view on a catalyst — earnings, a product launch, a squeeze — and a catalyst is precisely the moment memecoin flow is not neutral. So the correlation is strongest when it is least useful and weakest when it matters most. AJC states the caveat clearly and then returns to the bullish framing; the asymmetry is never named. This is the single most important qualification in the interview. 24:52–25:16

⚡ Tension

the same pairing is described as a value floor at 20:24 and as a depeg engine at 22:47, and the second description is the more recent evidence. At 20:24 the pairing raises ceilings via "value accrual". At 22:48 the identical structure produced a massive depeg in AMC because on-chain float was ~$40k. Both are consequences of the same design: a thin float turns a paired memecoin into a leveraged claim on scarce collateral, which raises the price and breaks the link to the underlying. He is not contradicting himself — he is describing a mechanism whose benefit and whose defect are the same property. Neither man says that out loud.

🎯 The Bounded-Downside Category Bet — His Actual Repeatable Method

Form a valuation prior for a category before the market gives you a price, then wait for an exogenous shock to hand you the leader at a fraction of it — sized so the loss is a shrug.

What It Includes

A category-level target ("the top launchpad on Robinhood Chain is worth like nine figures" 03:00), a leadership screen ("saw that PONS had the most traction outside of NOXA" 02:27), an exogenous shock unrelated to the asset's fundamentals (Armstrong's avatar 02:40), and a maximum loss stated in dollars ("So what? The Practice (Step-by-Step):

  1. Pick a category with demonstrated revenue, not a narrative. His filter: "one of the clearest examples of product-market fit that crypto has" 12:02.
  2. Write the category's terminal value down before you have a price. Nine figures, in his case, held as a prior.
  3. Rank the field by traction, not by chart. Second place behind an exiting incumbent is the buy.
  4. Wait for a price dislocation whose cause is unrelated to the asset. An 80% one-day move caused by a rotation event, not by the platform breaking.
  5. Size so the full loss is emotionally free. He names an absolute dollar figure, not a percentage of book.
  6. Have no exit plan — and know that is a defect, not a feature 21:08.

The Evidence

🔁 Horseshoe Theory of Token Launches

Launch your token on day one or never launch one at all — because retail now treats the absence of visible revenue alignment as evidence of intent to extract.

🧬 Value-Driver Pairing

Pair a memecoin to the asset it is actually a proxy for, not to the chain's base asset — because in an AMM, the pool's contents are the coin's value.

What It Includes

The rejection of default pairs ("it doesn't make sense if you're launching an AI-related memecoin to pair it with something like ETH. ETH ain't going up because AI is going crazy" 19:40), the substitution rule (pair to NVDA instead), and the extension to pre-IPO exposure via Stock Fund (Anthropic, OpenAI named) 19:46–19:53.

The Mechanism

In an x·y=k pool, "if the value of the pool increases by 10%, if you assume there's no buys or sells, the value of the other asset also went up 10%" 24:30–24:42.

The Evidence

By late August 2026 the chain's highest-volume memecoin pools were all stock-paired — amc/CINEMA ($5.7M/24h), TSLA/SHRUB ($3.8M), SPY/BUDDY ($3.7M), SAYLORMOON/MSTR ($2.8M). The category, per AJC, "literally just took off for the first time like last week" 29:53.

The Hard Limit

🕐 The Market-Hours Rule for Tokenised Equities

The peg on a tokenised stock is held by a discretionary, capacity-limited arbitrage that is switched off outside US market hours — so never hold a paired position over a weekend.
