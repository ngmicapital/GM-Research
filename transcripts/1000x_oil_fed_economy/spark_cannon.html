🛢️ The Headline Blamed Crypto; The Hosts Read It As The Adoption Story

The episode opens on a Financial Times investigation published the previous day.

"Do you understand how bullish that is for crypto? Forget the headline." 06:40

Felman's summary on tape: young traders, backed by a Polish state-sponsored organisation, were sent into Venezuela to negotiate crude directly with what he calls the "free-for-all producers that currently exist" after the collapse of the old order 03:29. They found brokers claiming access to Venezuelan barrels, sometimes below market. They paid in USDT delivered on USB sticks. The oil never arrived.

His read of the FT's framing is adversarial: the title, he says, made "crypto the reason that they lost $230 million" when the actual failure was diligence 05:06. He grants the piece is "well written and reasonably well researched" — the objection is to the headline's causal arrow, not the reporting 05:34. The point he thinks the industry under-celebrated:

"Governments are now engaging with crypto on a level that is being exposed that we've never seen before." 06:33

They were not wiring through banks or moving cash; a sovereign-linked entity authorised settlement in Tether for a physical commodity 06:16. Felman's extrapolation runs a decade out — everything transacts on chain, everybody uses stablecoins, and the money accrues to their producers and users 07:12.

🔍 Source Verified✅ The Financial Times published "How Poland lost $230mn trying to buy Venezuelan oil with crypto" on 15 September 2026 — the day before this taping. The counterparties are more specific than the on-air summary: Orlen Trading Switzerland (OTS), the Swiss trading arm of Polish state-controlled Orlen, contracted on 29 November 2023 with Dubai-based Hannon International — founded by then-25-year-old Kam Ho "Alex" Tse — for roughly 6 million barrels of Merey 16 crude at about $345mn. OTS wired the $230mn advance within five days; between January and March 2024, traders hand-delivered USB drives to Caracas intermediaries, and roughly $132mn moved in crypto. Only 500,000 barrels of fuel oil were ever delivered; three chartered supertankers sat off the José export terminal and loaded nothing. Poland puts the total loss, including freight and legal costs, at about 1.6bn złoty (~$424mn), and Orlen is pursuing $230mn in arbitration.

What most complicates the on-air framing is chronology rather than fact: the transaction dates from late 2023 and early 2024, inside the temporary US sanctions-relief window that opened that October — not from the post-Maduro reshuffle the hosts are otherwise discussing.

🌐 Consensus CheckThe stablecoin leg was not a Polish idea. PdVSA moved a large share of its spot cargo sales onto a model requiring USDT prepayment during 2024, to keep receipts out of bank accounts exposed to US sanctions; by that March, digital wallets were a standard requirement for spot buyers. Tether has since frozen dozens of wallets tied to Venezuelan sanctions evasion. The counterparty demanded the rails; the buyer's failure was in who it paid, not what it paid with. 06:16

Felman concedes one crypto-specific hazard: hand a 28-year-old a stick holding $50mn of USDT and an unethical person finds a way to skim 17:28. He is careful that this is incentive design rather than allegation, but treats the absence of the question as the reporter's gap 17:59.

💡 Key InsightThe hosts are not disagreeing about facts here; they are disagreeing about what a single data point licenses. Felman treats one exposed deal as proof of a distribution — "you know that there are hundred of these things happening, it's not just one" 18:32. Van Bourg treats it as an outlier generated by inexperience. Both can be right: sovereign-adjacent stablecoin settlement is real and growing, and this execution was, in Van Bourg's phrase, sketchy brokerville. 18:16

⚖️ Oil's Minimum Ticket Size Is Why The Deal Was Never Real

Van Bourg's contribution is mechanical, and it is the strongest argument on the tape.

"If it's literally meet me on the street corner and give me a thumb drive — no. It's beyond that." 12:18

He starts from experience. Seven years at Vitol running a derivatives pod — securitising cargoes, turning them into options, selling and re-buying them — made him a side door rather than the front door for physical enquiries 07:51. At least monthly for that whole tenure, somebody introduced a deal of exactly the Poland shape: a regional demand centre in Brazil needs gasoline, I have a contact, can you supply a long-range cargo into this port 08:21. He passed them to the gatekeepers — the heads of the gasoline and distillates desks — with an explicit non-endorsement 08:53. His strike rate: zero 09:11.

The reason is a property of the commodity, not of the people.

"If you want to play at scale in these markets… the minimum ticket size is tens of millions usually, if not larger." 09:36

Oil is not infinitely divisible the way crypto is, where the minimum unit is a satoshi 09:21. A Suezmax costs around $100mn; the cargo inside an ultra-large crude carrier runs to roughly $200mn at current prices 09:48. That size puts physical brokerage below the floor of the market: "physical commodities brokerage in oil and gas and products is not a thing" 10:04. Everything from a Venezuelan concession to a regional supply-and-offtake deal is handled by the giants.

He carves out a genuine exception, and it is where the grey-market question actually lives. Regional brokers shifting very large deals do exist — he knows several personally, they live in Monaco, and they are usually billionaires 10:33. Where he met them was structurally specific: the Kurdistan business, where gasoline went to Kurdish forces fighting ISIS and captured crude came back; and the post-invasion Russian flow 10:48. The generalisation: you find these people around the edges, and the edges are made of sanctions 11:05.

Which is precisely the argument against the Poland deal. Venezuela is no longer outside the sanctions perimeter — Van Bourg calls it "basically a client state of the United States," with client oil reserves and the international trading houses back in the room 19:14. If the sanctions arbitrage is gone, the reason a back-channel broker would exist goes with it.

💡 Key InsightVan Bourg's taxonomy of who receives a thumb drive inverts the intuition. The people who can credibly demand crypto on a street corner are not the ones who control the oil — they are the ones who can interrupt it: attack the pipeline to the loading terminal, or harm your local representative next time he shows up. Paying fees in USDT to an oil minister's nephew, he says, is "totally standard operating procedure." Paying for the barrels that way is not, because at the bottom of the structure there is always a legitimate institution with a bank account. 13:38–14:19

Felman presses the obvious follow-up — how did a government organisation not understand this? — and Van Bourg's answer is that governments get scammed constantly, and oil is an excellent venue because almost nobody has seen the physical chain from ground to refinery (12:24, 14:45). Felman supplies the control case: a former boss at Merrill Lynch in the early 2000s wired over $100mn for three Indonesian coal mines that turned out not to exist 15:44. No crypto involved.

🔗 ConnectionIndonesian coal mines (2000s, wire transfer) → Venezuelan crude (2023–24, USDT) — the constant is unverified physical assets at the far end of a payment, not the instrument. Van Bourg's concession is exactly this: it happens over SWIFT as readily as over Tether, and the modern rails probably just "felt a little cooler." 19:55

🌎 Venezuela Is Balkanising — And The Hosts Split On Who Gets To Play

This is the one place on the tape where the hosts genuinely disagree, and the disagreement is about access rather than direction.

"I don't think that your average entrepreneurial 24-year-old should fly to Venezuela and try to get involved." 23:12

Felman's case: when a government falls the economy fractures, and fracture is where opportunity sits. He knows someone flying down monthly who has closed deals 11:36, and frames a "new oil barons of Venezuela" story — private citizens buying into fields because the majors are too nervous to move 20:33.

The nervousness has a named cause on tape. Felman describes a Trump post from a few weeks earlier condemning Exxon, Shell, BP and others for not investing anywhere near enough in rebuilding Venezuelan oil infrastructure 20:47. His explanation is political-risk pricing: if the Democrats return, companies that moved early could be sanctioned or have investments stripped as a punitive measure — and an individual is far less likely to be targeted than a listed major 21:04. Hence the pivot toward individuals financing these deals.

🌐 Consensus CheckThe individual-investor channel is documented and further along than the episode implies. Coinbase co-founder Fred Ehrsam — named by Felman at 11:24 — is reported to be seeking control of at least three Venezuelan oil fields, the Boca, Guico and Guara blocks in the Orinoco Belt, through a vehicle he co-founded called Primavera, and has travelled to Caracas repeatedly since May. The blocks sit under contracts US officials are considering revoking, and Energy Secretary Chris Wright was due in Caracas to showcase as many as 17 oil and gas agreements. 20:33

Van Bourg's rebuttal does not deny the balkanisation; it denies the entry point. There is no room for the solo entrepreneur in a big-ticket market like crude unless you are somebody's nephew inside a sanctions regime with the ability to mix or launder barrels 22:45. Ehrsam is a billionaire and can do it legitimately; a 24-year-old cannot 23:06. His advice: do not try unless you have sat inside BP or Vitol for five to ten years, built the connections, and found a spot those firms cannot occupy but you can — which in practice means renouncing an American passport and living outside the global sanctions regime 23:37.

Then he redirects, which is more useful than saying no. The right frontier is a commodity with a smaller ticket. He points at a friend from his Lehman analyst class who became a rare-earths and minor-metals trader — cobalt, lithium, the names that go into batteries and chips — flying to the Congo roughly monthly 26:01. You do not need $100mn to lift a shipment of those. Felman adds his own variants — an accounting firm to buy or build, Venezuelan corporate debt where you might have edge by physically checking whether the issuer's offices exist (24:46, 25:38).

💡 Key InsightThe disagreement resolves into a rule that generalises well beyond Venezuela: opportunity for an individual exists where the minimum ticket size sits below institutional attention and above local capability. Crude fails that test in both directions; minor metals pass it. It is the same reasoning that makes the sanctions-edge brokers real — they occupy a band the majors are forbidden to enter. 26:34

Felman's closing frame carries into the macro half: this is a period "where decades happen in weeks," and the edge is in reading which trends you can profit from 24:57.

📉 The Long End Is Repricing Against AI Profits, Not Just Supply

The macro turn begins with Van Bourg sharing a chart of the 20-year total return index 27:46.

"Traders and investors are requiring a higher rate of return for holding treasuries." 28:55

Felman's description of the tape: the 20-year sits at yields last seen in 2008, pushing mortgage rates up, and yet retail sales are "crushing it" at the same time (27:46, 28:03). He treats that combination as the interesting fact rather than a contradiction, and explains it through forward equity returns. If AI and the productivity gains attached to it push corporate profitability projections sharply higher, the compensation required to sit in a government bond instead has to rise to compete 28:26. Layer on heavy corporate issuance and a Treasury intervening in its own market, and you get a long end that sells off into strong data rather than rallying on it.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Both halves of the observation land. August retail sales, released the morning of this taping, rose 1.2% month over month against a consensus of about 0.7% — the strongest gain in five months; the control group used in GDP calculations rose 1.4% against roughly 0.5% expected, and receipts were 6% higher year over year. On the bond side, the 30-year Treasury had hit a 19-year high in the preceding weeks, and Bessent responded by more than doubling debt buyback operations to at least $4bn each from 9 September, saying afterwards they could go larger; officials indicated the near-$1tn Treasury General Account could fund them. That is the intervention Felman refers to.

The dollar side of the argument arrives through two blocs the hosts say were not on their bingo card. Canada first: Felman notes business tax cuts and alignment with the EU, treats it as a genuine surprise given how anti-competitive both had looked, and is explicit that he will not take the trade — "it's not a trade that I'm going to take because I'm not a forex trader" — but says that if the path holds for a year, CAD versus USD is in a good spot (29:03, 30:52). Then Europe: a Mario Draghi article in the Financial Times arguing Europe must invest in data centres gave him "a sliver of hope for the European Union" 29:52. The substance he takes: the EU holds around 5% of capacity and cannot be paralysed by the overbuilding argument hamstringing the US debate, because Europe has not built anything to overbuild 30:05.

🌐 Consensus CheckDraghi's op-ed, "Europe's difficult choices on AI," ran in the FT on 11 September 2026, and the figure is compute rather than floor space: the EU hosts under 5% of the world's AI compute capacity against about 75% for the United States. He put Europe's gap between demand and installed supply at roughly 3GW — a quarter of existing capacity — widening to 14GW by 2030, citing ECB scenarios in which fast adoption adds 0.3–0.4 percentage points a year to total factor productivity growth that has been roughly zero since 2022. One correction of role rather than substance: Draghi is the former ECB president (2011–2019) and former Italian prime minister; Christine Lagarde holds the post now. 29:52
🌐 Consensus CheckThe Canadian move was two days old at taping. At the Canada Investment Summit on 15 September 2026, Prime Minister Mark Carney announced a "Productivity Mega Deduction" allowing full write-off of eligible new investment — fibre, mining property, pipelines, software, R&D, aircraft, patents, rail and roads. Ottawa puts the resulting tax rate on new business investment at 13%, some 4.5 points below the US and roughly half the G7 average, and Carney was simultaneously courting an EU partnership as new US tariffs hit Canadian goods. 29:07
🔗 ConnectionDraghi's data-centre argument → the copper thesis in the next segment. If Europe closes even part of a 14GW gap while the US keeps building, the binding constraint moves from capital to physical inputs — and the wiring is the input with no substitute. 30:05

🔩 Copper Is The AI Trade Nobody Is Calling A Memecoin

Van Bourg introduces copper while Felman's connection drops, and it is the clearest expression of conviction on the tape.

"…connects all of these thinking sand neuron things is copper. And there's just not enough of it." 32:58

The setup: what data centres are most short of is copper, it is the AI-adjacent asset to own, and it goes parabolic for the next couple of years 31:19. He says on tape that he is "a little bit positioned" and feels short of where he would like to be, and that the call came from a colleague who worked the supply and demand and simply said own it (31:58, 32:04).

The move that lands is comparing it to the thing his audience actually trades: copper "is literally going to be the next meme coin," except nobody talks about it the way the trenches get talked about, and you can ride it with leverage for five years (32:17, 32:26). The chip framing before it compresses the whole argument — you make sand think, which is what happens inside TSMC, and the wiring between the thinking sand is copper 32:39.

🌐 Consensus CheckThe scarcity claim runs with the consensus, not against it. Copper set records in 2026, printing $14,527/tonne on the LME in January and roughly $6.90/lb on COMEX by August, and forecasters put the refined market in structural deficit — from about 150,000 tonnes to J.P. Morgan's 330,000 and Morgan Stanley's 600,000. AI facilities alone are projected to absorb roughly 400,000 tonnes a year over the next decade, peaking near 572,000 in 2028; Citi has modelled $15,000/tonne. The trade is consensus among commodity desks — Van Bourg's point is that it is not consensus among crypto traders. 32:17

Felman's amendment is the practitioner's one, and the hosts debate it properly. His rule: with anything that can be produced, buy the producers rather than the underlying, because producers can raise output and capture the move — so COPX, the copper miners ETF 33:17. Van Bourg pushes back that this time you might want the commodity itself 33:38. Felman narrows instead: a basket rather than a single name, because you do not want idiosyncratic risk on one miner whose mine can be taken offline 34:05. The single name mentioned is Freeport-McMoRan 34:00.

💡 Key InsightFelman's stated rule cuts against the thesis. Producers outperform, he argues, because they can lift supply — but the entire bull case for copper is that supply cannot respond. Van Bourg's "this time you might want to buy the commodity" is pointing at exactly that seam. 33:38–34:05

🤖 Doomerism As A Sales Playbook — And Why The Meta Refusal Matters

The AI segment is the longest stretch on the tape and the most confidently argued. It begins from a market observation: chips sold off after the leading labs jointly called for restraint, because if model capability is deliberately paced, forward compute demand is lower than the price implies 35:33.

"That sales pitch to sell their companies for $2 trillion to people is: y'all going to die." 43:03

Felman's account of the week: Sam Altman and Dario Amodei came out "effectively holding hands in a way that they've never held hands before," saying AI must not be allowed to kill the world 35:16. Zuckerberg then pushed back against pacing the frontier — throwing, in Felman's framing, a grenade into the coordination by asserting that companies can regulate themselves and do not need government involved (35:02, 35:54). The structural claim is a competition one: if OpenAI and Anthropic pace while Meta does not, pacing is unenforceable, because keeping up becomes mandatory. "The monopoly is broken" (36:10, 36:29).

🔍 Source Verified✅ The sequence is real and tighter than the on-air version. Dario Amodei published "We Must Pace the Frontier" on 12 September 2026, arguing capabilities are advancing faster than labs can reliably understand, monitor and control, and committing Anthropic to give third-party evaluators including METR permanent employee-level system access. Sam Altman posted agreement — "I agree with Dario that we need to pace the frontier" — and matched the pledge for OpenAI; Musk and Demis Hassabis also endorsed. Zuckerberg rejected a coordinated slowdown on 15 September, the day before this taping, arguing competition, liability and independent evaluation already give labs sufficient reason to train safely. The title Felman recalls — "The Future is for Everyone" — belongs to Zuckerberg's manifesto of 10 August 2026, in which Meta opened the weights of a model called Muse Glimmer; the mid-September intervention was separate.

Felman's enthusiasm for Meta's assistant — he uses Muse and calls it "exceptionally good" 34:46 — feeds a bull case built on management rather than product: Zuckerberg as a phenomenal CEO despite the metaverse detour, reaching for a Roman framing of the future as something to expand into rather than defend against (36:47, 37:03). Against that he places Altman and Amodei as coming "from this culture of fear" 37:23.

His argument against near-term catastrophe has two legs: kill switches, since the data centres can be turned off (37:36, 39:29); and, more persuasively, adoption lag — even if AGI arrived tomorrow it would take years to penetrate the economy deeply enough to do serious damage 37:45. His empirical test is state actors: Russia and Iran have frontier models, Iran was "quite literally using Claude," and no large-scale cyber attacks have materialised, so the binding constraint is competence, not capability (38:07, 38:34). What he expects instead is the internet analogy — AI ends up in everything, so cutting undersea cables or jamming Wi-Fi becomes the real downside scenario, not autonomous machine agency (38:53, 39:04).

Van Bourg agrees and supplies a different mechanism. Doomerism, he says, is an op — the second-oldest trick in the broker's book. The oldest is last-look scarcity: somebody else is willing to pay better, act now or miss out, which works every time and which he admits using himself 40:31. The second is the Louisiana swamp shaman:

"You're going to die. YOU'RE GOING TO DIE NOW unless you buy this little voodoo doll and shaman service from me." 41:01

Applied: "only we have the antidote because we created the disease" 41:22, and then you buy cyber security from the people who midwifed the deity 43:23.

The commercial logic underneath is worth taking seriously. Open-source tokens price between 1% and 10% of frontier tokens, and we are rapidly approaching the point where a trailing-edge open model on a Mac Studio delivers more than enough intelligence for even fairly intensive coding needs (41:49, 42:21). In that world the frontier labs are not worth $2tn apiece — maybe $100bn — and as that point approaches, "these guys need a sales pitch" (42:37, 42:56).

🎯 Against ConsensusThe mainstream reading of September's pacing letters is a safety coalition forming under competitive pressure. Van Bourg's is the inverse: the coalition forms because of commoditisation, and safety framing defends a valuation against open-weight substitution. He offers no evidence of intent — it is a motive argument and he presents it as one — but it is falsifiable: if open-weight capability keeps closing the gap and frontier pricing holds, the premise fails. 42:56
📜 AnalogVan Bourg's frame for why the economy is holding up is shale, not AI policy. "AI is just like shale. Shale was the greatest deflationary force of our time" — the reason the economy is ripping rather than crashing, and the thing that kept the US from being hostage to a small number of oil producers. The claim he is making is about the deflation of a unit of output: a barrel then, a unit of intelligence now. 43:53

💥 The Next Crisis Won't Be A Credit Crisis

The two hosts converge on a specific negative claim, and it is the most load-bearing macro view on the tape.

"Credit crises have been solved by infinite money printing, and that genie is out of the bottle and will never go back in." 49:05

Felman's reasoning: every collapse in his lifetime has been credit-related, and credit crises are now met with money printing and government backstops, so the mechanism that produced 1931, 2008 and February 2020 has been disarmed (49:02, 49:19). The consequence is not that crises stop; it is that the next one must come through a different channel.

Van Bourg's candidate is software: an eventual migration of data, infrastructure and commerce off deterministic rails onto probabilistic ones, then a failure mode in which probabilistic software does the functional equivalent of cutting undersea cables (50:01, 50:14). He discounts the kinetic path — wars have not produced financial crises in recent decades, because the ones that would are civilisation-ending 49:47. His timeline: roughly two years before superintelligence starts generating hard-to-predict problems, and in the interval the deflation in the price of intelligence creates enough value that the trade is to be fully invested, with leverage sized so dips can be bought (44:24, 44:33).

⚡ TensionThis is the same speaker who, minutes earlier, called the labs' catastrophe warnings a voodoo sales pitch. He is not contradicting himself on the facts — his claim is that a real tail risk two years out is being marketed as an imminent one — but the distance between "they are selling you a disease they invented" and "I expect an AI-driven financial crisis around 2028" is narrower than the delivery suggests, and neither host tests it. (44:24 · 41:22)

One plain error, noted once. Felman cites "Origins of the Crash by Lowenstein" as the book covering the 1997–98 Long-Term Capital Management blow-up and the Asian debt crisis 48:36. Roger Lowenstein wrote both books, but the LTCM history is When Genius Failed (2000); Origins of the Crash (2004) covers the late-1990s equity bubble and the corporate scandals that followed. The point he draws from it — the anatomy of a collapse repeats but the trigger does not — survives intact.

🏛️ Hiking Into A Fiscal Fight: The Contrarian Case For A Hawkish Fed

With eight minutes to the announcement, Felman reverses the call he had made on previous episodes.

"This is a total nothing burger, but it just reinvigorates the idea that the Fed is being irresponsible." 61:07

The reversal is stated plainly: he had said they probably were not going to hike, the data came in extremely strong, and he now expects Warsh to move, with the market pricing roughly a 90% chance (50:36, 51:01). He states the conventional mechanism first — assets that produce no cash flow fall when rates rise, because treasuries become a more attractive place for money 51:09 — then argues that this time the signal dominates the mechanism. A Warsh hike says the government is acting irresponsibly, that Treasury interventions will distort markets, and that he intends to rein it in 51:24. Then the key step: Warsh loses, because he has far less political capital than Bessent and the Treasury, so what follows is more government irresponsibility into the midterms (51:35, 51:44). His evidence for that direction is the $5,000-per-American proposal, with a trillion-dollar price tag, which Bessent had said would not affect the deficit 51:48.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Trump pledged a $5,000 "dividend" to every adult citizen conditional on Republicans holding Congress in the 2026 midterms; estimated cost roughly $1.2tn, against about $195bn of customs duties collected in fiscal 2025. Bessent said on 15 September 2026 that it could be done without increasing the deficit — "there are ways to do it that would not affect the deficit" — without specifying offsets, and defended the plan before Congress on 16 September, the day of this taping. Any payment would require congressional approval.

The asymmetry is the useful part, because it is falsifiable: a hike plus a hawkish presser is bullish, "very very very bullish," because it sets up a Fed–Treasury fight the Treasury wins (61:26, 61:34), while a dovish, acquiescent Warsh would be the bearish outcome 52:33. The presser, not the decision, carries the information 61:21.

Van Bourg does not let it pass:

🎯 Against Consensus"That's a contrarian take. Higher interest rates should be pretty bearish. Like I remember telling myself this in 2021 — oh, whatever, it's just a head fake, it's bullish. And it wasn't bullish." This is the single most valuable thirty seconds on the tape: one host naming the exact reasoning error he personally made in the last cycle, in real time, against his co-host's live position. Felman's response is assertion rather than argument — "I'm telling you now, that's not going to be the case" — and gold is "a screaming buy here." 61:41–61:55

The print lands mid-conversation. Felman reads it out: the Fed hiked exactly what was expected, 25bp (59:59, 60:02). Neither Bitcoin nor Zcash reacts meaningfully, and his immediate advice is to do nothing — wait it out, make no decisions 60:11. 🔍 Source Verified✅ Both events resolve as described. 💡 Key InsightThe priced-in framework is internally consistent, and it is also why Felman's own thesis cannot be tested today. If a 90%-priced hike moves nothing, the hike cannot be the catalyst for the gold and Bitcoin repricing he expects — that has to be the subsequent fiscal behaviour he predicts, which unfolds over months, not minutes. He is making a midterm-horizon call while framing it as an FOMC-day call. 60:36

The chart talk is where the concrete levels live. Bitcoin broke prior support at 76,500 the day before; Felman is not advocating shorting but could see reducing risk, then inverts to a long — stop below 74,800, target the highs, with a reclaim of 76,000 as confirmation (59:35, 62:18, 63:01). 🎮 Back In The Trenches: Why A Runescape Memecoin Is On A Macro Show

The last substantive segment is the most personal, and the disclosure inside it matters more than the asset does.

Felman's account: Runescape's in-game economy, denominated in a currency called GP, was where he first got wealthy — a billionaire in-game at twelve while holding $2 in real life — and thousands of hours of trading inside it taught him supply and demand before any market did (54:24, 54:32). His claim that a meaningful number of crypto traders and hedge-fund people share that origin is what makes the segment more than nostalgia 54:53.

The asset is a memecoin called GP, surfaced by a reply to one of his tweets, pairing the Runescape meme with gold. The mechanic: a 3% tax on every buy and sell, redistributed to current holders, which he says has earned him around $5,000 (55:13, 55:22). His disclosures are unusually complete:

💡 Key InsightThe last disclosure is the one to weight. He says all of it unprompted — but enthusiasm and position are not separable here, and the 1KX disclaimer read at the end 64:12 covers exactly this case.
