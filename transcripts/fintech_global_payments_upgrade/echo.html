Fintech Won The Payments Upgrade And Blockchain Barely Showed Up — Tuckman's Scorecard, Two Years On

Source World Knowledge Forum · Date n/a · 36 min
Domain Payments & Financial Infrastructure ·
Reliability Data-backed — an academic market-structure lecture whose checkable figures hold up well against primary sources; the one substantive error is a terminology mistake about JPM Coin, and the whole talk predates the 2025 stablecoin regime it does not discuss
Content Type Academic Lecture (solo, slide-driven)
Full Mode

⚡ The Bottom Line

  • Tuckman's scorecard is blunt and mostly right: payment systems improved enormously over fifteen years, and fintech did it, not blockchain. Speed, convenience and access all moved — via UPI, Square, M-PESA and Wise. His verdict: "the only successful blockchain sortie there's been in large scale payment systems has been in cross-border payments." 01:06–01:24
  • The single most useful idea in the talk is that "real-time retail payments" is a marketing layer over batch-net settlement. UPI credits you instantly, but the money settles net, in batches, with the banking system carrying the interim risk — "world real-time retail payment is actually a lie." 15:40–16:20
  • Blockchain's design is natively real-time gross settlement, and Tuckman argues that is a liability, not an advantage. RTGS is liquidity-hungry: in his own worked example, nine payments that net to zero still require 30 units of reserves. Omnibus accounts and netting — the two things that made fintech cheap — are "quite alien" to the blockchain model. (05:56–07:07, 34:41–36:31)
  • One clear error: he calls JPM Coin a stablecoin "backed fully by US dollars." It is a deposit token. JPMorgan itself distinguishes the two — JPMD is a digital claim on an existing bank deposit, can be interest-bearing, and is not a 1:1 reserve-backed instrument. The distinction is the entire regulatory point.
  • The talk is dated to 2024 internally and therefore misses the regime change that answers its closing question. The GENIUS Act was signed on 18 July 2025; MiCA's stablecoin titles applied from 30 June 2024 and its service-provider title from 30 December 2024. Tuckman asks whether blockchain can grow on fintech's foundations; the statutory answer arrived a year after he asked.

🔑 Key Claims (Ranked)

1. Fintech, not blockchain, delivered the fifteen-year payments upgrade ↳ Evidence: Every large-scale improvement he documents — UPI, Square, M-PESA, Wise — is fintech built on conventional bank rails. Blockchain appears only in cross-border, via Ripple and JPM Coin. (01:06–01:24, 30:52–31:05) ↳ Caveat: The scorecard is drawn at 2024. Stablecoin settlement has since scaled substantially, and a US federal framework now exists. ↳ Consensus: Aligned — this is the mainstream central-banking and BIS reading of the period.

2. "Real-time retail payments" is real-time authorisation over batch-net settlement ↳ Evidence: In UPI the payee is credited immediately, "but it happens though that world real-time retail payment is actually a lie because the money is not actually going right from you to you — it's actually being settled net batch." The crediting bank carries the risk, which "has to be managed by the central bank." 15:40–16:08 ↳ Consensus: Aligned with payment-system economics, and almost never stated this plainly in public commentary.

3. Real-time gross settlement buys immediacy with liquidity, and the price is steep ↳ Evidence: His worked example — nine payments across three banks that net to exactly zero — still requires Bank A to hold 10 and Bank C to hold 20, for 30 units of reserves against 90 of payment value. 05:56–06:44 ↳ Caveat: RTGS also eliminates intraday credit accumulation, which is why every large-value interbank system uses it. ↳ Consensus: Aligned — standard settlement theory, well presented.

4. Fintech won by bundling, not by moving money faster ↳ Evidence (Square): hardware, payments processing, point-of-sale software, inventory management, customer contact, payroll, e-commerce integration, checking and savings accounts, a debit card, and lending. "It wasn't just sending money quickly from one person to another." (18:24–19:28, 34:41–35:27) ↳ Consensus: Aligned, and it is the talk's most transferable lesson.

5. Transaction data substitutes for collateral, and that is the real financial-inclusion unlock ↳ Evidence: Development economists identify absence of collateral as the core constraint; Square and M-PESA lend against observed revenue instead. His example is a market trader taking a loan in the morning and repaying from the day's sales. (19:28–20:00, 22:47–24:10) ↳ Consensus: Aligned with the microfinance and digital-credit literature; stated more crisply than most.

6. Kenya's access numbers are the strongest single evidence in the talk ↳ Evidence: Formal financial inclusion from 27% of the population in 2006 to 83% in 2019; complete exclusion from 41% to 11%. 24:51–25:14 ↳ Caveat: None on the numbers — they check out to the decimal against the Central Bank of Kenya's FinAccess survey (26.7% → 82.9%; 41.3% → 11.0%). ↳ Consensus: Aligned and verified.

7. Cross-border payments remain expensive and slow because every hop in the correspondent chain is paid ↳ Evidence: His cost distribution — only 37% of international transactions cost under 5%, 45% cost 5–10%, and 10% cost 10–15% — plus a JPMorgan quote that "a lot of time is spent just chasing around money that was supposed to be in place and isn't there." 27:32–28:00 ↳ Caveat: Verified for 2024. The World Bank's global average for sending $200 was 6.35% in Q1 2024 and 6.49% in Q4, with banks the most expensive channel at 13.40%. ↳ Consensus: Aligned — this is the G20 cross-border roadmap's own diagnosis.

8. Both blockchain cross-border examples abandon the peer-to-peer premise ↳ Evidence: Ripple's On-Demand Liquidity depends on market makers willing to quote both legs — "you're relying on a set of market participants, so maybe it's not exactly the same as a bank but they're kind of similar." JPM Coin is permissioned and restricted to JPMorgan and its clients. 32:56–33:41 ↳ Consensus: Contrarian against crypto messaging, aligned with market-structure analysis. This is the sharpest observation in the lecture.

9. Blockchain must shed its philosophy to become infrastructure ↳ Evidence: "Some of the purity of philosophy is going to have to go away to use blockchain just as a technology along with all of these things alongside it." He specifically flags that omnibus accounts and netting are hard to reproduce on chain. 35:40–36:34 ↳ Consensus: Emerging in 2024, substantially vindicated since — the 2025–26 growth has come from regulated stablecoins and bank deposit tokens, not permissionless peer-to-peer transfer.

💬 Best Quotes

"Money only moves by ledgers moving around at various places. There's no moving of money by train." 09:05 — His dismissal of the "rails" metaphor, and the cleanest statement of what payment systems actually are.
"World real-time retail payment is actually a lie, because the money is not actually going right from you to you." 15:40 — The observation most payments commentary never makes.
"Some of the purity of philosophy is going to have to go away to use blockchain just as a technology." 36:24 — The thesis, and in hindsight the correct 2024 call.
