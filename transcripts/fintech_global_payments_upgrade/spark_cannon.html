Fintech Won The Payments Upgrade And Blockchain Barely Showed Up: Tuckman's 2024 Scorecard, Audited In 2026

Source World Knowledge Forum · Date n/a · 36 min
Domain Payments & Financial Infrastructure
Duration 36 min
Date
Speakers
  • Bruce Tuckman — Clinical Professor of Finance, NYU Stern School of Business; Chief Economist at the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission from 2017 to 2020. Sole speaker; slide-driven academic lecture, no panel and no interviewer.
Full Mode

Executive Summary

  • Tuckman's scorecard is blunt and, for 2024, correct: payment systems improved enormously across fifteen years, and fintech did it without blockchain. Speed, convenience and access all moved — through UPI, Square, M-PESA and Wise, every one of them built on conventional bank rails. "The only successful blockchain sortie there's been in large scale payment systems has been in cross-border payments." 01:06–01:24
  • The most valuable idea in the lecture is that "real-time retail payments" is real-time authorisation draped over batch-net settlement. UPI credits the payee instantly, but the money settles net in batches, with the crediting bank carrying interim risk that "has to be managed by the central bank." He says it plainly: "world real-time retail payment is actually a lie." 15:40–16:20
  • Blockchain is natively real-time gross settlement, and Tuckman argues that is a cost, not a feature. RTGS is liquidity-hungry: his worked example has nine payments across three banks that net to exactly zero and still require 30 units of reserves against 90 of payment value. Omnibus accounts and netting — the two structures that made fintech cheap — are "quite alien" to the blockchain model. (05:56–07:07, 35:27–36:31)
  • One substantive error, and it is the one that matters most for regulation: he calls JPM Coin a stablecoin "backed fully by US dollars." It is a deposit token — a digital claim on an existing bank deposit, capable of bearing interest, not a 1:1 reserve-backed instrument. JPMorgan itself draws the distinction, and the reserve requirement, regulatory treatment and failure mode all differ. 33:41–33:59
  • Both of his blockchain cross-border examples abandon the premise blockchain was sold on, and he says so. Ripple's On-Demand Liquidity depends on market makers quoting both legs — "you're relying on a set of market participants, so maybe it's not exactly the same as a bank but they're kind of similar." JPM Coin is permissioned, restricted to JPMorgan and its clients. 32:56–33:41
  • The talk's checkable numbers hold up unusually well. Kenya's inclusion figures are accurate to the decimal against the Central Bank of Kenya's own survey; the cross-border cost distribution matches the World Bank's 2024 series; Square's 2009 founding and Wise's 2011 founding are both right.
  • Its closing question was answered by statute within a year of being asked. Tuckman ends by asking whether blockchain can grow on fintech's foundations if it gives up its purity. The GENIUS Act was signed on 18 July 2025 and MiCA's crypto-asset service provider title applied from 30 December 2024 — both of which build exactly the regulated, intermediated, reserve-backed architecture he describes as the price of admission.

Format note: the assignment brief described this as a panel. It is not — it is a single-speaker lecture with a Q&A-free structure, ending in applause at 36:35. No second voice appears anywhere in the transcript.

Date note: metadata carries no upload date. The talk dates itself internally — "look back now from 2024" 00:45 and "this stuff is always of 2024, so not even of 2009, even of today" 27:17 — and the speaker refers to being in Korea, with Korean won as his worked cross-border example. 💵 Three Kinds Of Money, One Settlement Asset — The Frame Everything Else Rests On

Tuckman spends the first four minutes on a taxonomy that most payments commentary skips, and every later argument depends on it.

There are two traditional kinds of money. Central bank money — physical cash, a liability of the central bank, plus the reserves banks hold there 01:45–02:26. Private bank money — your deposit, which counts as money "because each depositor is willing to accept a bank deposit in payment" 02:28–02:53.

The structural point follows: "ultimate settlement is always in central bank money" 03:08–03:13. Same-bank transfer: one ledger entry down, one up, and "nothing else happens... no actual funds are moving anywhere" 03:36–03:46. Cross-bank transfer: both ledgers move and reserves move between the banks, "because the orange bank wants final settlement" 04:03–04:22.

💡 Key InsightThe same-bank case is the whole business model of every fintech in the lecture. If both parties are inside one ledger, no settlement asset has to move at all — which is why Square, Alipay, WeChat Pay, Paytm, PayPal and M-PESA all hold customer funds in a single segregated omnibus account rather than per-customer bank accounts 20:06–20:32. Tuckman draws the conclusion himself at 20:32: this is effectively "a third kind of money," and the firms issuing it "are not regulated anyway the same way the banks are regulated." He flags it and moves on. It is the most under-explored observation in the talk.
🎯 Against ConsensusHe rejects the industry's own central metaphor. "It's called rails because people like to think of money moving on a train over train rails, and that's a silly analogy because money only moves by ledgers moving around at various places. There's no moving of money by train" 08:56–09:13. The correction is not pedantic: once you accept that payment is bookkeeping, the design question stops being "how fast can value travel" and becomes "whose ledger is authoritative, and when does it become final." Every disagreement about blockchain in payments is a disagreement about that second question.

⏱️ Real-Time Gross Versus Batch Net: The Liquidity-For-Immediacy Trade Nobody Escapes

The lecture's analytical core is a worked comparison, and it is the part worth keeping.

Under real-time gross settlement, each payment settles in central bank money the moment it is made. Tuckman runs nine payments across three banks, constructed so everyone ends at net zero. Nobody owes anybody anything at the close — and yet Bank A needed 10 of reserves and Bank C, unluckier in ordering, needed 20. "In total the system needed 30 of reserves even though there's only 90 worth of payments" 05:56–06:44. Under batch net settlement you wait, then settle the net — which in his example requires zero reserves 07:07–07:32.

"Real-time gross settlement is great because you get your money right away, and also it's great because there's no opportunity for some bank to owe a lot of money... but the disadvantage is you have to hold a lot of liquidity."

How the world resolved it pre-fintech: RTGS for large interbank payments — "it's just too risky for big banks to wait for each other to pay and then maybe they won't pay" — and batch net for everyone else. "All the rest of us, we have to wait" 08:13–08:45.

🔗 ConnectionSettlement economics → the blockchain verdict, thirty minutes later. This section is set up as neutral exposition but it is the load-bearing premise of the conclusion. Blockchain is structurally RTGS: transfer the token, done, final. Tuckman's framework says RTGS is precisely the design that maximises liquidity requirements — which means blockchain's most-advertised property is, in settlement terms, its most expensive one. He returns to this explicitly at 34:41–36:31. Everything between is evidence.

🎭 "Real-Time Retail Payments" Is A Lie — And A Well-Engineered One

India's Unified Payments Interface is his poster child, and his treatment of it is the best thing in the lecture because he refuses to accept the marketing at face value.

The design elements he credits: a national electronic ID system, all accounts linked to mobile phone numbers rather than account and routing numbers, QR codes for merchants, and — the part he emphasises — mandated openness. "Any company can create an app that links into this system, as opposed to just the banks doing it and you've got this clunky thing and you can't use it and there's no competition forcing better provision of services." The result is PhonePe, GPay and Amazon Pay competing on top of shared infrastructure 14:38–15:27.

Then the puncture:

"It happens though that world real-time retail payment is actually a lie, because the money is not actually going right from you to you — it's actually being settled net batch."

The consequence he draws matters more than the debunking. The bank crediting the payee "is taking some risk... the banking system is taking some risk that the payments are going to come in and ultimately [be] made, and that has to be managed by the central bank" 15:52–16:08. Instant retail payment is not a technology achievement; it is a risk-allocation achievement, with a central bank standing behind an intraday exposure that the user never sees.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Right on substance, wrong on the name, and his league table mixes two metrics. UPI is the Unified Payments Interface, not "United." He names "the five biggest — India, Bahrain, Thailand, Argentina, Nigeria." By absolute real-time volume in 2024 the top five were India (49% of global real-time transactions), Brazil (14%), Thailand (8%), China (7%) and South Korea (3%). Bahrain and Nigeria top the tables measured by share of electronic payments or per-capita adoption — Nigeria recorded 7.9bn real-time transactions in 2023 at 82.1% of all electronic payments, and Bahrain is forecast to lead on per-capita adoption by 2027. Argentina appears in neither list located. Two different league tables, not one. Source: ACI Worldwide Prime Time for Real-Time 2024.
🔍 Source Verified✅ His choice of poster child was correct, and the trajectory has steepened since. UPI is now the world's largest real-time payments platform by volume: 23.2 billion transactions worth ₹29.9 trillion in May 2026, roughly 738 million per day, with July 2026 setting a further record near 23.7 billion. Sources: NPCI monthly statistics; ACI Worldwide.
🌐 Consensus CheckHis "more than 70 countries now have real-time retail payment systems" 16:48 is defensible but depends on the counter. ACI's flagship report tracked 51 markets in its 2024 edition; industry counts that include every live domestic instant-payment scheme reach the 70–80 range. His explanatory claim is the stronger one and it holds: the urgency came from countries "that had mostly cash, very little access, no credit availability," while Korea (~90% electronic transactions) and the US (~80s%) were late precisely because they had less to fix 16:56–17:49.

🏦 Fintech Won On Bundles, Not On Speed — The Square And M-PESA Evidence

The lecture's most transferable argument is that none of these systems won by moving money faster. They won by wrapping payment in everything adjacent to it.

Square's stack: card reader hardware, payments processing "a little bit faster than traditional payment systems," point-of-sale software for inventory, customer contact and payroll, e-commerce integration, then banking products — checking, savings, a linked debit card 18:24–19:28. His framing is pointed: "Block promised to do boom, one payment from one person to another, but to get this to be successful, look at all of these other things they did" 18:28–18:37. M-PESA reached the same place through a different asset: Safaricom was Kenya's dominant mobile provider, so "basically everyone is our customer" — e-money on the SIM card, plus an existing agent network repurposed to convert cash into e-money and back 21:32–22:26.

📜 AnalogBoth firms solved the same problem the same way — by using an omnibus account to make internal transfers costless, exactly as a single bank does for its own depositors. Square "doesn't have a bank account for every single Square customer, it has one big omnibus account" 20:17–20:24; M-PESA holds customer cash "in an omnibus trust account... one account for all of the customers" 22:26–22:36. Fintech's structural innovation was not a new payment technology. It was becoming a bank ledger without becoming a bank.
🔍 Source Verified✅ Both founding claims check out, and the coincidence he draws is real. Square was founded in 2009, the same year as the first Bitcoin transaction, which is the parallel he uses to argue fintech was "already moving along" independently 18:14–18:22. Wise was founded in 2011 in London by two Estonians — Taavet Hinrikus, Skype's first employee, and Kristo Käärmann — launched as TransferWise and renamed Wise in early 2021 28:57–29:11. Sources: company histories; Wise corporate record.
⚡ TensionAt 01:11 Tuckman calls cross-border "the only successful blockchain sortie there's been in large scale payment systems." But at 30:56 he says blockchain "hasn't been used in large magnitude in any of the other things that I talked about, except perhaps illegally." The two are hard to hold together once the numbers are attached: Ripple Payments has processed roughly $70bn cumulatively across 90+ markets, against UPI clearing ₹29.9 trillion — on the order of $340bn — in a single month. "Sortie" is the right word, which he uses at 01:13 and then quietly upgrades. The honest framing is that blockchain established a real but small beachhead in cross-border, not a large-scale one.

📊 Data As Collateral: The Lending Unlock That Made Payments Stick

The section Tuckman flags as "the grander theoretical picture" is the strongest economics in the talk.

The standard development-economics constraint is collateral: "people have no collateral to borrow against, so you can never get your life going" 23:39–24:01. Transaction data substitutes for it — "here your credit history, just the history of making money every day, a small amount, is enough. It substitutes instead of collateral" 24:01–24:10. Square runs the same play from the merchant side: "once all the payments are in this system, Square can say, oh I see how much money you're getting, I'm willing to make you a loan because I know what your revenue is" 19:31–19:43. M-Shwari does it for individuals, with repayment swept automatically from incoming funds; his illustration is a market trader borrowing in the morning and repaying from the day's sales 22:47–23:37. He makes the privacy trade explicit rather than eliding it: "we're always worried about giving up our data, but this is a great use of giving up your data" 19:47–19:56.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed to the decimal — the strongest evidence in the lecture. Tuckman cites Kenyan access going from "only 27%" of the population with formal financial services in 2006 to "83%" in 2019, and complete exclusion falling from 41% to 11%. The Central Bank of Kenya's 2019 FinAccess Household Survey records formal inclusion at 26.7% in 2006 rising to 82.9% in 2019, and complete exclusion narrowing from 41.3% to 11.0%. Inclusion reached 83.7% by the 2021 survey. Source: Central Bank of Kenya, 2019 FinAccess Household Survey.
💡 Key InsightHe also records the regulator's answer to the criticism that easy accounts enable illicit use, attributed to the Governor of the Central Bank of Kenya: better than the cash and informal systems it replaced, so "we have to put up with a little leakage if we want to make progress" 24:37–24:51. That is a clean statement of the access-versus-AML trade he sets up earlier, where know-your-customer, anti-money-laundering, privacy and tax-enforcement rules all "fight a little bit against access" — resolved by tiered "light bank accounts" with fewer services 13:16–13:56. The specific attribution is plausible but unverified here; treat the quote as paraphrase.

🌐 Cross-Border Is Where Correspondent Banking Still Bleeds

His correspondent-banking walkthrough is the clearest thing in the lecture: payer to its own bank; bank to an international correspondent; the correspondent engages an FX trader to sell dollars and buy won; the won is deposited into the sending bank's account at the Korean correspondent; from there to the payee's bank; finally to the payee 25:34–26:41. The reason for the structure is a constraint he states cleanly — "you can only trade US dollars in the US system and you can only trade won in the Korean system," so each bank maintains an account at the other, which is what "correspondent" means 26:04–26:26.

"That's a lot of steps, and in the financial system every step somebody gets paid. No one's doing any of this — there's not volunteer work."

His structural explanation matches the G20's own diagnosis: "every country has its own payment system, its own set of regulations for banks and how payments are made, its own way data is stored, its own technological systems. It's no surprise, if you think about it, that this is so hard to do" 26:56–27:11.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Cost distribution confirmed against the primary series. Tuckman's breakdown — only 37% of international transactions costing under 5%, 45% costing 5–10%, and about 10% costing 10–15% — is consistent with the World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide data for the period: the global average cost of sending $200 was 6.35% in Q1 2024 and 6.49% in Q4 2024, with digital channels at 4.96%, non-digital at 6.94%, and banks the most expensive provider type at 13.40%. Sub-Saharan Africa remains the costliest receiving region at 8.37%. The database covers 367 corridors, 48 sending and 105 receiving countries. For context he does not supply: the UN Sustainable Development Goal target is 3% by 2030. Source: World Bank Remittance Prices Worldwide, 2024 issues.

⛓️ Blockchain's Only Foothold Is Cross-Border — And Both Examples Break The Purity

Tuckman gives blockchain a fair mechanical description — "it's a ledger, it keeps track of who owns a certain token, and you can just transfer ownership very quickly" 31:11–31:21 — and then a three-step cross-border version: sell dollars, buy a token, switch ownership on chain, recipient sells the token for won 31:35–32:00. "Whoa, that's so fast compared to all those arrows before."

Then he takes it apart.

On Ripple (public, permissionless, XRP, operating since 2012): the sender searches the Ripple system for someone to quote a price, and the transaction works in seconds if the quote is there. "But I just want to point out what it's depending on. Like, the blockchain dream is to have no financial intermediaries — but you're depending on someone willing to buy your dollars and sell you the token, and then on the other side to buy your token and give you won. So you're relying on a set of market participants. So maybe it's not exactly the same as a bank, but they're kind of similar" 32:44–33:23.

On JPM Coin (2020): "this is not the blockchain dream — it's not public, you know, anyone could participate. The only people who can play in this playground is JP Morgan and its clients" 33:26–33:41. He nonetheless rates it "an incredibly important step in blockchain" because the largest bank in the United States is saying payments can be made more efficient this way 34:00–34:16.

🔍 Source Verified❌ The one clear error in the lecture, and it is the regulatorily important one. Tuckman says of JPM Coin: "this is not a token, this is what's called a stable coin, so it's backed fully by US dollars." It is a deposit token, and JPMorgan draws the distinction deliberately. JPMD is a digital claim on an existing commercial bank deposit — backed by bank deposits rather than a segregated 1:1 reserve, and capable of bearing interest, which a payment stablecoin under the GENIUS Act cannot. The bank's blockchain unit was rebranded Kinexys in late 2024, and JPMD went live for institutional clients on Base, Coinbase's Ethereum layer-2, after a June 2025 proof of concept with B2C2, Coinbase and Mastercard. The distinction is not semantic: deposit tokens sit inside the regulated banking perimeter and inherit deposit-insurance and capital treatment; stablecoins do not. Sources: J.P. Morgan / Kinexys product documentation; The Block; Ledger Insights.
🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Ripple's position has changed materially since the talk. The SEC litigation that overhung the company for nearly five years ended in August 2025 when both sides withdrew their appeals, leaving the district court's holding that XRP is not a security when traded on public exchanges, a $50m settlement, and an agreement to stop direct institutional XRP sales in the US. More significantly for Tuckman's argument, Ripple launched its own stablecoin, RLUSD, in December 2024 under a New York DFS limited-purpose trust charter, with BNY Mellon appointed primary reserve custodian in July 2025; it passed $1bn market capitalisation by November 2025. Ripple Payments reports roughly $70bn in cumulative payment volume across 90+ markets. The direction of travel is precisely what Tuckman predicted: away from the volatile bridge token toward a regulated, reserve-backed, intermediated instrument. Sources: Decrypt; Ripple; CryptoSlate.
🔍 Source Verified⚠️ The Wise compliance aside does not match the headline enforcement action. Tuckman attributes Wise's "compliance issues" to operating "across jurisdictions with different rules" 30:16–30:40. The most prominent action was different in kind: in October 2024 the UK FCA fined CEO Kristo Käärmann £350,000 for failing to notify the regulator that HMRC had fined him £365,651 over a deliberately unpaid capital gains liability and added him to its public tax-defaulters list — a personal disclosure failure, not a cross-border compliance failure. ⚠️ Unverified — no primary source located for the specific cross-jurisdictional episodes he has in mind. Sources: FCA final notice coverage; Forbes; Wise corporate record.
🧵 Running ThesisBlockchain will only enter mainstream payments by abandoning its founding design commitments — no intermediaries, permissionless access, gross settlement. Status: confirmed since recording. Both of Tuckman's 2024 examples already broke the premise; since then Ripple has issued a reserve-backed, NYDFS-chartered stablecoin, JPMorgan has shipped a permissioned deposit token on a public L2, and the US and EU have built statutory regimes around regulated issuers with reserve requirements. The 2024 call was right and the evidence has only accumulated.

🧮 The Verdict, And The Question 2025 Answered For Him

His conclusion is a list of what fintech actually had to build, offered as the bar blockchain must clear: hardware, an identity system, on-ramps for the unbanked, software for tracking and budgeting and analytics, data-driven lending, dispute resolution, and omnibus accounts with netting 34:41–35:37. His verdict on the last item is the sharpest:

"The idea of keeping everyone's money in one omnibus account is quite alien to that. Yeah, you could do it in a layer two blockchain with smart contracts, but it's not that natural, because blockchain is naturally just real-time gross payments."

He is explicit that this requires giving something up: blockchain "needs to get rid of the dream a little bit of the 2008 quote that I gave you, that it's only going to do real-time payments" 35:45–35:58.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ The talk predates the regulatory answer to its own closing question by roughly a year, and readers should not treat it as current. The GENIUS Act (P.L. 119-27) was signed on 18 July 2025, establishing a US federal framework for payment stablecoins: they are not securities or commodities, are not federally insured, and issuers must hold at least one dollar of permitted reserves per dollar issued. Its effective date is the earlier of 18 months post-enactment or 120 days after final implementing rules, with the OCC issuing a notice of proposed rulemaking in 2026. In the EU, MiCA entered into force on 29 June 2023, with the stablecoin titles applicable from 30 June 2024 and the crypto-asset service provider title from 30 December 2024; national transitional cover runs to 1 July 2026 at the latest. Sources: Congressional Research Service; Covington & Burling; White House fact sheet; OCC Bulletin 2026-3.
🔍 Source Verified⚠️ The number most often quoted against Tuckman's thesis is the one most often misquoted. Stablecoin "settlement volume" figures for 2025 range from roughly $33 trillion unadjusted (Artemis) down to about $10.2 trillion adjusted over a trailing twelve months (Visa Onchain Analytics), with Chainalysis at around $28 trillion on its own adjusted basis and Visa reporting organic annualised settlement near $14.9 trillion. The spread is methodological, not factual: adjusted series strip high-frequency and bot activity using Allium's labelling of over three million addresses. Anyone citing a single headline number without saying which basis it uses is citing noise. Source: Visa Onchain Analytics methodology and dashboard.
📋 Protocol Cheat Sheet
  • Two settlement modes: RTGS = immediacy, no intraday credit build-up, high liquidity cost. Batch-net = low liquidity cost, delayed finality, accumulating exposure
  • Tuckman's worked example: 9 payments netting to zero still needed 30 units of reserves under RTGS, 0 under batch-net
  • Three kinds of money: central bank (cash + reserves) · private bank (deposits) · fintech e-money in omnibus accounts — the third largely outside bank regulation
  • Omnibus account: one pooled account for all customers, segregated from the operator's own funds — used by Square, Alipay, WeChat Pay, Paytm, PayPal, M-PESA, Wise
  • Cross-border chain: payer → sending bank → international correspondent → FX trade → correspondent account in destination country → payee's bank → payee. Every hop is paid
  • Cross-border cost benchmark (2024): global average 6.35–6.49% to send $200; digital 4.96%; non-digital 6.94%; banks 13.40%. SDG target 3% by 2030
  • Kenya access (verified): formal inclusion 26.7% (2006) → 82.9% (2019) → 83.7% (2021); exclusion 41.3% → 11.0%
  • Deposit token ≠ stablecoin: deposit token = claim on a bank deposit, can bear interest (JPMD). Payment stablecoin under GENIUS = 1:1 permitted reserves, no interest
  • Stablecoin volume, state your basis: ~$33tn unadjusted (Artemis 2025) vs ~$10.2tn adjusted trailing-twelve-month (Visa)
  • World Bank Remittance Prices Worldwide (free, https://remittanceprices.worldbank.org/)

Phase 3: Specific Item Breakdown

🟢 UPI (India) — Real-Time Authorisation Over Batch-Net Settlement, Opened To Competition

Tuckman's poster child for the real-time retail upgrade, and the case he uses to show that "real-time" describes the user experience, not the settlement.

What It Includes: A national electronic ID layer, accounts linked to mobile numbers instead of account and routing details, merchant QR codes, and — the design choice he emphasises most — mandated open access so any company, not just banks, can build an app on top. PhonePe, GPay and Amazon Pay are the resulting competitors 14:38–15:27.

The Evidence: Banks were also compelled to offer basic accounts linked to UPI, plus cash-in and cash-out paths, which is what converted the system from a convenience upgrade into an access upgrade 16:20–16:48. The settlement reality underneath: net batch, with the crediting bank bearing interim risk managed by the central bank 15:40–16:08. It now clears ~23.2bn transactions monthly — roughly half of global real-time volume.

🔵 M-PESA (Kenya) — Distribution Was The Moat, Not The Technology

The access case study, and the one whose headline numbers survive verification to the decimal.

What It Includes: Safaricom, already the dominant mobile provider, put e-money on the SIM card so any phone owner could pay for goods and services — then converted its existing network of phone-service agents into cash-in/cash-out points. Customer funds sit in an omnibus trust account, not individual bank accounts 21:32–22:36.

The Evidence: Banks initially opposed it, then joined by offering simple accounts that connect to M-PESA 22:36–22:47. M-Shwari savings and loan products lend against transaction and savings history, with repayment swept automatically from incoming funds 22:47–23:37. On the criticism that easy accounts enable illicit use, Tuckman records Kenya's central bank governor answering that it beats the cash and informal systems it displaced, so "we have to put up with a little leakage if we want to make progress" 24:37–24:51.

🟣 Square / Block — The Bundle That Proved Payments Alone Is Not A Product

Founded the same year as the first Bitcoin transaction, and the clearest counter-example to the idea that faster transfer is what customers were missing.

What It Includes: Card reader hardware, payments processing marginally faster than traditional rails, point-of-sale software covering inventory, customer contact and payroll, e-commerce integration, and banking products — checking, savings and a linked debit card 18:24–19:28.

The Evidence: Square is a money transmitter, not a bank, holding customer funds in a segregated omnibus account — segregated from corporate money, pooled across customers 19:56–20:24. Lending is underwritten on observed revenue running through the system rather than on collateral 19:31–19:56. Founded 2009, verified.

Why Tuckman Uses It: To make the contrast explicit — "Block promised to do boom, one payment from one person to another, but to get this to be successful, look at all of these other things they did" 18:28–18:37.

🟠 Wise — Cross-Border Solved With Two Omnibus Accounts And No Blockchain

The demonstration that the correspondent-banking chain can be collapsed by ledger design alone, with the balancing problem simply moved onto the operator.

What It Includes: Separate currency-denominated omnibus accounts — a US dollar pool and a Korean won pool. A customer moving money converts between their own balances on a phone; no funds cross a border in that moment 29:11–29:58.

The Evidence: Tuckman is careful about what this displaces rather than eliminates: after the customer's conversion, "the amounts in these accounts don't exactly correspond anymore to what the customers hold," so Wise manages the imbalance "either by actually doing FX transactions, or just hedging, or holding reserves, or even by changing its fees and spreads to attract transactions in the opposite direction" 29:58–30:30. Founded 2011 in London by two Estonians, Taavet Hinrikus and Kristo Käärmann, as TransferWise.

The Compliance Note, Corrected: His attribution of Wise's difficulties to cross-jurisdictional rule conflicts does not match the headline action: the FCA's October 2024 £350,000 fine was against CEO Käärmann personally, for failing to disclose an HMRC tax penalty and defaulter listing.

🔴 Ripple / XRP — Blockchain Speed That Still Depends On Somebody Quoting A Price

Public and permissionless, and by Tuckman's own analysis still intermediated — just by market makers instead of correspondent banks.

What It Includes: A public, permissionless chain operating since 2012 with XRP as the bridge token. The sender looks for a quote to convert dollars into the destination currency via On-Demand Liquidity; if a quote is available, settlement takes seconds 32:35–32:56.

The Evidence: His objection is structural, not technical: "the blockchain dream is to have no financial intermediaries, but you're depending on someone willing to buy your dollars and sell you the token, and then on the other side to buy your token and give you won. So you're relying on market liquidity to get this to work" 32:56–33:25. The intermediary has been replaced, not removed.

Since Recording: The SEC case closed in August 2025; Ripple launched the reserve-backed RLUSD stablecoin in December 2024. Ripple Payments reports ~$70bn cumulative volume across 90+ markets — a real business, and small beside a single month of UPI.

⚪ JPM Coin / Kinexys — The Bank Version, And The Term Tuckman Gets Wrong

A permissioned bank-only instrument that Tuckman rates as the most important blockchain development in payments, and misclassifies as a stablecoin.

What It Includes: Launched 2020, restricted to JPMorgan and its clients — "this is not the blockchain dream, it's not public" 33:26–33:41. He rates it highly nonetheless: "a super serious player... the largest bank in the United States that's now saying we can make payments more efficient by using blockchain in this way" 34:00–34:16.

The Correction: He calls it "a stable coin, so it's backed fully by US dollars." JPMD is a deposit token — a tokenised claim on an existing commercial bank deposit, backed by deposits rather than segregated 1:1 reserves, and capable of bearing interest, which a GENIUS Act payment stablecoin cannot be. The distinction determines the reserve requirement, the regulatory perimeter and the failure mode.

Since Recording: The unit was rebranded Kinexys in late 2024, and JPMD went live on Base for institutional clients in 2025 — making JPMorgan the first bank to issue a US dollar token on a public blockchain, a notably less permissioned posture than the 2020 version Tuckman describes.
