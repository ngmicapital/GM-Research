🎯 "Crypto Consumer Is Not a Big Enough Category": The Frame That Kills the Genre

"I think a lot of it is [that] crypto consumer is not a big enough category… we think of ourselves as a consumer app that's built on crypto rails." — 04:13–04:18

Jason opens the strategy section with the graveyard question: many, many people have tried consumer crypto, "we had like friend.tech for a little bit, was like very hot and then very dead" 04:00, so why did they all fail and why is FOMO the first breakout in years?

The Argument. The failure is a TAM error, not an execution error. The founder's number: "there's what, maybe like 50, 100,000 daily people that are actually using [crypto] apps every day" 04:28 — Jason repeats it back as "50 to 100,000 crypto TAM" 04:38 and gets confirmation, with a bull-market multiplier: "maybe it's like 5 to 10x that" 04:42. If your entire addressable market on an ordinary day is 100,000 people, capturing a meaningful share of it is not a business.

The Trap Inside the Trap. The second half of the argument is sharper and less obvious. Crypto users are "arguably the best consumer kind of type in the world" 04:52 — they will try anything, give feedback, and reward being early. That makes them seductive, and the seduction is the failure mode: "you build [and] overindex for an audience that is going to… work their way around whatever product you build" 05:09. A user base that routes around your bad UX will never force you to fix it. "You need to be able to build for the mass consumers, which is the rest of the world" 05:16.

🔗 Connection

04:13 → the companion file's opening. FOMO's Head of Growth, Ren, opened the April interview with "I think if I thought of this as we're building a crypto app, we've lost." Two months later a founder gives the identical doctrine unprompted to a different interviewer. This is the clearest evidence in either file that the "consumer app on crypto rails" frame is not a growth hire's talking point but the company's actual operating premise — it survives a change of speaker and a change of venue. 04:18

💡 Key Insight — the TAM argument is also a defence of the fee schedule.

If your market is 100,000 crypto natives, you must monetise each one hard. If your market is "the rest of the world," you can afford to charge 5bps on majors, sponsor gas, and refuse payment for order flow 38:23, because the unit you are optimising is count of humans onboarded, not revenue per degenerate. Every product trade-off in §Product Trade-offs falls out of this one paragraph. 04:28 · 34:51

🏦 The Party Round Was a Distribution Purchase, and the Series B Was a Deliberate Snub of Crypto VCs

"We wanted to work with non-crypto VCs, because they've scaled the largest consumer [apps]." — 15:05

The chronology, as given on tape. Incorporated "December 30th 2024, like the last day of the year" 02:42; started raising "early January 2025" 02:48; a party round of "like 100, 150 angels" (Jason, 02:12), closed around "January, February" 2025 02:17. Then Benchmark (Series A), then Index Ventures with Union Square Ventures for the $75M Series B.

Why a party round. Not for the money: "it's really important for a consumer product to build distribution. So instead of having like one institutional anchor, we want… to be able to leverage all these different partners, whether they're builders, etc., but then also have some early traders that'll be testers and loyal users of the product to have ownership as well" 02:54–03:11. Asked flatly "Did it work?" — "Yeah, I think it worked pretty well. It helped us bootstrap" 03:14.

And the real reason, stated one sentence later. "The hardest thing with consumer, especially a marketplace like a social trading app, is building a two-sided market. It's like a chicken-and-egg problem, because you need new traders to fund, but then you also need the top traders to provide information" 03:16–03:29. Jason: "Makers and takers" 03:33. The angel round was a purchase of the supply side of a two-sided market.

The Benchmark story. Cold-ish: a warm intro from an existing angel, framed as "they don't really look at crypto, but it's worth just discussing the landscape" (14:19 area, 13:17–13:23). The founders were "late 15 minutes to our first meeting with Chetan" 12:52 — "that's the only meeting that we're actually late to" — and admit "we admittedly didn't know much about them until that point" 13:10. Chetan Puttagunta's verdict landed "within the first five minutes": "I get it… this is something that we have not really looked at for a while" 13:25–13:30. Benchmark's prior crypto exposure is given as "chain analysis and then something with Telegram as well back in the day" 13:32.

Index. The intro came from Chetan; a prior approach to Paul had come through "Julia… through [Henri] at Privy" 14:19, and had been declined — "we just weren't really interested in fundraising" 14:26. What changed the answer was pattern-matching, not price: "a partner that has done it before with the Robinhoods, the Revoluts, and really just understands how to scale [a] consumer app" 14:32.

The thesis behind the choice of investor is the sharpest thing in the section. Crypto VCs have seen the social-trading pitch too often to see it: "the crypto companies only see these crypto deals and they get fatigue from seeing the same deal over and over. So then they put these blinders on. They're like, okay, no, I already said no to this deal… whereas the non-crypto VCs actually look at things individually because they have such a wide array. They're doing data centres in space to design software to FOMO" 15:18–15:49. Jason confirms he had the same reflex about FOMO itself: "I was like, oh my god, another social trading app" 15:39.

📜 Analog

The party round mirrors a pre-IPO road show run in reverse. Se makes the comparison himself later, complaining that crypto tokens never get one 72:37 — but the 140–150-angel round is a road show: a distributed placement to a list of holders chosen for their ability to move demand, priced early, executed before the product existed. The difference from a token launch is that the instrument was equity, so the holders could not sell into the users. 02:54 · 72:37

🎯 Against Consensus

Deliberately avoiding crypto-native capital is close to heresy for a crypto company, because crypto VCs are the ones who supply ecosystem introductions, exchange relationships and token-launch machinery. FOMO's answer is that it wanted none of those things, and that the standard crypto cap table would have pulled it toward the standard crypto playbook. The Series B syndicate — Index, USV, Benchmark — contains no firm with a structural need for a token, which is the capital-structure expression of the policy at 71:26. 15:05

⚙️ The Product Trade-offs Are the Real Interview: Five Decisions That Cost Them Something

Jason's best sequence is 31:29–34:44, where he asks not what FOMO built but what it gave up. Paul answers with five specific trade-offs, each with the cost named. This is the densest operational passage in either FOMO file.

1. No market makers. DEXs only. "Today we don't work with any market makers. We go through DEXs entirely" 31:35 — despite the founders' own institutional background: "[Deutsche] Bank and dYdX, where I spent a lot of time just onboarding a lot of crypto market makers, and market makers into crypto as well" 31:44. The reason given is timing rather than principle: "it just didn't feel like something that made sense at the moment" 31:52.

2. Paying routers not to take positive slippage. The mechanism, in full: "we actually work with all of our aggregators and routers so that they don't take positive slippage, which is a concept that people probably haven't heard of before… you go buy SOL at $80, it goes up to 81, but because we showed you 80, they can take a dollar and pocket that. We pay them a monthly amount to say you're not taking that amount, because if the price moves in favour of their execution, they should get the full amount" 31:56–32:19. The industry gloss: "it's something that every single trading app pretty much turns a blind eye to, because it's in their best interest to either take a profit share or to let the other aggregator earn more money" 32:20–32:28. And the conclusion: "we will actively lose money to make sure the user gets the absolute best execution" 32:29.

3. Cash-balance (USDC) trading, not native-token balances. "You trade from a cash balance. You don't trade from Solana into coins, or BNB or ETH — and that's a decision that I think is very rare. Nobody has said that before" 33:06–33:16. The justification is beta: "you shouldn't have to face market beta to whatever the underlying gas token is" 33:21, made possible "with how gas sponsorship works today" 33:18. The cost, named: "crypto native traders are like, I think in SOL, or I think in ETH, or I think in BNB" 33:27.

4. No user-controlled gas or slippage. "We don't let you pay gas fees. So we pay that on your behalf. We pay priority fees, and it's all just based on an algorithm that we use. We don't let you set slippage. We don't let you set all these things that will be very foreign to a normal user, but [that a] crypto user might want" 33:40–33:51. The concession: "maybe for a more advanced tool like a web application, we can actually let you do these things on your own if you're a crypto trader" 33:55.

5. No wallet import — the expensive one. "From the early days, people were like, we want you to be able to import a wallet to FOMO, so then we can [trade on] an external platform and it show[s] there. But I think there's this trust vector where if I know that you're only trading on FOMO, then I know we have the same tooling as you" 34:02–34:17. The social consequence is the point: a normie can be sure the trader they are following is not running a professional terminal off-app. "That was a trade-off, because we kind of let go of external growth. And I think that made the path to where we are now harder. But now the benefit is instrumental" 34:30–34:39.

🎯 Against Consensus

Four of the five trade-offs reduce optionality for the sophisticated user in exchange for legibility to the unsophisticated one, which is the opposite of how crypto products are normally built. The orthodox move is to expose every parameter and let the power user tune it; FOMO removes the parameters and eats the cost of the defaults. Only #2 (positive slippage) is a straightforward cost to the company with no user-experience trade-off attached — which is why it is the one worth checking independently. 33:40 · 34:02

💡 Key Insight — the no-import rule is the only one that is genuinely load-bearing for the social product, and it is the only one with no stated upgrade path.

Gas, slippage and cash balances all come with a stated escape hatch for advanced users on the web app 33:55. Wallet import does not, and cannot: the moment a user can import an external wallet, the average-hold-time statistic on their profile 55:25 stops describing their actual trading and the transparency argument at 55:10 collapses. Every social feature FOMO has is downstream of this one refusal. 34:10

📜 Analog

"Crypto rails should dissolve into the background of the experience" 39:54 is the same design claim Stripe made about payments and Twilio made about telephony — the infrastructure wins by becoming invisible to the person using the thing built on top of it. The five trade-offs above are what "dissolving into the background" costs in practice: you cannot let the user configure a thing you are trying to make them forget exists.

🔍 Source Verified

⚠️ The fee schedule checks out; the Robinhood comparison is built from the least favourable end of Robinhood's range and the most favourable end of FOMO's.

(a) FOMO's own numbers — ✅ confirmed. Independent 2026 fee guides describe FOMO as charging 0.50% per trade on Solana with ~0.05% on blue chips (BTC, ETH and similar), matching 34:51 exactly. One material omission: the same guides document a ~$0.95 minimum fee per trade on Solana, which the founders do not mention when saying majors are "basically free" 34:57. On a $100 major purchase, 5bps is 5 cents; the applicable floor is ~19× that. The claim is true for large tickets and false for the small ones a new "normie" user makes first.

(b) Robinhood's 85bps — ⚠️ top of range, presented as typical. Robinhood does not publish a flat crypto commission; it earns a spread, which 2026 retail guides put at roughly 0.35%–0.85% on majors. 85bps is the ceiling, not the norm. Robinhood also runs advanced tier pricing reaching 3–5bps for high-volume traders — i.e. at parity with FOMO's majors rate. "Each way" is a further embellishment: the spread is embedded per transaction.

(c) "Robinhood has maybe 100 to 150 crypto assets" — ⚠️ overstated, and against his own interest. Retail trackers put Robinhood's supported crypto list closer to ~30 assets in 2026. This one runs against the flattering direction — inflating the competitor's coverage weakens his own "most of Robinhood's assets are on FOMO for 5bps" point — which is a useful reminder that the drift here is imprecise recall, not construction. Sourcing on this specific figure is retail-guide quality; treat the exact number as soft, the direction as sound.

(d) "tens of millions of assets available on FOMO" — not independently verifiable, but consistent with the platform's stated architecture: "everything's automatically available on FOMO and we don't actually do any listing" 73:31.

⚡ Tension — the same comparator, two ways, four minutes apart.

At 38:30 Robinhood crypto is "85 bips each transaction… more than us." At 38:00 the founder concedes the perception problem in the opposite direction: "for a normal user, they see Robinhood [as] free, [and FOMO as] 50 bips." 🌐 Consensus Check

The refusal of payment for order flow 38:23 is FOMO's cleanest genuine differentiator against the incumbent it most wants to be compared to, and it is under-argued on tape. PFOF is the mechanism that made Robinhood's equities business commission-free and is the subject of a decade of regulatory argument. FOMO's model — an explicit, disclosed, per-trade fee plus a monthly retainer paid to routers not to capture positive slippage 31:56 — is the structurally more transparent arrangement, and is the one claim in the fee section a sceptical reader could actually go and verify with a counterparty.

🌐 "The Biggest Revolution Since Trading Was Computerised in the 1970s": The Vision, Stated Once, In Full

"Our goal is for FOMO to be the largest trading app in the world and the social layer of all… finance." — 10:04

Asked how they describe the company, Se gives the pitch in one uninterrupted two-minute block — the only stretch in 74 minutes where either founder speaks at length without being cut off.

The diagnosis of everyone else's failure. Previous consumer-crypto attempts treated the blockchain as "this new distribution channel of either crypto products, or this new platform that we could capitalise [on] with skeuomorphic products that worked on the internet [but] wasn't on chain" 10:16–10:26. They ported existing internet products onto a chain instead of asking what the chain uniquely enables.

The two native properties. "One, that it's… a global distribution channel. From day one, you could globally distribute this product" 10:32–10:36. "Two is that it's fully transparent, and all the infrastructure for this transparency exists in this distributed network where people have these wallets and there's this consensus mechanism around it" 10:37–10:47.

The synthesis, which is the actual product insight. Give a user full custody of a wallet, then attach an arbitrary display name — "you could be Jason, or you could be blocks lover, or you could be… New York 3835 whatever" 10:54–11:01 — and you get pseudonymity plus a non-curatable record. "This automatically creates this transparent social layer, where all you have to do is use this as a trading product and all of the social automatically follows from that" 11:03–11:12. The social network is a byproduct of settlement, not a feature bolted on top of one.

The historical claim. "This is now the biggest revolution in financial products since trading was computerised for the first time in the 1970s, where NASDAQ, New York Stock Exchange, all of them are realising that everything is moving on chain" 11:14–11:25.

The explanation of why social trading has failed for a decade — the best line in the section. "Everyone has kind of realised and known that there's going to be a… social finance consumer app that will take off, for the past decade. And the reason it hasn't worked is because it's filled with confirmation bias, because you only show trades that you want to show, or there's… obfuscation of what's actually going on" 11:49–12:05. On-chain settlement removes the ability to curate your own record. That is the entire company in one sentence.

Jason's immediate association is the obvious one: "the first time I saw FOMO, the company that popped up in my head was eToro… one of the things that made eToro so big and so successful was copy trading, like 15 years ago" 12:15–12:27 — which sets up the copy-trading refusal two sections later.

📜 Analog

The claim at 11:14 is that on-chain settlement is to social trading what the stock ticker was to price discovery — not a new product, but the first time an underlying private ledger became publicly readable in near-real time. The founders reach for the 1970s computerisation of exchanges; the closer analogue is 1867, because the ticker did not merely speed up existing trading, it created the entire category of financial media as a side effect. FOMO's paid-data-API idea at 68:02 is the same business model that the tickers eventually produced.

🌐 Consensus Check

The claim that transparent settlement solves social trading's confirmation-bias problem is directionally supported and materially incomplete. On-chain settlement does make a wallet's trade history non-curatable, which is a genuine advance over screenshot culture. It does not solve selection at the account level: nothing stops a trader running several wallets and surfacing only the winning one — which is precisely why the no-wallet-import rule 34:02 exists and why it is load-bearing. The founders never join these two arguments explicitly, but the second is the only thing keeping the first from being false.

🚫 No Automatic Copy Trading: The Refusal That Costs Them eToro's Entire Business Model

"Let's say you had like a million dollars following your trade automatically. You're going to trade differently." — 29:58

The manipulation argument. "We just think it's… a downward slope and you can manipulate it, right? Because you could buy an illiquid token and then dump on your followers. There is the social consensus mechanism and reputation tied to it, but it's just not something we want to play with" 30:16–30:27.

Creator monetisation today is referral links, and Jason supplies the precedent. "Ref links were really how Bybit became… third, then second biggest exchange" 30:35–30:39. Confirmed: "there are people making tons of money on refs today" 30:40. Attribution-based monetisation is under consideration and does not exist: buying from someone's half-sheet could one day credit them "some of the fees… that's something we're thinking about building in the future, but that doesn't exist today" 30:47–30:53.

🎯 Against Consensus

eToro built a multi-billion-dollar business on precisely the feature FOMO refuses, and Jason names it in the first fifteen minutes 12:22. The orthodox product answer is that automatic copy trading is the killer app of social finance. FOMO's counter is that it destroys the signal it feeds on: if following is automatic, the followed trader stops trading honestly, and the transparency that makes the graph valuable evaporates. They are choosing a smaller, slower monetisation path — referrals now, possibly fee-share attribution later — to protect data quality. This is the same trade as the no-wallet-import rule 34:02, one layer up.

💡 Key Insight — "on a fee basis and not a profit basis" 31:18 is the whole design compressed into six words.

Paying a creator out of their followers' profits makes their incentive to pump. Paying out of fees makes their incentive to be worth following often. It is said in passing, in a list of maybes, and it is the difference between a signal network and a pump-and-dump venue.

🔗 Connection
