Executive Summary

  • The entire framework collapses to one diagnostic, and it is genuinely good. "In dating, there's really only ever two problems… a volume or leads problem, or a conversion problem." (10:58) Everything else Seda says — approach scripts, photo doctrine, wardrobe overhauls, text game — is a treatment assigned to one branch of that fork. It is a funnel model imported wholesale from sales, and it is the most transferable idea in 75 minutes.
  • His highest-conviction claim is the one his audience least expects: texting, not looks, is the bottleneck. "It's one of the most important dating skills, period." (11:41) rasmr immediately flags it as unpopular (11:44). Seda's proof is a 6'6" college roommate who converts above-average women easily from apps and loses the top of the market entirely on text (11:49). His prescription is unusually specific: ask for the date within three messages or less on an app (12:49), and never propose a specific day and time cold (14:33).
  • The one number he gives for his own performance is 60%, and it is the only measured figure in the episode. In a 2024 self-imposed 30-day challenge he ended by pulling 24 numbers/Instagrams in 24 hours — "I did it in about 12 hours" — at a conversion rate of "just about 60%… all daytime" (27:16 · 27:23 · 27:29). Every other rate in the conversation is an impression from his client book.
  • The photo doctrine reduces to a single question, and it is a good one. "What is the context in which this photo was taken?" (41:42) Not "do you look hot" — is this image explicable? His firm briefs photographers with a written PDF and a standard operating procedure specifically so the photos don't look like a photoshoot (42:06). The self-defeating loop — pay a professional to fake candour — is stated openly and never resolved.
  • His stated-vs-revealed-preference claim gets tested live, on camera, and passes. A woman in the chat calls rasmr's shirtless gym photo "gross" (51:59), and Seda answers within seconds: "If you have a shirtless photo, chances are you will get more results with that shirtless photo than without it… these are the worst people you can ask" (52:28 · 52:40). The academic literature does not agree with the outcome he predicts, but it does agree about the mechanism — see the verification block in that section.
  • "Social circle degradation" is the sharpest idea in the episode and it is empirically supported. "Your social circle's going to look very different at 26 than it will at 29… at 31, at 34" (68:14), which is why "the average client of mine is 33" (68:43). Mobile-phone network data backs this: contact counts peak in the early-to-mid twenties and decline steadily thereafter. He has correctly identified that his business is downstream of a demographic curve.
  • The age-cliff claim is the weakest thing he says, and it contradicts the largest study on the question. "If you just turn 35 or 40, you will see at minimum a 30 to 50% decrease in your results… At 35, 40, and 50, those are the three age milestones" (72:39 · 73:02). The Bruch & Newman analysis of a full month of messaging in four US cities finds male desirability rising until roughly 50. Both can be partly true — they measure different things — but the "30 to 50%" figure is uncited and unverifiable.
  • The most emotionally loaded exchange isn't about tactics at all. rasmr volunteers, unprompted, that of every woman he has kissed "probably like 3%… I care about" (09:19), and later abandons the entire optimisation frame mid-audit: "honestly, bro, fuck girls then… I just want to be myself as an internet creative" (32:29). Seda's answer is the argument of the whole episode compressed to one line — the same standard applies to "your worst stream, your worst post, your worst tweet" (32:41).

💼 "Top 5% Financially Doesn't Mean Top 5% in Dating": The Resume-Exchange Fallacy

"Dating and relationships are not a resume exchange." — Seda (04:15)

This is the thesis of the practice and Seda opens with it. rasmr asks the obvious question — why does a man earning $250k need a dating consultant? (01:27) — and Seda's answer is structural rather than personal.

The Claim. Every other domain of male achievement has a legible path. "If you want to learn how to excel in the corporate world, you go to college, you get a degree, you work your way up the corporate ladder, you can have mentors, you can pay for career coaching. In fitness, you eat less calories than you consume, you work out, maybe you hire a personal trainer" (01:55–02:11). Dating has no equivalent institution, because "the concept of romantic love and marrying for romantic love is a relatively new concept" (02:13) — historically recent as the primary basis for marriage, and, in his reading, destabilised again in the last two generations: "it's gotten really wonky ever since women are now in the workforce, they're empowered, they have the ability to earn more money on average" (02:28), so "women's standards for what they're looking for for marriage now are much higher than they were even one or two generations ago" (02:40).

Note his own hedge, which is worth preserving: "Sometimes women — I, you'd have to look at the statistics, but women earn more than men now" (02:35). He flags his own uncertainty rather than asserting it. That is more intellectually honest than the genre norm.

The Mechanism. "If you make roughly around 200k a year as a man, you're top 5% financially. But if you're top 5% financially, that doesn't mean you're going to automatically be top 5% in dating" (03:43). The disconnect is what brings clients in: "why is everything else good? Women should like me for these things" (04:11). And in the cities where his clients live, the resume is table stakes: "if you're in one of these high ELO low PvP cities like New York, Miami, LA, you're not the only rich guy a girl has ever dated" (04:26). So what breaks the tie? "Usually it's going to be some form of what she feels, aka the emotional connection" (04:47).

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ "$200k = top 5%" is close but slightly overstated, and directionally wrong for men specifically. Against 2025 US individual-earnings distributions, the top 5% threshold sits around $210,351 and the top 10% around $155,042 — placing a $200,000 individual earner at roughly the 93rd–94th percentile, i.e. top 6–7%, not top 5%. The correction cuts against his framing in a way he'd probably accept: because male earners out-earn female earners on average, $200k ranks lower within the male distribution than within the all-earner distribution. His point survives comfortably; the number is a round-off in his own favour. (DQYDJ US individual income percentile data, 2025.)
🌐 Consensus CheckThe historical claim — that romantic love as the primary basis for marriage is a recent development — is mainstream social history, not a contrarian take; it is the central argument of Stephanie Coontz's Marriage, a History (2005), which dates the love-based marriage norm to roughly the late 18th century in the West. Seda gets the direction right. Where he goes beyond the evidence is the causal chain from female labour-force participation to elevated marital standards; that is a contested empirical question in family sociology, not a settled one, and he offers no source.
💡 Key InsightStrip the vocabulary and Seda is describing a market where the supply of qualifying resumes exceeded the number of slots, so the clearing mechanism moved from credentials to experience delivered. That is the same move that happens in any commoditised market: when everyone meets spec, competition shifts to the non-spec attribute. His clients are men who optimised hard for the spec sheet and are surprised the auction isn't scored on it. (04:26 · 04:47)
🎯 Against ConsensusThe dominant online-dating discourse for men is appearance-deterministic — the "looksmaxxing" frame in which photos, height and jawline are ~everything and behaviour is noise. Seda is inside that culture (he uses its vocabulary fluently: mogging, PSL, high ELO, nerf) but his commercial position is the opposite: the highest-leverage variables he names are texting (11:41), context in photos (41:42) and congruence (34:47) — all behavioural, all trainable. He is selling against the determinism his own audience believes in, which is either a genuine finding from thousands of profiles or the only proposition a coach can sell. The interview does not contain enough evidence to separate those.

🔻 The Two-Problem Diagnostic: Every Dating Failure Is Volume or Conversion

"In dating, there's really only ever two problems… a volume or leads problem, or a conversion problem." — Seda (10:58)

The Claim. This is the load-bearing structure of the whole practice, and it arrives casually eleven minutes in. Either you are not meeting enough women — "you're not getting matches, you're not doing approaches, you're not DMing enough girls on Instagram, or they're not replying" — or you are meeting them and failing to convert: "you're struggling to get them out on a date" (11:04–11:18).

The Mechanism. The diagnostic is prefaced with a prior that does real work: "rare people are rare. So even if you have the best system, volume is important" (10:53). This is a base-rate argument. If the qualifying population is small, no amount of per-encounter skill compensates for an insufficient number of encounters — which is precisely why every downstream prescription (daytime approach, Instagram over phone number, one-a-day compounding) is a volume intervention, and why the texting doctrine is a conversion intervention. The two branches don't compete; they multiply.

The Practice. Applied live to rasmr in under a minute: he reports meeting women in person, disliking dates, and having no roster six weeks into New York (05:52 · 07:05). Seda's read: "it sounds like you're getting interest… but trying to get them locked down on a date to meet you out is the problem" (06:53), refined at 11:18 to "which it sounds like is a texting issue for you" and confirmed by rasmr at the end of the episode: "No, this is perfectly accurate" (67:38).

🔗 ConnectionTwo-problem diagnostic → the entire structure of the episode. Once you see the fork, the running order stops looking like a rambling stream and starts looking like a consultation: minutes 11–17 are conversion (texting, first dates), 19–27 are volume (approach, Instagram), 28–65 are conversion again at the profile layer, and 67–74 are a forecast about volume decaying with age. He never names the structure a second time, but he never leaves it either.
📜 AnalogThis is a sales funnel with the labels changed — leads at the top, conversion in the middle, and a diagnostic that asks which stage is leaking before prescribing anything. Seda's client base (30–33, $200k+, big-city professionals, 01:16) is drawn from exactly the population fluent in that language, and the framing is likely part of why it lands with them. The interview's other recurring vocabulary — ELO, PvP, lobbies — imports the same modelling instinct from competitive gaming.

💬 "Texting's the Easiest Place to Mess It Up": The Unpopular Core Claim

"It's one of the most important dating skills, period." — Seda (11:41) "Wow. Can you explain why? Because this is not a popular take." — rasmr (11:44)

The Claim. Text game outranks looks, money and venue for access to the top of the market. Seda's evidence is a single vivid case: a college roommate, "6'6", super good-looking, looks like a Chad", whose "text game is really bad". Result: "he can get above-average girls out from the apps very, very easily. But when it comes to the super, super hot girls…" he cannot (11:49–12:06).

The Mechanism. Two forces. First, the attention environment of an attractive woman: "in any city, or even in a small town, a hot girl's going to have a million options of anything to do at any one time" (12:11), and on an app, "she has like a thousand other likes, a thousand other messages, unlimited stimulus" (13:43). Second, fragility of the channel: "there's a lot of different little skills that happen in the texting, and if you mess up at any point, it's so easy for her to just stop responding and move on" (12:29).

His attention-scarcity evidence is a stunt he ran years earlier: "I made a fake girl account, hot girl. I made a fake Tinder. It got 10,000 likes in 24 hours in New York City" using stolen Instagram-model photos (12:57–13:08).

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ The stunt is unverifiable, but the phenomenon it illustrates is documented — and the real numbers are more extreme than the anecdote. No independent record of Seda's fake-account experiment could be located; treat "10,000 likes in 24 hours" as an uncorroborated personal claim. The underlying asymmetry is well documented, using Hinge's own internal data. An analysis by Hinge growth engineer Aviv Goldgeier, reported by Quartz in August 2017, found that on Hinge the top 1% of men receive 16.4% of all likes from women, the top 5% receive 41.1%, the top 10% receive 58.0%, and the bottom 50% of men share 4.3% between them. Expressed as a Gini coefficient, the straight-male "dating economy" scores 0.542 versus 0.376 for the straight-female one — an inequality gap that, on a like-for-like comparison with national income distributions, would rank the male side among the most unequal economies in the world. Seda's "unlimited stimulus" characterisation is, if anything, conservative. (Aviv Goldgeier / Hinge internal data, via Quartz, Aug 2017.)

The Practice — the three-message rule. "On dating apps, you want to be asking for the date within three messages or less" (12:49), and reply immediately rather than strategically: "if she just matched you back… it means she's interested, she's on her phone right then and there, she's on the app. So we need to capitalize on those things as quickly as possible" (13:24). The failure mode he names is a specific cognitive error: "guys tend to overestimate how much a girl is thinking about them and the perception of their actions" (13:47) — at least "early on before the first date".

His own risk preference is stated explicitly and is worth quoting because it inverts the standard advice: "I would rather give an inch and maybe be a little persistent over text to go and take a mile once we're on the first date" (14:08).

⚡ TensionAt (13:17) Seda tells men to message instantly because delay signals nothing and costs everything. At (34:47) his governing principle is congruence — "whenever someone detects an incongruence or something that feels off, what's their natural reaction? To pull away." But instant, high-persistence texting from a man whose profile is engineered to read as effortless is itself a congruence risk: the profile says low investment, the text cadence says high investment. Seda never reconciles these, and the resolution he'd probably give — that pre-first-date attention is not yet legible as investment — is available in the transcript (13:47) but never actually applied to this contradiction.
💡 Key InsightRead alongside (25:10) — "if you can't get her out within a week or two, the likelihood that you'll get her out on a date is very slim" — the rule stops being about confidence and becomes about a half-life. Match-to-date conversion decays fast; every additional message spends a fixed budget of attention on rapport that a first date would build better anyway. That reframing is the useful version, and it generalises far beyond dating.

📅 The Date Ask: Why "Coffee Wednesday at 7" Is the Worst Sentence in Texting

"That is the worst way to ask for a date, literally the worst way." — Seda (14:41)

The Claim. The most common "confident" date ask — a specific day and a specific time proposed cold — is the single highest-failure construction available. His example: you approach a woman at Starbucks, then text "Hey, nice meeting you earlier. We should go for a coffee Wednesday at 7:00" (14:33).

The Mechanism. Two distinct failures. First, information: "You have no context of her schedule, you have no idea what she's doing, so you're very likely to run into a rejection or a no" (14:43). Second, and more interesting, frame contagion: "once you get her in the no frame, she's more likely to keep saying no and now you have to backpedal and start asking questions" (14:49). The cost of the first no is not the lost Wednesday; it is that the conversation now has a refusal precedent in it.

The Practice — the two-step ask. Step one, soft-throw the idea: "Hey, nice meeting you earlier. We should go out sometime" — "now I'm soft throwing the idea of the date, get her to say yes to the idea of the date in a vague way" (14:55). Step two, once she's agreed in principle: "propose one specific day and one vague time. This is the best one especially on the apps. And this is free game, I shouldn't even be saying this" (15:11). The literal script: "Are you free tomorrow — are you free Tuesday, or maybe another day this weekend?" (15:23).

Why that shape: "it makes it really easy for her to respond. If you say 'when are you free?' she's going to think, okay, when am I free? Oh, Tuesday I'm doing this and I don't really know what I'm doing Wednesday… And you're making her think too much. She's just going to not reply" (15:30–15:40). And the fallback is pre-built: "she'll either say yes or no to Tuesday, and if she says no Tuesday — am I free another day this week? Okay, I can give you something else" (15:53).

🌐 Consensus CheckThe mechanism Seda describes maps cleanly onto two well-established findings in judgment and decision research, though he cites neither. The first is choice overload / decision avoidance — open-ended questions with a large option space measurably increase the rate of no decision at all, which is exactly his "she's just going to not reply". The second is commitment and consistency: securing agreement to a small, low-cost version of a request raises compliance with the larger version, the "foot-in-the-door" effect first demonstrated by Freedman & Fraser (1966). His two-step ask is a textbook foot-in-the-door with the door being a date. Seda derived it from volume, not from the literature; it happens to land on the literature anyway.
🔗 ConnectionThe "no frame" (14:49) → the congruence doctrine (34:47) → the incongruence-as-red-flag close (74:10). All three are the same underlying model: a woman's decision process is treated as state-dependent and path-dependent, not as a running utility calculation. Once a negative state is entered — a refusal, a detected inconsistency, an unexplainable photo — subsequent inputs are evaluated through it. This is the closest thing Seda has to a theory of mind, and it is never stated outright; it has to be assembled from three places in the transcript.

🚶 The Daytime Cold Approach: An Arbitrage on Male Friction

"There's no catalyst that motivates guys to do it in person… So that's also why I prefer to do it and I recommend guys to do it, because it's the easiest way to stand out and bypass all of the competition on dating apps, Instagram, etc." — Seda (23:57 · 24:36)

The Claim. Daytime street approach is the highest-yield channel in a city like New York precisely because it is unpleasant. This is his strongest structural argument and he delivers it as a throwaway.

The Mechanism. Competition is concentrated where friction is lowest. On apps, in DMs, in bars after two drinks, the cost of an attempt is near zero, so everyone is there and the top of the market is saturated (see the Gini figures above). On a sidewalk at 2pm the cost is high: "It's super high friction. It's nerve-wracking. You're going to get rejected. It might be awkward" (23:59). Seda's read on the resulting frequency: "Far less than you would think" (23:35), and the line he says he hears constantly from women and from clients' matches: "This doesn't really happen to me" — "especially during the daytime. At a nightclub, a bar, a nightlife venue, all the time" (23:44–23:50).

He also names the selection effect on the male side, without judgment: the introverted software engineer "will just go to the bar, have a drink or two, feel loose, and then approach that way. Or he'll just go on the apps" (24:24).

The Practice — the four-beat open. "Excuse me — hey, I saw you, I thought you were cute, wanted to say hi real fast. What's your name?" (19:53). Seda decomposes it immediately: "Direct, establish context, you tell her why you're there, and then you give her an instruction. What's your name?" (19:58). Two mechanical constraints: get attention at a safe distance because "she's walking, she probably has headphones in, she might be on the phone" (22:25), and "usually you want to make eye contact at least seven to five feet away" (22:31 — as spoken; he means from roughly seven feet in to five).

The objection he pre-empts. "A lot of guys think, oh, I'm giving too much power away by saying she's cute" (22:44). His answer: "Well then what are you there for? You think she's hot. You're not saying you want to marry her. You're not asking her for her number immediately. You're not offering to take her on a date. You're just there, you establish the context. Now you ask some questions and you learn a little bit more" (23:14–23:25).

The volume maths. "Imagine you had two or three opportunities every single day with the time investment of five minutes — two and a half minutes each conversation" (21:46–21:56). Compounded: "if you wanted a five girl roster, you could definitely have it within two to three weeks" (22:08). Note this is presented as illustrative arithmetic, not as a measured conversion rate.

Geography, specifically. "From Washington Square Park all the way down — from West Village down to Washington Square Park to Soho, it's just nonstop attractive girls" (19:28), and the New York advantage over dedicated pickup practice: "you don't need to set aside 2 hours a day to go do day game like one of these pickup guys" (19:39).

🔍 Source Verified❌ "Far less than you would think" is unverifiable — there is no reliable dataset on how often women are approached in public. Extensive searching found no peer-reviewed study, platform report or credible survey measuring daytime cold-approach frequency by city or demographic. Seda's basis is explicitly anecdotal and is stated as such — "the number one thing clients of mine hear, and I hear from women that I would have approached" (23:37). Mark this as expert impression, not data. The adjacent literature that does exist measures something different (street harassment prevalence surveys, which record unwanted attention and cannot distinguish a two-minute polite approach from harassment), so it cannot be used to confirm or refute him in either direction.
🎯 Against ConsensusThe mainstream dating-advice position of the last decade is that cold approach is obsolete, creepy, or both, and that apps are the rational channel. Seda's position is the exact inverse and — importantly — his reasoning is economic rather than moral: he is not arguing approach is more authentic, he is arguing it is less crowded, and he is explicit that the crowding is caused by everyone else optimising for comfort (23:57). Whether the etiquette objection is right is outside what the transcript settles; the market-structure claim is internally coherent and consistent with the Hinge distribution data above.
💡 Key InsightAt minute four he tells rasmr the problem with New York is that it is a high-competition lobby. At minute twenty-four he tells him the solution is to find the sub-venue inside that lobby where nobody is competing. Same city, same women, different queue. The generalisable rule is not "approach women on the street" — it is "locate where competitor density is lowest and enter there," which is a strategy statement, not a dating tip.

📸 Instagram Over Phone Number: The Compounding-Asset Argument

"She may ignore your first message. She may not even reply to your first message, but she'll see your story, she'll see your posts. That familiarity and that rapport will build." — Seda (25:18)

The Claim. After a cold approach, take the Instagram, not the number. rasmr asks directly whether Instagram is the right ask; Seda answers "Yeah" without qualification (24:50).

The Mechanism. A phone number is a one-way channel with a manual cost per use: "you have to manually text that girl every single time. If you want to get her out on a date, the only way you're going to initiate conversation is if you send her an individual message" (24:58). It also has a hard expiry — "let's say a girl's busy, she's going on vacation, you're busy, you can't get her out within a week or two, the likelihood that you'll get her out on a date is very slim" (25:10).

An Instagram follow is a passive, bidirectional, non-expiring channel. Even with zero replies, stories and posts keep delivering exposure, and the return path opens: "You'll see hers. It's way easier and more organic to start conversations both ways" (25:29). rasmr supplies the crude version of the mechanism and Seda accepts it: "If she likes my story, I know she wants [it]" → "Exactly, right?" (25:33 · 25:37).

The Practice — compounding, stated explicitly. Seda asks how many potential roster candidates rasmr has on Instagram in New York; rasmr estimates eight (26:04). "Okay. Imagine we 10x that" (26:06). Then the mechanism: "if you approach even just one girl a day every day for the next 30 days" (26:15), the follower base accumulates rather than decaying, and each addition raises the probability that some conversation is live at any given time.

🌐 Consensus CheckThe mechanism Seda is describing is mere exposure — repeated incidental contact with a stimulus increasing liking for it — one of the most replicated effects in social psychology (Zajonc, 1968; meta-analysed many times since). His "that familiarity and that rapport will build" (25:18) is a lay statement of it, and applying it to Instagram stories is a reasonable extension: story views are low-cost repeated exposures with no reciprocity demand. Two caveats he doesn't mention: mere exposure is strongest for initially neutral or mildly positive stimuli and can reverse for negative ones (repetition amplifies dislike as readily as liking), and it decays without novelty. Both are consistent with his own advice at (30:46) to post more and at (32:19) to raise the floor on your worst content.
🔗 ConnectionInstagram-as-asset (25:18) → the "intrigue factor" (30:09) → "guy with motion" (33:48). Seda uses three different vocabularies for one variable: an Instagram is not a photo album, it is a continuously broadcasting status signal. That is also why he tells rasmr the dating-coach handicap is survivable: an unusual, legible occupation generates intrigue, and "the intrigue factor of you having motion will most of the time outweigh the cringe factor or the uncertainty factor" (30:09).

🖼️ The Photo Doctrine: One Question, Asked of Every Image

"The question you need to answer with every single Instagram or dating app photo is: what is the context in which this photo was taken?" — Seda (41:42)

This is the longest continuous segment of the episode — roughly minutes 31 to 65, conducted live as rasmr screen-shares his camera roll to a Twitch audience. It is also where the doctrine is most concrete.

The Claim. A photo is judged first on explicability, not attractiveness. If a viewer cannot construct a plausible story for why the image exists, it registers as contrived and the whole profile takes the hit: "girls are going to be like, what's the context that he would take this? It's kind of weird" (42:21).

The Mechanism — the photoshoot paradox, stated openly. Seda's firm hires photographers and briefs them with "a full strict standard operating procedure and instructions" (39:59) and "a full PDF" — and the entire purpose is concealment of the process: "to make sure the photos look like they just happened. Like a friend took them" (42:06). He applies it to rasmr's first image immediately and unkindly: "This looks like a staged photo, whether you propped up your phone… or a photographer took it" (42:01), and he notes rasmr had said minutes earlier that he'd never do a solo shoot (41:55). He also rules out the obvious shortcut: "you have to be careful if you use AI photos, which I would not recommend" (50:56), partly because "they reverse image search you before the date, which some girls will do" (50:52), and "we actually don't really edit photos at all" (51:15).

The Practice — the mechanical checklist, as delivered live.

  • Camera height. "You usually want the camera to be eye level or just above eye level, because if it's underneath… you're going to get like a double chin. It's not going to highlight your jawline" (31:38). Above eye level, "especially on a slight angle" (31:57).
  • Sun position. On a beach photo: "the sun's behind you, so your whole face is going to look like one big shadow. Because it's a shadow, your jawline and your neck are going to blend into one" (42:56). The fix is free: "if you literally just turned around and the sun was on you, it would have been better" (43:14).
  • Lens. "It's also a high angle and it also looks like it's on point five x, which distorts your features" (43:09). Related, on why rasmr looks better on stream than in photos: "lens distortion tends to be less severe with video than with photo" (51:24).
  • Body angle. On a shirtless side-on shot: "when you're sideways like this, you actually look skinnier… there's no depth to the photo, so your physique doesn't look as good" (45:16). The correction: "you want to be slightly turned at an angle so that you look and appear bigger, and you can see more of the physique" (44:35), with the reference class named — "if you notice any of the bodybuilders take aesthetic photos, they're usually not straight on, they're usually on an angle, so then you would see the shoulder, the separation, the lat, your obliques, your abs" (45:30). Also: "your arm is blocking your abs and where the obliques would be" (44:23).
  • Side profiles. "It's very rare someone's best angle is a side profile" (49:51).
  • Group photos. "You do have to be careful when you take photos with other people that you're not getting mogged" (48:54) — i.e. visibly outranked by whoever is standing next to you. Applied twice in the episode, to a Halloween photo (56:01) and a photo with a friend (49:35).
  • Categories, not a single standard. "There's different categories of photos. There's photos where — I call them portrait photos — where you just look your best… Then you want a photo where you look kind of silly and kind of funny and self-aware" (61:34–61:44). A photo failing the portrait test isn't a bad photo if it was never a portrait: "This photo isn't meant to check that box" (61:32).
  • The self-aware pose exemption. "If you're going to take a pic where it's obvious you sat down and posed to take a picture, it needs to be self-aware" (60:23). A staged photo at a visibly-staged event (deer, costumes, a party) passes; "posing like Mr. want-to-be alpha male guy in front of a car" fails (60:38).
  • Novelty categories that work. Troll photos "can be good" (46:19); childhood photos and costume photos are named specifically — "a girl scrolling, she sees you, you look hot, you look good — oh, like funny photo of you as a little kid" (46:50); "photos like that with an animal do well" (60:56).

The 10-out-of-10 rule. "Every photo needs to be like a 10 out of 10. You can't have any eight out of 10s or seven out of 10s, cuz a woman's going to judge you based on your worst photo" (32:20).

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ "Judged by your worst photo" is a good heuristic that overstates a real effect. No study was found testing that literal claim — that overall profile rating tracks the minimum photo rating. What the profile-evaluation literature does support is weaker and differently shaped: Fiore, Taylor, Mendelsohn & Hearst (CHI 2008, Assessing Attractiveness in Online Dating Profiles) found that when the photo component is judged unattractive, whole-profile attractiveness falls below the mean regardless of how attractive the other components are — i.e. the photo acts as a near-veto on the profile, but that is a claim about the photo set as a component, not about the weakest image dominating a strong set. Separately, platform-behaviour analyses consistently find the primary photo dominates the swipe decision, with later photos mattering more for post-match messaging than for the initial yes/no. Verdict: right that photo quality is a floor rather than an average, and right that a weak image is disproportionately costly; the specific "worst photo decides" formulation is unevidenced and probably too strong for the swipe decision, though closer to correct for post-match behaviour.
⚡ TensionAt (32:20) every photo must be a 10/10. At (61:32) "this photo isn't meant to check that box" and a deliberately unflattering, self-aware photo is endorsed. At (46:19) troll photos "can be good". The unqualified version is the one that produced the biggest emotional response in the episode, and it is the version most listeners will carry away.
📜 AnalogSeda's own explanation for why polished photos stopped working is a market-sophistication cycle, and he draws the analogy to crypto himself: "5, 6 years ago, if you took LinkedIn-style professional photos on dating apps, you would have crushed. About 3, 3.5, 4 years ago that stopped working because it became saturated" (54:31); "you even see this in crypto, where people moved away from high float high FDV coins to memes" (54:19). Seda gets close to noticing this and doesn't say it.

🗣️ Stated vs. Revealed Preference: The Claim That Gets Tested Live On Air

"This is the disconnect between women — what women say and their behavior — which is very often large. And that's not just women, but men in general." — Seda (52:20)

The setup is unplanned and it is the best moment in the episode. rasmr shows a shirtless gym photo. The woman in his Twitch chat, "Griffy girls", who has been rating photos all segment, responds: "She said gross" (51:59). Seda answers within seconds.

The Claim. "If you ever ask a woman what type of photo shouldn't I have on my profile, they always say, oh, no shirtless photos, you look like a douche" (52:12). But: "I promise you, I built thousands of profiles. If you have a shirtless photo, chances are you will get more results with that shirtless photo than without it" (52:28).

The Practice — a specific, actionable corollary. "A lot of guys think, oh, I'll just ask my female friends for advice on my Hinge. It's the worst — these are the worst people you can ask" (52:40). The reasoning is a clean statement of why friend feedback is biased: "one, they already know you and they have all the context. You want someone who has zero relationship to you, doesn't care about your feelings, and will just be absolutely ruthless with responding to you" (52:47) — and, crucially, someone answering the actual decision question, "whether they would swipe right or left" (53:00).

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ The mechanism is well supported; his predicted outcome is contradicted by the experimental literature — but the two are measuring different things. Experimental work on sexualised dating-profile images finds that men shown shirtless in a Tinder-style profile are rated lower in competence and higher in likelihood of risky sexual behaviour by female raters (published in Sex Roles), and more recent work led by Prof. Gurit Birnbaum (Reichman University) with Prof. Harry Reis (University of Rochester) finds sexualised profiles are judged more negatively and elicit significantly less interest in long-term relationships. Notably, the Birnbaum work also reproduces Seda's own congruence argument from the opposite direction: a shirtless photo paired with a sensitive bio read as incongruent and increased suspicion of the man's intentions — which is exactly his (34:47). Where the disagreement is real and where it isn't: the studies measure rated impressions and long-term-relationship interest; Seda measures likes and matches on live profiles. Those can diverge, and the divergence is his entire point. Verdict: his stated-vs-revealed framing is a legitimate and well-attested phenomenon in attitude research; his specific outcome claim ("more results with the shirtless photo") rests entirely on unaudited in-house data from his own client book and has no independent corroboration. Take the advice about who to ask; treat the shirtless prediction as an untested house view.
💡 Key InsightGriffy girls is precisely the sample Seda says is useless — someone with a prior relationship to the subject, answering a stated-preference question out loud, in public, in front of the man being rated and his audience. Every methodological problem he names at (52:47) is present. And yet her verdicts are read aloud and treated as evidence throughout the segment, including by Seda, who at one point defers to her ("It's funny someone said, 'No way girls care about this.' I promise you they are", 45:47). The stated-preference channel he tells you to ignore was the one running live for thirty minutes. Neither speaker notices.
🎯 Against Consensus"Ask your female friends what women want" is close to universal advice and is the default recommendation in mainstream dating guidance. Seda inverts it completely and his stated reason is incentive structure, not gender: friends optimise for your feelings, strangers optimise for the answer. The version of this that survives scrutiny is narrow but real — the question "would you swipe right on this" asked of someone with no relationship to you is a materially different measurement from "is this a good photo of me" asked of a friend.

  4. Directed purchasing. 📉 Social Circle Degradation: Why His Average Client Is Exactly 33

"As you get older, you're going to experience what's called social circle degradation." — Seda (67:53)

The Claim. In-person dating success is a derivative of social network size, and network size declines predictably from the mid-twenties. Therefore in-person success declines on a schedule, and the decline is invisible while it's happening.

The Mechanism. "Your group of friends who you're out with in New York or LA — some of them are going to move out of the city, some of them are going to get cuffed, locked down, not going to go out as much, some of them are going to get married" (67:58–68:09). The timescale is the part that lands: "your social circle's going to look very different at 26 than it will at 29, maybe even 28, at 31, at 34. And that may sound far off, but these are like 2-year increments" (68:14–68:22). Consequence: "your success in person will shrink unless you have something to counteract that shrink" (68:34).

Why this is the business model. "That's usually why the average client of mine is 33. Because at 33, you're not going out as much, your friends are more fragmented, more focused on their own lives. Whereas you're 26 — this is about peak social circle and optionality, life-wise, that you're going to have" (68:43–68:57).

🔍 Source Verified✅ This is the best-supported claim in the episode. Bhattacharya, Ghosh, Monsivais, Dunbar & Kaski, "Sex differences in social focus across the life cycle in humans", Royal Society Open Science (2016), analysed a large mobile-phone call dataset and found that younger individuals have substantially more contacts, that the number declines with age, and that the rate of decline differs by sex — with a crossover in the late 30s after which women maintain more contacts than men. Seda's "peak at ~26, meaningful degradation by 31–34" is consistent with the shape of that curve, and his framing of the decline as driven by others' life-stage transitions (partnering, relocating, having children) is precisely the reproductive-investment mechanism the authors propose. Verdict: correct, and better-evidenced than he appears to know. (Royal Society Open Science, 1 April 2016.)
🔗 ConnectionSocial circle degradation (67:53) → the two-problem diagnostic (10:58). This is the volume branch with a time axis added. A man at 26 has volume supplied for free by an inherited social graph; a man at 33 has to manufacture it. That is exactly why the prescriptions Seda sells — cold approach, Instagram accumulation, apps — are all replacements for a decayed network, and why they feel unnecessary to the 26-year-old being told to start them. The advice is a hedge against a decline that hasn't arrived yet, which is also why it is a hard sale.
📜 AnalogThe structure is identical to deferred maintenance on infrastructure that is currently working. The cost of building the skill is constant and payable now; the cost of not having it is zero now and rises steeply later, at which point building it is harder because the practice environment (a large, active social circle) has itself degraded. Seda's "compounding that negative momentum" (10:37) is the same observation from the other end.

⏳ The Age Cliffs at 35, 40 and 50 — the Episode's Least Verifiable Claim

"If you just turn 35 or 40, you will see at minimum a 30 to 50% decrease in your results." — Seda (72:39)

The Claim. Men's app performance drops discontinuously at three thresholds. "At 35, 40, and 50, those are the three age milestones where guys see a big drop-off" (73:02) — with an important qualifier he adds immediately: "on the apps at least. In person, not as much, but on the apps, yes" (73:04).

The Mechanism. Pool contraction plus narrative penalty. "Once you hit like 35, 40, girls are going to start think — why are you 35? You're not married, you don't have kids, you don't have a house. 40's even worse" (72:21). And mechanically: "the pool of options of women who are willing to date 35-year-olds — you get filtered out. So now you have a smaller pool of options, so your conversion matters more, cuz there's less people to choose from" (72:47).

The supporting anecdotes. Two, both from his client book. A 54-year-old New York client "almost exclusively going on dates with girls in their 20s and 30s. From approaching on the street. There's a video of him literally getting a model's number on the street on my YouTube" (71:32–71:42), who "consulted with me for an extended period of time" (71:46). And a Fortune 500 partner in Manhattan who attends networking-social events with a hard 38-year-old age limit: "now he's turning 40 soon. He's slowly starting to get uninvited and not allowed to show up at certain events" (73:19–73:35).

On whether age is raised as an objection: "there's a selection effect. On the dating app, if they're swiping on him, they're okay with his age. In person, if there's an issue with their age, it'll usually come up in the approach itself or in the text conversation that takes place afterwards" (71:54–72:05).

🔍 Source Verified❌ The "30 to 50% decrease" figure is uncited and unverifiable, and the largest published analysis of the question points the other way. Seda attributes it to "statistically, from the data" (72:29) but the data in question is his own client observations; no external source was located. The reference study is Bruch & Newman, "Aspirational pursuit of mates in online dating markets", Science Advances (2018) — a full month (January 2014) of heterosexual messaging data from a large free dating site across New York, Boston, Chicago and Seattle. Its central age finding is the opposite of his: male desirability rises with age and peaks around 50 before declining, while female desirability declines continuously from 18 to 60 — the "gendered double standard of aging". The same paper found men message partners about 26% further up the desirability ranking than themselves (women 23%), and that reply probability falls as the desirability gap widens, staying below 21% for the most desirable targets. The honest reconciliation: the two are measuring different quantities. Bruch & Newman's "desirability" is a PageRank-style score derived from who messages whom and who replies on a free site skewed toward an older, relationship-seeking user base in 2014. Seda is describing volume of inbound likes and match counts for high-income men on modern swipe apps in 2026. Age can raise your ranking among those who consider you while shrinking the set who consider you at all. Verdict: the "35/40/50 cliff" is plausible as a description of inbound app volume, unsupported as a general claim about male desirability, and the specific 30–50% number should be treated as a house figure with no external corroboration.
🎯 Against ConsensusHis practical advice — a 33-year-old man should target women 24 to 28 (69:48) — sits in a genuinely awkward place relative to the revealed-preference data. Christian Rudder's OkCupid analyses (Dataclysm, 2014) found that men of every age, from 20 to 50, direct most first messages toward women in their early twenties, while women's stated and revealed preference tracks their own age, drifting a few years older. So Seda's prescription is not contrarian at all; it is the modal male behaviour, given a rationale. The rationale he supplies (below) is about timeline compatibility, not attraction — which is a different and more defensible argument than the one the data would otherwise assign to it.

The rationale, in his words. "If you're going to date a woman who's 30, 31, 32, 33, the biggest issue he's going to have is that she's going to be pushing to settle down and pushing for commitment and imposing her timeline on his… he may feel forced into that decision. Whereas if a girl's 24 at least, she's out of her party phase, she's out of her post-college phase. 24 to 28, she's in that band where she could be open to something longer term. And if you need to date her two, three years before you get married and have kids, she has time without being rushed" (69:57–70:37). Under 24: "in her party phase, too young, probably not thinking about marriage" (70:44).

And the caveat he volunteers, unprompted, which most of the genre omits. Asked whether he tells clients this: "No, they have a preference. I do not tell — I don't tell guys, hey, this is [the rule]" (70:54). It is a pattern observation, not a prescription: "Patterns-wise, that's where guys are most happy" (71:01). And it is explicitly contingent: "if I have a guy who's 33, he's divorced, he has a kid already, it might make sense for him to date a woman who's a little bit older if he's looking to start a family again… So it just depends on the context. There's no one-size-fits-all" (71:05–71:18).

⚡ TensionAt (73:04) the age penalty is "on the apps at least. In person, not as much." At (67:53) the case for in-person volume decaying with age is social circle degradation. Phase 3: The Trainable Skills, Card by Card

🚶 Skill 1 — The Daytime Cold Approach — The highest-yield channel precisely because it's the most uncomfortable one

Four beats, under three minutes, executed in daylight where nobody else is competing.

What It Includes

Attention capture at distance, a direct statement of intent, and an instruction — not a question that can be answered "no".

The Practice (Step-by-Step)

  1. Approach angle and distance. She is walking, likely with headphones. "You have to get her attention at a safe distance" (22:29); "make eye contact at least seven to five feet away" (22:31).
  2. The line, verbatim: "Excuse me — hey, I saw you. I thought you were cute. Wanted to say hi real fast. What's your name?" (19:53).
  3. The decomposition, in Seda's words: "Direct, establish context, you tell her why you're there, and then you give her an instruction. What's your name?" (19:58).
  4. Continue with questions. "Now you ask some questions and you learn a little bit more" (23:25).
  5. Close on Instagram, not the number — see Skill 3.
  6. Budget: "two and a half minutes each conversation", two to three per day (21:53).

The Evidence

Seda's own measured rate, from the final day of his 2024 challenge: 24 contacts in ~12 hours, ~60% conversion, all daytime (27:23 · 27:29). No independent verification; it is his own public challenge on his own channel.

Common Mistakes

Withholding the compliment to protect status — "a lot of guys think, oh, I'm giving too much power away by saying she's cute" (22:44); opening from behind or without eye contact; escalating to a date ask in the first interaction.

💬 Skill 2 — The Two-Step Date Ask — Never let her say no to a specific plan she has no information about

Get a yes to the abstraction first, then attach exactly one specific day and one vague time.

What It Includes

Two messages, deliberately sequenced, plus a pre-built fallback.

The Practice (Step-by-Step)

  1. Soft throw: "Hey, nice meeting you earlier. We should go out sometime" (14:55). Purpose: "get her to say yes to the idea of the date in a vague way" (15:00).
  2. On her yes, narrow once: "Cool — what's your schedule like, what are you up to this week?" (15:05), or the preferred app version: one specific day, one vague time.
  3. The line, verbatim: "Are you free tomorrow — are you free Tuesday, or maybe another day this weekend?" (15:23).
  4. On a no to Tuesday: the message already contains the escape hatch, so "I can give you something else" (16:00) without backpedalling.
  5. Timing constraint on apps: ask "within three messages or less" (12:49); reply immediately on match (13:22).

The Evidence

Mechanism only, no data offered. Independently, the two-step structure matches the foot-in-the-door compliance effect (Freedman & Fraser, 1966) and the decision-avoidance literature on open-ended choice.

Common Mistakes

The cold specific — "We should go for a coffee Wednesday at 7:00" — which Seda calls "the worst way to ask for a date, literally the worst way" (14:41); open-ended "when are you free?", which makes her do the scheduling work and produces silence (15:31).

📸 Skill 3 — The Instagram Pivot — Trade a decaying one-way channel for a compounding two-way one

Take the Instagram because a number expires in two weeks and a follow never does.

What It Includes

The close of the approach, and the passive nurture channel it opens.

The Practice (Step-by-Step)

  1. Ask for the Instagram, not the number, at the end of a cold approach (24:50).
  2. Message anyway — but treat a non-reply as non-fatal, because "she'll see your story, she'll see your posts" (25:22).
  3. Keep posting. Seda tells rasmr directly to increase post volume: "I would definitely have more posts" (30:49), and that a near-empty profile — "You have one post" (30:46) — is a hard aesthetic to execute: "it's a thing to have aura, to have no posts… but that's hard to pull off" (30:51).
  4. Let the volume compound. One approach a day for 30 days (26:15); "imagine we 10x that" (26:06).

The Evidence

No figures. Mechanism is mere exposure (Zajonc, 1968) plus the removal of a per-contact activation cost. The expiry claim — "if you can't get her out within a week or two, the likelihood… is very slim" (25:10) — is an impression from his client book, uncorroborated.

Common Mistakes

Collecting numbers you never text; treating a first-message non-reply as a dead lead; running an empty profile that gives the exposure channel nothing to deliver.

🖼️ Skill 4 — The Photo Audit — One question per image: is this photo explicable?

Judge every photo on whether a stranger can construct the story of why it exists — then fix lighting, lens and angle.

What It Includes

A context test, four mechanical checks, a category assignment, and a floor rule.

The Practice (Step-by-Step)

  1. Context test: "What is the context in which this photo was taken?" (41:42). Fail = staged-looking with no visible reason. Pass = "a friend took them" (42:14), or a self-aware pose at a visibly staged event (60:32).
  2. Camera height: eye level or slightly above, never below (31:38). Slight angle (31:57).
  3. Sun position: light on your face, not behind you (42:56).
  4. Lens: avoid 0.5x wide — "it distorts your features" (43:09).
  5. Body angle for physique shots: turned slightly, never straight-on side profile (44:35 · 45:16); don't let an arm block the obliques (44:23).
  6. Assign a category: portrait (look your best), silly/self-aware, activity, group. "There's different categories of photos" (61:34).
  7. Check for mogging in every group photo (48:54).
  8. Floor rule: "a woman's going to judge you based on your worst photo" (32:25) — cut the weakest image rather than adding a stronger one.
  9. Prohibited: AI-generated photos (50:56); heavy editing — "we actually don't really edit photos at all" (51:15).

The Evidence

Volume-based (thousands of profiles, 33:20). External support is partial: whole-profile ratings fall below the mean when the photo component is unattractive (Fiore et al., CHI 2008), and the primary photo dominates the swipe decision; the literal "worst photo decides" formulation is unevidenced.

Common Mistakes

Optimising every photo for attractiveness and none for explicability; keeping a side profile; keeping a photo where a friend outranks you; using a professional shoot that reads as a professional shoot.

✍️ Skill 5 — The Hinge Prompt Upgrade — Above-average prompts are common; long-form prompts are not

Short and punchy clears 95% of the field; length and self-disclosure are what separate you from the rest.

What It Includes

Live grading of three real prompts and one concrete instruction.

The Practice (Step-by-Step)

  1. The graded set (rasmr's actual profile):
  2. "The best way to ask me out is by — I will do the asking." → "That's excellent. That's excellent" (57:57 · 58:02).
  3. "When I need advice, I go to — my homie's girlfriend." → "Not bad… girls may say that's a red flag. Why are you friends with your friend's girlfriend? That's a little bit risky" (58:13–58:20).
  4. "The way to win me over is — worship me." → "Maybe a little bit basic" (58:29).
  5. The benchmark: "your prompts are better than 95% of the ones I see" (58:46) — and prompts are the rarer skill: "some clients can get good photos on their own, that's easier, but being able to write really good prompts, I don't see that super often" (57:33).
  6. The upgrade instruction: "get a little long-form on them. Write a little bit more. Pour your heart out into it" (58:52) — the stated purpose is separation from the field (58:59).

The Evidence

Practitioner judgment only. No conversion data offered for prompt length.

Common Mistakes

Copying the same three-word joke format everyone uses; prompts that invite a plausible red-flag reading; treating "above average" as finished.

👔 Skill 6 — The Image & Wardrobe Overhaul — The first intervention is what you wear, not what you write

Change the input before touching the representation — clothes first, photographer second, profile last.

What It Includes

A five-step engagement sequence run before a profile is edited.

The Practice (Step-by-Step)

  1. Application → call → custom plan. No public sign-up (35:03).
  2. Assignment one: "send me your best five to 10 outfits in a full-length mirror, you wearing everything head-to-toe" (39:28).
  3. Directed purchases matched to "your height, weight, body shape, your style" and to the demographic of women targeted (39:39–39:52).
  4. Photographer, briefed with a written PDF and SOP, output constrained to look candid (39:55 · 42:06).
  5. Shot list balanced to what's missing — solo, group, or activity (40:22).
  6. Then the profile is built (33:20).

The Evidence

Process description only. The only performance figure attached to the pipeline anywhere in the episode is the client baseline it starts from — "already getting one or two dates a week with sevens" (38:29).

Common Mistakes

Buying a style that isn't congruent with how you actually present (34:56); LinkedIn-style shoots (40:06); height inserts, because they're "too explicit" (27:57) — buy the boot instead.

🔻 Skill 7 — The Two-Problem Diagnostic — Before any fix, decide whether you have a volume problem or a conversion problem

Every dating failure is one of exactly two failures, and they take opposite treatments.

What It Includes

A binary triage that determines which of the other six skills you should be working on.

The Practice (Step-by-Step)

  1. Ask: am I meeting enough women? If not → volume problem: "you're not getting matches, you're not doing approaches, you're not DMing enough girls on Instagram" (11:11).
  2. If yes, ask: am I converting them to dates? If not → conversion problem, and "texting's the easiest place to mess it up" (11:19).
  3. Treat volume with Skills 1 and 3 (approach, Instagram accumulation).
  4. Treat conversion with Skills 2, 4, 5 (date ask, photos, prompts).
  5. Re-run the diagnostic on a schedule, because the volume side degrades with age even if you change nothing (67:53).
  6. Hold the base rate in mind: "rare people are rare. So even if you have the best system, volume is important" (10:53).

The Evidence

No data; it is a framework. It correctly predicted rasmr's own diagnosis, confirmed by him at (67:38).

Common Mistakes

Optimising photos when the problem is that you meet three new people a month; grinding approach volume when every conversation dies over text.
