🪙 "The Token Becomes Your Product": The Refusal That Defines the Company

"The biggest problem with a token is the token becomes your product and you have to spend all of your time focusing on the token." — Paul 45:38

This is the most consequential thing said in 135 minutes, and it arrives as an aside. rasmr asks the obvious question — dYdX had a token, Paul came from dYdX, other crypto companies have tokens, so what is the calculus? 45:07–45:24

The Argument. Paul's case is not regulatory and it is not ideological. It is about where founder attention goes. Launching a token creates a second product with its own price, its own holders and its own daily performance review, and that product will consume the schedule. Worse, it inverts your relationship with your best users: "everyone that has conviction and cares about your product, they buy the token, and if the token price goes down then everyone who cared about you just lost money" 45:43–45:50. He names it plainly: "it's a double-edged sword in that sense" 45:53.

The Counter-Example, Handled Honestly. Paul does not pretend the model never works. "Hyperliquid has done an amazing job here, but they're not a US company and they've been able to buy back their token and they've been able to support the price" 45:58–46:06. Then he punctures the standard Hyperliquid origin myth in one line: "Everyone talks about how Hyperliquid didn't take any venture capital, which is true, but then Paradigm bought 10% of their token supply, right? Or like some big number of it. I'm not sure it's exactly 10%" 46:16–46:26. Note the self-flag — he hedges his own figure mid-sentence, which is more than most operators do.

His conclusion from that: "institutions are going to get in no matter what" 46:33. The choice is not whether institutional capital touches your equity story; it is whether you also hand it a liquid instrument to express itself in.

🔍 Source Verified

⚠️ The Paradigm-bought-10%-of-HYPE claim is the weakest specific in Paul's segment, and he flags it himself. Hyperliquid's public token distribution allocated 31% of total HYPE supply to a community airdrop in November 2024, and the founding team's stated position — repeated in most independent write-ups — was an explicit rejection of the model in which firms take large discounted allocations. There are subsequent reports of Paradigm accumulating HYPE, but no verifiable public record was found for a 10%-of-supply purchase, and no source located confirms the figure. Treat it as an uncorroborated market rumour that Paul relayed with an explicit "I'm not sure". The structural point he uses it to make — that institutions find their way in regardless — stands independently.

🌐 Consensus Check

Paul's "the token becomes your product" is a minority position inside crypto and the majority position in consumer fintech. The standard crypto playbook (points programme → airdrop → token → buybacks) is what almost every venue in his competitive set has run. His refusal aligns him instead with Robinhood, Cash App and Coinbase's pre-2021 posture: build the app, monetise the flow, keep one instrument. The interesting tell is that the founders' answer survives being asked twice, by the same interviewer, 80 minutes apart, to two different people — Say gets the identical question at 127:23 and answers "it's not something that we've really given much thought to" 127:36 before pivoting to the same reasoning Paul used: it "is its own product when you launch it" 127:44.

💡 Key Insight

Key Insight — the refusal is an attention-allocation argument, not a token-economics one. Every objection Paul raises is about what the founders spend their days on, and every objection the crypto orthodoxy raises about not having a token is about capital formation and user incentives. These are not opposing answers to one question; they are answers to two different questions. Paul is optimising the scarcest input at a 16-person company (founder hours) and accepting a worse answer on the second. That trade only makes sense if you believe the product itself can carry growth — which is exactly the bet the rest of the interview describes. 45:38 · 38:31

🧵 Running Thesis

Running Thesis — "no token" as an identity, not a phase. Status: confirmed across both founders and, as of this writing, still holding. FOMO's June 2026 Series B raised $75M in equity, at a $550M valuation, from Index Ventures, Union Square Ventures and Benchmark — three firms with no structural need for a token. 📱 "Think of Yourself as Building a Consumer App": Ren's Distribution Doctrine

"I think if I thought of this as we're building a crypto app, we've lost." — Ren 04:06

Ren opens the stream and gives the clearest statement of FOMO's positioning anyone offers all day. Asked why FOMO is growing, he names two causes: the social layer, and the product itself 00:18–01:10.

The Frame. The whole growth function collapses to one substitution — consumer app, not crypto app. He walks the funnel explicitly: "I need a net new normie, someone who has no clue about crypto. I need him to be able to see some piece of content on Instagram or TikTok, go to our app store page, see that it's something that they're interested in, download, onboard, trade" 04:19–04:34.

Why It Matters. The contrast case is the crypto-native acquisition pipeline, which he describes with obvious contempt for its intellectual laziness: "hey, here's some free money, here are points to come use my app, and then you're going to figure it out because you have tried like 100 different protocols in the past few years and I don't need to explain anything to you" 04:44–04:55. Points programmes are, in his framing, a way of avoiding the product-explanation problem by paying people to solve it themselves.

The Product Complaint That Justifies the Company. "It's kind of crazy to think that in 2025 the bulk of crypto still traded crosschain by going to the bridge, sending the USDC to some other chain, having gas… it's really really crazy that no one built a really solid either mobile or web interface around that" 01:12–01:29.

🎯 Against Consensus

Ren's own job title argues against him and he says so. The founder cliché he cites — "first time founders focus on product, second time founders focus on distribution" — he calls "kind of just this thing founders say to make themselves sound smart and better" 02:14–02:16. His actual model is multiplicative, not sequential: great product with no distribution is invisible; great distribution with "a complete dog[shit] product" gives "zero reason to come use us" 02:40–02:44. This is a more honest answer than the aphorism, and it is the reason he can be Head of Growth and still say the product is the primary driver.

Both Sides of the Funnel, Weighted Differently. Asked whether he prioritises crypto-native conversion or normie onboarding, Ren answers "it's both" and explains why the two groups are complements rather than substitutes 05:10–06:17. The crypto natives supply the volume and the spectacle — he cites a trader putting on "a million dollar PENGU position in app over like one hour… just spam buying" 05:25–05:35. The normies supply the audience for that spectacle, and get a discovery layer in exchange: "instead of going to like [a] token screener, this third-party chat, this paid Discord group, they're just on FOMO. That's the discovery layer. That is all you need" 05:44–05:53.

⛓️ Unified Balance and the Chain-Season Lottery: Why FOMO Won Base and BNB and Lost ETH Mainnet

The Chain Order and the Lottery. Solana at launch, then Base, then BNB Chain, then Monad 33:37–33:41. 💡 Key Insight

Key Insight — "we sponsor gas" is the sentence that explains the whole chain roadmap. Paul mentions it in passing 35:09, but it converts chain support from an engineering decision into a unit-economics decision. On Solana or Base, sponsored gas is a rounding error. On ETH mainnet it is $20–30 per user action, which is more than the lifetime revenue of most normie accounts. FOMO's chain-expansion order is therefore not "biggest chains first" — it is cheapest-to-subsidise first, and ETH mainnet is last precisely because it is expensive, not because it is hard. That reframing is the useful version of this section, and it goes unstated on camera.

🎯 Against Consensus

The prevailing crypto-product view in 2025–26 is chain-maximalist distribution — pick an ecosystem, take its grants, ride its season. FOMO's stated position is the opposite: "for us to win we always need to be focused on onchain and we need to have the best onchain trading app that ever existed and have every chain available" 35:46–35:58. Being chain-agnostic means never receiving ecosystem subsidy and never getting the co-marketing, which is a real cost. It also means the company survives any single chain's winter. This is the same structural bet Robinhood made against being a single-exchange front end, and it is the reason the "chain season lottery" reads as a feature rather than a fluke.

📰 "2006 Facebook, 2026 FOMO": Say's Push-vs-Pull Thesis, and Two Different Protest Numbers

"All they did was, instead of the data being something that existed in a passive place, they ended up just pushing it. So it ended up from pull data to push data." — Say 102:15–102:26

This is the intellectual centre of the video, and it happens twice: once at 62:40 when rasmr reads Say's essay aloud on stream, and again at 101:08 when Say tells the story himself.

The Story. Before September 2006, Facebook was a pull medium — "you had to visit their profiles… okay, relationship status, oh married, like when did that happen" 101:16–101:23. The News Feed made every update push. "As soon as your relationship status went from in a relationship to single, that went out to everybody that was actually your friend" 101:36–101:43. The backlash was immediate and enormous; Zuckerberg published what the essay calls a "now famous blog post. Calm down, breathe. We hear you" 63:10–63:12; the company held the line; "it ended up being the thing that drove Facebook to 1000x growth pretty quickly" 102:46–102:48.

The Named Concept — "practical obscurity". Say uses a precise term and it deserves its own callout, because it is the mechanism the whole analogy runs on: "all it did was take this concept of practical obscurity — which is like something exists, it's just hard to find, or it's not in your face — and they made it into a feed" 102:28–102:35. Nothing new was disclosed. The cost of finding what was already disclosed went to zero. That is the entire event, and it is the entire FOMO product.

🔍 Source Verified

⚠️ Both protest figures given on stream are too high, and the two contradict each other. Facebook's News Feed launched 5 September 2006. By 8 September 2006 — three days later — the largest protest group, "Students Against Facebook News Feed", had passed roughly 744,000 members, with contemporaneous reporting describing "well over 700,000". Facebook's user base at the time is generally put at roughly 8–12 million. That yields about 6–9% for the single largest group, and a somewhat higher figure if every protest group is aggregated. Say's essay claims "12% of the entire user base joined petition groups against the feature within 3 days" 62:57–63:01 — defensible only on a generous aggregate-groups-plus-low-denominator reading, and unsourced. Say's spoken recollection — "some ridiculous number like 20% of all users signed a petition" 102:03–102:07 — is not supported by any source located and is roughly 2–3× the verifiable figure. Zuckerberg's response post is real and is correctly characterised. The mechanism is right; the magnitude is inflated in both tellings, and the two tellings do not agree with each other.

The ambition attached to it is the largest number anyone states: "how do you cultivate an environment that is not just like a 10,000-person [private group] and something where you can get 10 million, 100 million, a billion people to come and partake in the social economy" 104:34–104:42.

📜 Analog

Say's "it shouldn't be an edge to properly sleuth on chain" is the same argument as the decimalisation and Reg NMS transitions in US equities, and he half-makes it himself 25 minutes later: "edge in the stock market used to be, can you go to whatever building and write in your order faster than other people… those are things that shouldn't be real edges" 132:48–133:01. In both cases an infrastructural advantage — physical proximity then, wallet-tracking tooling now — is competed away, and the surviving edge is "your ability to trade the thing, develop the thesis" 133:03–133:06. The historical record on this is mixed in a way he does not mention: removing one infrastructural edge in equities did not remove infrastructural edges; it relocated them to co-location and latency. There is no reason to expect on-chain to behave differently.

💡 Key Insight

Key Insight — FOMO's product is a wallet tracker sold to the people who were being tracked. Read 104:02 and 56:07 together and the business becomes legible. Say built the transparency layer because he had the tracking edge; Paul defends it on the grounds that the alternative is "that person would hold that much supply in a side wall. You wouldn't know, you would buy, and then that person would dump on you" 56:18–56:26. The pitch to the small trader is: we are giving you the surveillance apparatus that was previously used on you. The unstated cost is that you must now be surveilled in return, by name, in public, on a leaderboard — which is exactly the trade Facebook's News Feed made in 2006. Say's analogy is more exact than he says out loud.

🪟 "There's No FOMO Cabal": The Transparency Objection, Answered and Half-Conceded

"What you're saying is basically give me less information and less transparency. And then there's no world where less transparency is not good." — Paul 56:07–56:15

The Objection. rasmr puts the standard trencher complaint to Paul directly: FOMO makes it harder for coins to run, because "you see someone's up a million dollars" 55:53–56:00. He then adds his own version, which is subtly different and sharper: "it's just like another glass ceiling. It's like coins can't go over 50 million" 56:29–56:33.

Paul's Answer — the counterfactual, not the feature. His reply does not defend visible P&L on its merits. It compares it to what happens without it: "historically what would happen is that person would hold that much supply in a side wall. You wouldn't know, you would buy, and then that person would dump on you. So if anything it's just protecting users" 56:18–56:29. The information existed; only the access changed. This is the same practical-obscurity move Say makes about Facebook 102:28, arrived at independently in a different segment.

The Concession, Volunteered. Paul then gives the strongest version of the criticism himself, which almost nobody does on a sponsored stream: "there's this thing where, oh it's the same FOMO cabal. First of all, there's no FOMO cabal. We've never paid anyone to be on FOMO" 56:41–56:46 — but — "because we are such a small social graph right now, like 500,000 total users, there's a heavy weight in the distribution of the people who are top traders on the platform" 56:51–57:01. His fix is dilution by asset class: once equities, prediction markets, perps, memecoins and majors coexist, "the people trading these different types of assets becomes much more diverse… those people will have their own influence and their spheres of influence, but less global influence" 57:09–57:22.

The Growing-Pain Example That Proves the Discipline. He offers a decision that cost them growth: users asked to import wallets from web terminals, "and we never did it because we wanted to build our social graph organically. Now that would have shot ourselves in the foot if we had done that, but it would have actually helped us grow faster at the time" 57:32–57:45. Say confirms the same policy independently 65 minutes later: sign-in is Apple/Google only, because "we don't want you to be able to import a wallet and have all this activity elsewhere. It doesn't really contribute to the social graph" 122:00–122:05.

💡 Key Insight

Key Insight — refusing wallet import is the single hardest product decision FOMO made, and both founders name it unprompted. Wallet import is the standard growth cheat code for a crypto app: it instantly imports history, balances and credibility, and it is what every terminal competitor offers. Declining it means every user's track record starts at zero inside FOMO, which is slower — and which is precisely why the resulting social graph is not fakeable. This is a cost-to-fake argument in the same family as costly-signalling: the graph is trustworthy because building a position in it is expensive. Neither founder uses that vocabulary, but it is the mechanism, and it is why Say can say "there's never going to be a more valuable social graph in existence" 53:41 without it being pure hype. 57:32 · 122:00

🌐 Consensus Check

Paul's "no world where less transparency is not good" is a strong claim and the finance literature does not fully agree with it. Post-trade transparency is well supported: the TRACE reforms in US corporate bonds are the canonical case, and reduced trading costs for retail followed. Pre-trade and position-level transparency are much more contested — visible large positions invite front-running and predatory liquidation-hunting, which is the documented mechanism behind the public-position blow-ups on Hyperliquid that the market watched through 2025. FOMO publishes something closer to position-level than post-trade data. rasmr's "glass ceiling" framing 56:29 is therefore the more sophisticated of the two positions in the exchange, and Paul answers a different, easier version of it.

🏆 The Leaderboard Is the Product Surface, and Both Founders Know It Currently Rewards Size

"You want the leaderboard to be both aspirational and inspirational." — Paul 53:39–53:41

Paul's Design Frame. He splits the objective in two: the board must be hard enough that reaching it means something, and reachable enough that people believe they can. His own gloss inverts the conventional definitions of the two words — "all time is more inspirational. 24-hour is more aspirational because it's easier to be the best trader on a 24-hour basis than an all-time basis" 53:55–54:02 — but the underlying design point is clear regardless of the labels: you need at least two time horizons so the board has both a hall of fame and an open lane.

The Structural Flaw, Named by rasmr. The host is the one who identifies the real problem: "it's usually people who deposit more money that end up at the top. It's people making bigger trades" 104:45–104:50. Concretely: "you size 100K, if you go up 50% on a coin, you'll be number one for 24 hour" 104:57–105:01. It is a capital-weighted ranking wearing the clothes of a skill ranking.

Say's Answer. He accepts it immediately — "definitely" — and names the fix: "we want a localized leaderboard. So we want it to be percentage based. We want it to be maybe even regional based" 105:15–105:21, with further slicing by asset, by chain, or "maybe it's just mobile versus web" 105:25–105:27. The design principle: "at the end of the game you want everybody to feel like they can have a chance and to compete in their own rights" 105:33–105:39. Paul had gestured at the same thing 50 minutes earlier — "maybe you have sub-leaderboards of equities or sub-leaderboards of prediction markets" 54:05–54:11.

Say does push back on rasmr's premise once, and correctly: "that's not easy to do, right? If somebody could do that every day, they'd be able to make eight figures in very short time" 105:03–105:07. Sizing does buy you the top of a 24-hour board; sustaining it does not.

Why the Leaderboard Is the Business, Not a Feature. Paul makes the boldest claim in the video about it: "the most valuable place for you to ever build a social graph will be on FOMO, because that's the place where people could actually see the conviction… there's never going to be a more valuable social graph in existence" 53:31–53:45. The mechanism is verifiability: an equities influencer discussing an earnings call can post a thesis and the position behind it, so "they know I'm not blowing smoke up their ass. They know I'm not just saying buy this. They see that I actually own it" 55:18–55:22.

🔗 Connection

Leaderboard design 53:39 → the no-wallet-import rule 57:32 → credible neutrality 119:49. These are one system. The leaderboard is only worth competing on if the graph is un-fakeable (hence no imports), and the graph is only trustworthy if the house is not playing (hence founders holding no public positions). Break any one and the other two lose their meaning. This is the closest thing FOMO has to an architecture, and it is assembled from three separate segments 60 minutes apart.

⚡ Tension

Say tells rasmr the leaderboard should reward skill rather than size, and wants it percentage-based 105:19. Twenty minutes later he predicts the future leaderboard will showcase "some of the most euphoric P&Ls that you've seen" across asset classes, with people "tweeting about I just made $50 million on oil" 125:28–125:40. Those are opposite products. A percentage-based, regional, small-account-friendly board is a participation surface; a $50M-P&L board is a spectacle surface. Both are strategically defensible. They are not the same board, and nobody asks which one wins.

🧊 Credible Neutrality: Why the Founders Don't Trade in Public, and What They Do Instead

"We try to remain very credibly neutral. We don't really hold positions on our own accounts." — Say 119:49–119:51

The Question. rasmr sets it up with a genuinely good observation: there is a founder archetype in crypto that becomes "like a cult leader… and a lot of it happens when you have a token", where "everything you say there's a coin" 118:38–118:55. He names two examples; the captioner renders them as "Avi Patel or like Alain" 118:46 — [unclear — the second is most likely "Alon", the Pump.fun co-founder, given the context and the immediately preceding Pump.fun discussion; the first is not confidently identifiable]. His question: have you decided against becoming that, or are you waiting and seeing? 119:13–119:17

Say's Reasoning — Pandora's box, not principle. He does not answer on ethics. He answers on irreversibility: people shill because "it benefits them, their company, their product, their ecosystem", but "it's impossible to keep everybody happy… hey, you didn't buy my coin, or why did you sell, or you didn't buy enough, or you don't talk about it enough. And once you open Pandora's box, it's very hard to put it in" 119:31–119:48. The costs are unbounded and permanent; the benefit is a one-time bump.

The Free-Market Caveat. He explicitly declines to police what others do with his name: people "will tokenize anything you say or anything you post… I think that is actually a feature not a bug. The world is free, finance is free, and you should be able to do what you want" 119:58–120:09 — with one condition — "you shouldn't get mad if somebody else decides that this is not worth something, or the person doesn't endorse it" 120:13–120:17.

The Part Worth Sitting With. rasmr asks whether Say should trade more on the app, since he wants "more pings" 118:09–118:12. Say's answer contains the whole policy and its shadow: publicly, "say bought the coin" creates a slippery slope 118:16–118:22. Privately, "I like buying things, I like holding things and I don't really sell. So I do have many accounts on FOMO that I just like buy things and hold, and people won't have to know it's me. And that's great. That's the beauty of FOMO. You can be whoever you want" 118:22–118:34.

⚡ Tension

Tension — this is the sharpest unresolved contradiction in the video, and nobody notices it. FOMO's entire pitch is that on-chain pseudonymity was an unfair edge and the fix is a public, identity-attached social graph (Say at 104:02: "I knew everybody's wallet whether you knew it or not… it kind of shouldn't be an edge"). Fifteen minutes later the same founder describes maintaining multiple unlabelled accounts on his own platform specifically so that "people won't have to know it's me" 118:31, and calls that "the beauty of FOMO". Both statements are defensible in isolation — a founder's positions genuinely would distort the market, and optional pseudonymity is a real product feature. Together they say: transparency is the product for users, and optionality is the product for the house. rasmr asked a friendly question and got an unusually candid answer; neither follows it up.

🎯 Against Consensus

The default crypto-founder position is that public conviction is a duty — you should be visibly in your own ecosystem, and holding nothing reads as not believing. Say inverts it: holding nothing publicly is the precondition for the platform being trustworthy. This is closer to how a stock exchange or a broker thinks about proprietary trading than to how a token issuer thinks about alignment, and it is consistent with the no-token decision 45:38, the refusal to run a launchpad 53:07, and Paul's "we've never paid anyone to be on FOMO" 56:44. It is a coherent regulatory-adjacent posture assembled without anyone using the word "conflict of interest".

🔍 Source Verified

✅ The SEC guidance Say alludes to is real and the timing fits. The SEC issued staff guidance in January–February 2026 clarifying that tokenised stocks remain subject to existing securities and derivatives rules regardless of whether they sit on a blockchain, and drawing a sharp line between issuer-sponsored tokenised securities (which can represent true equity ownership) and third-party products offering synthetic exposure or custodial entitlements. Further interpretive guidance on crypto-asset classification followed in April 2026. Say's characterisation — "exciting for interfaces" — is the optimistic read: the guidance clarified the rules rather than relaxing them, and notably did not explicitly resolve whether non-custodial systems escape the Customer Protection Rule (Rule 15c3-3), which is precisely the question FOMO's model turns on. His hedge — "we're still figuring it out" 49:48 — is doing real work.

The Custody Answer, Which Is the Whole Legal Posture in Two Sentences. Asked whether equities on FOMO would be FDIC-insured 126:51, Say's reply is the clearest statement of the company's regulatory position anywhere in the video: "we don't really touch your funds. It's decentralized, it's permissionless. We're not a money transmitter in any way. So it's not really something for us to be able to secure even if we wanted to" 127:12–127:21. Any protection would come from whichever tokenisation partner sits behind the asset — he names Robinhood and Ondo 127:07–127:09.

⚠️ Reader Caution

Reader caution — this is a legal characterisation by a founder, not a legal conclusion, and it is doing a lot of load-bearing work. "Non-custodial, therefore not a money transmitter" is a widely asserted position in crypto and has been repeatedly tested; the SEC's own 2026 tokenisation guidance explicitly declined to confirm that decentralised systems using non-custodial wallets fall outside the Customer Protection Rule. Nothing here should be read as settled.

Prediction Markets — Wanted, With a Real Caveat. Say calls them "something that's also inevitable" 50:45. rasmr's pitch is a filter tab for "who's up the most on prediction markets" to copy-trade 50:53–51:01. Paul gives the best technical objection in the video, and it is the one moment where a founder tells the host his idea is partly wrong:

Copy-trading a memecoin is reflexive — "you see someone buy it on the feed and then you tail them, the price keeps going up" 51:11–51:16. Prediction markets are not: "prediction markets [have] a fair value… there's a probability that's certain of whether he'll win the election or not. There's a fair value there that market makers will kind of arbitrage" 51:18–51:28. So tailing size into a thin market destroys your own execution: "if you go in and that market's illiquid and buy a bunch of it, then it actually moves the price. And if you don't wait for a market maker to come in and reprice the market fairly, then I'm actually getting bad execution" 51:31–51:41. His worked example: a true 50% resolves at 75% after your order, and "I'm getting priced at 75% when the true value is 50%" 51:57–52:01.

His alternative framing for what social adds to prediction markets: "what's more important and socially relevant is people's preferences and then also how the markets are breaking" 51:06–51:11 — narrative, not copy-trading.

💡 Key Insight

Key Insight — Paul just described why FOMO's core mechanic breaks on bounded-payoff instruments, and the implication is bigger than he lets on. The copy-trade loop works when the asset has no fair value to anchor to — memecoins, where the crowd is the valuation. It degrades on anything with a defensible price: prediction markets (bounded 0–1), equities (earnings, comparables), majors. As FOMO expands into exactly those asset classes to dilute its "cabal" problem 57:09, it is expanding into instruments where its social layer is worth less as a trading signal and more as pure content. That is not fatal — content is a fine product — but it means the leaderboard's function changes meaning as the asset mix broadens, and nobody in 135 minutes says so. 51:18 · 57:09

💡 Key Insight

Key Insight — the interesting claim isn't "AI writes code", it's that a growth function became a shipping function. Ren's role is distribution 02:03. 🎯 Against Consensus

His mitigation is unremarkable and effective: hard scope limits (cosmetic and list-level changes only), a human engineer on every merge, and an explicit carve-out for "the important stuff" 13:37. This is the boring version of the practice, and boring is why it works. Note the one thing he does not claim: that AI wrote anything in the trading path, the wallet path, or the cross-chain solver.
