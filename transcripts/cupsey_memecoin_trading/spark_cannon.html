🎯 "Attention and Catalyst": The Whole Method in One Sentence

"I was going to say like attention and catalyst. That's literally all that matters." 29:04

rasmr had barely finished asking what makes a token go up. Cupsey cuts in with the answer, and it is the closest thing to a stated framework in the entire interview. There is no technical analysis, no market-structure vocabulary, no risk model. The two inputs are:

Attention

Is the thing going viral outside crypto? His read on the $67 coin is the purest example: "the only reason I was hot on 67 is cuz it's just a coin that deserved so much higher because of how viral it was" 25:42. He is not valuing a token; he is pricing a meme's distribution and asking whether the market cap has caught up to it yet.

Catalyst

Is there a discrete event that forces the attention to arrive? For $67 to run again, he says, it needs "the main main adoption again — like if Trump and Elon started talking about it" 26:15. Absent a catalyst, virality alone decays.

His two favourite trade types fall directly out of this:

  1. Narrative runners / tweet coins — the attention leg. 07:31
  2. Launch information from wallets — "finding like info on some type of launch… you know with wallets like, oh yeah, this guy dev'd this coin" 07:37. This is the catalyst leg, run pre-attention: identify the deployer by wallet history, and you are early by construction rather than by speed.
🌐 Consensus Check This is aligned with, not contrarian to, how the academic and on-chain research literature now models memecoin pricing — as an attention asset with no cash-flow anchor, where price is a function of social distribution and reflexivity rather than fundamentals. Where Cupsey departs from the literature is operationally: researchers describe the dynamic; he has built a sixteen-hour-a-day pipeline for detecting it before the crowd. The insight is common; the execution loop is not.
⚡ Tension At 26:44 he tells a hypothetical TikTok-scrolling beginner who spots the next $67 to "just buy the coin and hold" — and rasmr immediately catches it: "So you would tell them to do exactly what you don't do ever." Cupsey's defence is thin ("I do it sometimes" 26:53), then he lands on the real reason: "with those TikTok plays… they take a long time to cook" 26:58. The resolution is that he is prescribing a different strategy for a different player — a beginner with no execution speed, no VC, and no wallet-tracking should be trading a slower, higher-conviction timeframe. That is genuinely good advice; it is simply not his advice for himself.
🧵 Running Thesis (Cupsey) "Tokenized attention has just become a thing now — it'll always happen when something goes viral." 07:19 Status: confirmed and load-bearing. Everything else he says — why the trenches survive a bear, why pump.fun revenue persists, why chains are worth fighting over — is downstream of this one claim. 🕹️ The Fortnite Thesis: Reflex Games Produce Trenchers, School Doesn't

"Definitely Fortnite. Definitely not school." 03:26

Asked where his trading intelligence came from — grades, AP scholar, being a student — Cupsey answers without hesitating. And he sharpens it into a genuine claim about selection:

"The trenching game is basically just like it's like Fortnite… the buying and holding type traders, you'll notice those aren't the Fortnite players. The Fortnite players are like the hard hard trenchers." 03:42 03:50

He names Scented and himself as the archetype. The claim is not "gaming makes you smart" — it is that high-frequency competitive gaming and trenching select for the same trait bundle: sub-second decision-making under incomplete information, tolerance for thousands of repeated low-stakes outcomes, and comfort with a loop that resets constantly. Buy-and-hold, by contrast, selects for patience and conviction — a different personality entirely.

The same analogy governs his answer on whether a newcomer can still make it:

"Yes. But it's going to take a lot of effort… take it with Fortnite for example. You hop on and you go against someone who's been grinding every day for two years, you're just going to get destroyed. Like, you're going to have to build yourself up for a while." 08:06
🔗 Connection The Fortnite frame → the infrastructure question the interview never asks. If trenching is a reflex game, then latency is edge, and latency on Solana is bought: a fast terminal, a private RPC endpoint, priority fees, and — for launch snipes — Jito bundles. Cupsey is asked repeatedly about his setup and answers only in consumer terms (a $300 chair, an $8K PC, two monitors). The single most decisive variable in a speed game is the one nobody in the room thinks to ask about. Treat the setup answers as incomplete, not as the whole picture.
🎯 Against Consensus The prevailing narrative around top on-chain wallets is that they are bots, or bot-assisted. Cupsey rejects it flatly and personalises the rebuttal: "the only person who said that was Mitch and it's because he doesn't actually trade… Mitch doesn't sit there and trench" 27:57. He then half-concedes the point anyway — "I guess I feel like I work like one" 46:18. The honest read: the decisions are plausibly manual; the execution rails almost certainly are not, and the interview never separates the two.

This is the most important sentence in the interview for anyone trying to learn from him, and it goes by in four seconds. Every public statistical portrait of Cupsey — the four-minute average hold, the tens of thousands of flips, the "enters at launch, exits within a minute" description — is a portrait of a prior version of his strategy. He is telling you, unprompted, that he has moved.

Three corroborating details in the same conversation:

  • "I've had a lot of buy and holds recently." 12:00
  • On $67: "I just swung it at a high market cap" 25:34 — a swing, not a flip.
  • His sizing advice is built around a "high cap" of "a few mil" 38:53, not around brand-new pairs.

Why the shift? He doesn't say directly, but the market context is on tape: a bear, dead flow, and the observation that "there's just more like multi multi-wallet dev farms… more like new pair rape" on Solana now 18:49. When the new-pair layer is saturated with deployer-controlled wallet clusters, the edge in racing them shrinks — and moving up to midcaps, where "it's the same game" 19:05, is a rational retreat to a less-crowded battlefield.

💡 Key Insight — regime adaptation This is the most transferable thing he says. He did not find a better strategy in the abstract; he moved when his old edge got crowded. The generalisable rule is not "hold longer" — it is "track where competition is concentrating and step one layer away from it." The same logic drives his Robinhood-rotation trade below. Two different expressions, one underlying move.

Both can be true — 500/day may be a reduction from his peak, and it may include tiny sizing on a wide net — but "buy a lot less" should be read as relative to a previously insane baseline, not as selective.

"When you're in VC, there's like multiple people, there's multiple eyes… when there's a bunch of different shit coming out and you have like five different people looking at everything, it's like we don't really miss anything. Someone's like 'Yo, look at this.' And then we all look at it together… Or someone sees something on a tracker." 47:01 47:13

The economics are explicit: "we all just like help each other make more money. That's what we do. The entry barrier is the part he can't help with: "you have to either have friends or find a way to meet people." 46:43

🔗 Connection VC-as-sensor-array → the denominator problem below. The single largest structural disadvantage a retail trencher has is not capital or speed — it is being one pair of eyes against a five-person network with tracker coverage. Every "why do I always see it thirty seconds late?" is answered here. The published base rates are, in effect, measuring solo players against networked ones.

"When Robin Hood was hot, no one was looking at Soul, so I was able to hit every single cook… they're insanely free to hit. Like I don't miss a single coin." 17:30 17:49

This is the sharpest structural trade in the interview and he delivers it as an aside. When Robinhood Chain launched and pulled the crowd's attention, Cupsey did the opposite of chasing it — he stayed on Solana and harvested a venue that had temporarily lost its competitors.

The trade-off is stated honestly: "the ceilings are lower" 18:01 — fewer participants means smaller maximum outcomes — but not always. He cites Jimothy reaching ~$45M market cap 18:06 during exactly that window, and it was his largest single P&L of the year at $60–70k 19:35. Fewer competitors, more time to enter, a hit rate near 100%.

He extends the point into a genuinely good observation about chain tribalism: "People don't realize it's like the same traders, but a different chain… It's all the same people. Like, it doesn't really matter." 18:36 The one real difference he identifies is at the new-pair layer — more multi-wallet dev farms on Solana — but "once you get up to the midcaps, it's the same game." 19:05

📜 Analog This is the oldest edge in trading in a new costume — provide where nobody else is providing. It is the market-maker's quiet-hours spread, the options desk's illiquid-name premium, the value investor's un-covered small cap. The mechanism is identical: competitor density, not asset quality, sets the return. Cupsey arrives at it empirically, from watching a Discord empty out.

🔎 Reading a Launch: Supply Control and Spotting the Bundle

"You can look at the chart and you can tell if there's like supply control — and if those early entries, like 3, 5k, 10k entries, are selling, it's probably the bundle selling." 51:37

The one concrete, replicable analytical technique in the interview. The read is two-step:

  1. Supply control — read the chart shape for evidence that a small number of wallets hold a dominant share of float. A chart under supply control behaves differently from an organically distributed one: it does not bleed, it steps.
  2. Bundle exit signature — identify the earliest entries by size bracket (he names ~$3k, $5k, $10k as the tells), then watch whether those specific entries are the ones selling into strength. If they are, the deployer's own wallet cluster is distributing, and the run is over regardless of how the chart looks.

He also flags the structural context in the same conversation: "there's just more multi-wallet dev farms or whatever on Soul than there is on Robin Hood" 18:49.

🌐 Consensus Check His method matches documented practice, though the professional toolkit goes further. A "bundle" on Solana is a set of transactions submitted atomically to a block builder (Jito) — all execute in sequence or none do. A deployer bundles the liquidity-creation transaction together with buys from 20+ of their own wallets in block zero, capturing a dominant share of supply before any outsider can transact. Cupsey's chart-and-entry-size read is the fast, eyeball version. The rigorous version adds funding-graph tracing (walk each early buyer's SOL back — if the paths converge on one wallet cluster, it's a bundle) and wallet-age checks (bundle wallets are typically minted hours or minutes before launch with no prior history). His method is directionally correct and fast enough to use live; the graph method is the one that actually proves it.
🔍 Source Verified ✅ Coordinated deployer wallet clusters are systematic, measured, and enormous — his "dev farms" comment understates it. A 2026 study using data from over 160,000 Pump.fun launches identifies 1,012 persistent wallet cohorts (2–12 wallets each) that systematically co-fire as early buyers across multiple, unrelated launches. Separately, in one recent 30-day window, more than 15,000 Solana token launches on Pump.fun were manipulated by deployer-backed sniper wallets. This is not an occasional bad actor; it is the ambient structure of the new-pair layer, and it is a substantial part of why Cupsey moved up to midcaps.

📉 The Denominator: What the Population Data Says

Neither speaker raises this once in 57 minutes. It is the single most important context the interview does not contain, so it belongs here — beside his method, not instead of it.

Everything above is an accurate description of what one exceptional operator does. None of it is evidence that the method generalises. Cupsey is a single observation drawn from the extreme right tail of a distribution, selected because he is in that tail. Here is the distribution.

🔍 Source Verified — the base rates ✅ From on-chain data rather than self-report. Dune Analytics, across 13.55 million Pump.fun wallet addresses: only 55,296 (≈0.41%) have realized more than $10,000 in profit. Only 0.048% have realized above $100,000. And 0.00217% — roughly 293 wallets — have ever realized more than $1 million. Cupsey is inside that 293. The platform itself earned 2,016,391 SOL (~$398M) by 2 January 2025 regardless of how those wallets fared. Two honest caveats on the data, raised by on-chain analysts at the time: realized P&L excludes unrealized holdings, and post-bonding purchases on Raydium are omitted — so the true count of profitable wallets is likely somewhat higher than the headline. It is not higher by two orders of magnitude. (Dune Analytics via Cointelegraph/CoinMarketCap, 2025.)
🔍 Source Verified — the monthly picture ✅ Better than the lifetime picture, and the difference is instructive. CoinGecko data puts 73.28% of Pump.fun traders profitable in April 2026, the best rate in at least two years and the fourth consecutive month above 50%, up from a low of 30.08% in June 2025. But read the size distribution: of ~3.143 million wallets, ~2.047 million (65.1%) made between $1 and $500, and only ~169,000 (5.4%) made over $1,000. So the honest statement is: in a good month, most participants finish green — by an amount that does not change their life. Meanwhile more than 99% of Solana memecoin buyers end at a net loss over any longer horizon, and the same window that produced 73% monthly profitability also had >15,000 launches manipulated by deployer-backed sniper wallets in 30 days. The distribution is not "most people lose" — it is "most people win pennies, almost nobody wins meaningfully, and a few hundred wallets take the tail." (CoinGecko; Dune; academic launch-cohort analysis, 2026.)
💡 Key Insight — what survivorship bias actually costs you here The failure mode is not "Cupsey is lying." Nothing in this interview reads as fabricated, and his self-reported figures are notably more conservative than his host's. The failure mode is inferring a process from an outcome. With ~13.55M wallets running, a purely luck-driven process would still produce hundreds of eight-figure winners, and each of them would give a coherent, internally consistent account of their method — because humans always can. What would distinguish skill from tail-luck is not present in this interview and cannot be: his loss distribution, his drawdowns, his months in the red, his P&L path. He is asked for his biggest P&L 19:08; he is never once asked for his worst. Treat the method as a hypothesis with one supporting anecdote, not as a validated system.
🎯 Against Consensus — his sizing advice, put beside the base rate Asked what someone with $1,000 to their name should trade with, Cupsey says: "All of it." 38:25 He immediately adds the qualifier that actually carries the advice — "but obviously don't be putting in $1,000 into coins"; per-position size is "like $50 or 100 if it's like a high cap [a few million] or super confident" 38:44 38:53. So the real instruction is 5–10% of bankroll per position, ~10–20 concurrent positions of risk capacity — which, as a position-sizing rule in a game with 5x+ outcomes at roughly 1-in-1,000, is aggressive but not incoherent. The part the base rates argue with is the premise, not the fraction: put the whole $1,000 on-chain and, on the population data, the modal outcome is finishing somewhere between +$1 and +$500 or down the lot — and the 5.4%-above-$1,000 cohort is the ceiling most participants are actually playing for. His fraction is defensible. His denominator is somebody else's problem.
🧵 Running Thesis (analytical, not the speaker's) The interview's own structure is the bias in miniature. Every question is "how much did you make," "what's your biggest," "who's the best." Not one is "what's your worst month," "what's your drawdown," "how many of the people who started when you did are still here." Status: confirmed — the format selects for the tail and then interviews it about being the tail.

🧰 The Techniques, Broken Out

🎯 The Attention-and-Catalyst Screen — His Entire Stated Framework

Two inputs, no third: is it going viral, and is there an event forcing that virality into the market right now?

What It Includes

Virality assessment (is the meme spreading outside crypto — TikTok, mainstream feeds, television?) and catalyst identification (a launch, a named-account post, an adoption event).

The Practice (Step-by-Step)

(1) Detect a meme with real distribution. (2) Compare the distribution to the current market cap — his $67 read was "a coin that deserved so much higher because of how viral it was" 25:42. (3) Identify what forces the repricing: a catalyst, or a second virality wave ("if Trump and Elon started talking about it" 26:15). (4) With no catalyst, expect decay.

The Evidence

$67 → ~$40k profit, swung at a high market cap 25:34. Independently, $67 reached a $40M+ market cap on a 10x run, driven by a "6-7" meme that spread across TikTok/YouTube/Instagram/television — the virality was real and measurable.

Where It Breaks

No downside rule is ever stated. He describes what makes a token go up and never once describes what makes him exit at a loss.

🎧 The VC Trading Desk — Five Pairs of Eyes

A small, trusted, reciprocal voice call is his most important piece of infrastructure, and it costs nothing.

Live Discord VC throughout the session; multiple people watching different trackers and surfaces; shared callouts.

The Practice

(1) Assemble a small group of people who actually trade — "you have to either have friends or find a way to meet people" 46:43. (2) Stay in voice, not text, for the whole session. (3) Split coverage — someone on the tracker, someone on new pairs, someone on socials. (4) Call out, look together, decide individually. (5) Read the overnight backlog on waking 36:19.

VC ≠ Telegram group. "Telegram group's different than VC" 47:55 — closed reciprocal voice, not a broadcast channel you paid to join.

🔎 The Bundle Check — Is the Deployer Selling?

Read the chart for supply control, then watch whether the earliest size brackets are the ones distributing.

What It Includes

Chart-shape read + early-entry size analysis.

The Practice

(1) Assess whether the chart shows supply control rather than organic distribution. (2) Isolate the earliest entries by size — he names ~$3k / $5k / $10k as the tells. (3) If those entries are selling into strength, assume the bundle is distributing and treat the move as over. 51:37

The Rigorous Extension (not his, but the professional version)

Trace each early buyer's SOL funding backwards — convergence on one cluster confirms a bundle — and check wallet age, since bundle wallets are typically created minutes-to-hours pre-launch with no history.

What It Includes

The Practice

(1) $1,000 bankroll → all of it on-chain 38:25. (2) Never $1,000 into one coin — "obviously don't be putting in $1,000 into coins" 38:32. (3) $50–100 per position 38:44. (4) That upper size is reserved for a "high cap" ("a few mil" market cap 38:53) or genuine conviction.

🌒 The Off-Peak Rotation — Trade Where the Crowd Isn't

When attention leaves a venue, its opportunities become free to hit; take the lower ceiling in exchange for the higher hit rate.

The Practice

(1) Watch where attention has migrated (a new chain, a new launchpad). (2) Stay on the venue it left. (3) Accept smaller maximum outcomes for a near-100% hit rate — "I don't miss a single coin" 17:49. (4) Reassess when the crowd returns.

The Evidence

During the Robinhood Chain surge, Jimothy ran to ~$45M on Solana 18:06 and produced his largest single P&L of the year, $60–70k 19:35.

The Generalisation

Competitor density sets the return, not asset quality. The same move underlies his shift from new pairs to midcaps.
