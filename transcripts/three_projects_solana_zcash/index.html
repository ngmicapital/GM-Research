⚡ The Bottom Line

  • Mumtaz screens assets on exactly two criteria — "physics and incentives" — and uses them to rank Solana and Zcash as high conviction and NEAR as a smaller, explicitly higher-risk asymmetric bet. Everything else in the interview is downstream of that two-variable filter, including his dismissal of Ethereum's L2 strategy as "fighting physics."
  • His answer to "why own SOL if the chain succeeds but the token doesn't capture value" is that block space is not a commodity. Liquidity is sticky, state is impossible to replicate, and anyone trading competitively on Solana — his example is a Citadel or a Jane Street racing to place a trade first — must hold and spend SOL to win the fee auction.
  • He predicted with "100% certainty" that Solana's inflation-reduction proposal would pass around autumn, roughly two months from recording. It passed in late August 2026 — narrowly, at 67% of participating stake against a two-thirds threshold. The compute-proportional burn he floated as a second step has since become a live proposal.
  • On Zcash he separates two things the market conflated: a genuine four-year counterfeiting bug in the Orchard circuit, and a fake "Zcash is down" panic caused by block explorers that had not updated their nodes. The turnstile mechanism caps the worst case at pool insolvency, not infinite supply.
  • The calls have aged extremely well in eleven weeks. ZEC traded around $450 with a $6–7B market cap at recording; it is $1,115 and $18.9B today, helped by a Grayscale spot ETF he never mentions. SOL has gone from roughly $60 to $101. Neither move was predictable from his arguments, but both are consistent with them.

🧭 Two Variables Decide Everything: "Physics And Incentives"

Mumtaz's framework emerges from a real decision, which makes it more interesting than an abstract heuristic. With Solana's price collapsing, Hyperliquid ascendant and Helius being paid to expand onto other chains, he had to decide whether his company stayed Solana-native 17:59.

"There's only two things that are important that are fundamentally important. One is physics and two is incentives." 18:40

Physics means the architecture does not evade the hard problem. His charge against Ethereum: "I would argue that Ethereum's L2 approach fights physics, because if you don't scale the core engine, then fundamentally, you're limiting the scaling of everything else. And so, you're moving physics around, but you're not actually improving — it'll come back at you at some point unless you tackle it head-on" 18:56.

Incentives means the sum of the parts exceeds the whole. His worked example is a new Solana launch, World XYZ: as an RPC provider, he benefits from its success, so he distributes it — and so do the wallets, the DEXs and the foundation. The contrast: "Ethereum L1 loses value capture to the L2s, but then the L2s don't benefit from one of the other L2s succeeding, and so they're in this knife fight against each other" 19:29.

🎯 Against ConsensusThe modular-versus-monolithic debate is usually argued on throughput. Mumtaz reframes it as an incentive-design failure — not that rollups are slow, but that they are structurally hostile to one another and to the chain that hosts them, which caps how much any of them can be helped by the rest. 19:29

The conclusion he draws is deliberately narrow. If a chain has both properties, "the only thing remaining are solvable problems that require entrepreneurs and builders to push forward" 20:08. Physics and incentives are the two things you cannot fix later; everything else is execution.

He applies the same filter to buying assets, and is blunt that it is the opposite of rotation trading:

"I don't do this game of what's everybody talking about and then maybe let's do some rotation games. It's more like does it have the physics? Does it have the incentives? Is it a distressed asset? Is it slept on? Is there asymmetrical upside given that basically nobody knows what they're talking about? If the answer is yes, then that's your edge." 30:16
💡 Key InsightThe framework is really a two-stage filter — the first stage (physics, incentives) is technical and binary; the second stage (distressed, slept on, asymmetric) is a market-structure test about whether the technical fact is priced. Both must be true. This is why he can be fundamentally bullish on something and still say the price is wrong to buy it. 30:22

His stated inspiration for the company-level version of the decision is Jeff Bezos, to whom he says he "always look[s]... for advice on most company related things" 18:27 — the lineage being to identify the things that will not change and build around them.

📉 Why Solana Fell: Hyperliquid, A Foundation Rotation, And A Trader-Dominated Narrative

Kevin lays out the arc bluntly: SOL ran to roughly $260 in 2021, collapsed to $8 after FTX, ran from $8 to roughly $300, and then fell about 80% back to around $60 09:53. Mumtaz's explanation has four parts and only one of them is about Solana.

First, the market fell as a whole. He describes watching total crypto market cap slide on a site he was vibe-coding — "every day is just check price, awful price" — from $3 trillion toward $2.7 trillion, with Bitcoin falling from a high of about $120K to as low as around $57K 12:06. His estimate: the market lost about $2 trillion in combined market cap 13:08.

Second, Hyperliquid's rise, which he attributes to two genuinely new speculative surfaces. Geopolitical instability made commodities tradeable in a way they had not been for crypto users — "imagine how interesting it is for traders to just leverage trade oil without KYC on the internet based off of monitoring the situation on Iran or whatever Trump tweets" 13:55. And the AI pre-IPO wave created demand for price discovery on unlisted equities, where perpetual futures fit naturally — he cites Cerebras price discovery appearing on Trade.xyz before the Nasdaq listing 14:44.

🔗 ConnectionAI capital formation → crypto market structure. The same private-market boom that drained risk appetite from crypto also created the pre-IPO perp market that pulled attention to Hyperliquid. One macro force produced both the headwind and a competitor. 14:26

Third, doubts about SOL's monetary policy — the issue the rest of the episode is largely devoted to answering 15:05.

Fourth, and least discussed publicly, leadership rotation at the Solana Foundation. Mumtaz says it was comparable in scale to the Ethereum Foundation changes that received far more coverage, though "it wasn't a net outflux on Solana. It was more like a rotation" 16:01. The departure that worried him was Akshay BD — "the most competent foundation type person that I've seen in all of crypto, and it's not even close" 17:02, the person who coined Internet Capital Markets and organised Breakpoint. Kevin discloses that a conversation with the same person is why he put 80% of his portfolio into SOL in summer 2023 16:30.

🧵 Running ThesisNarrative supply, not fundamentals, set the price during the drawdown. Mumtaz admits he "maybe stopped talking about Solana as much" and was instead shipping infrastructure nobody discusses, which removed the loudest counterweight to bearish sentiment at exactly the wrong time. Status: developing — it is also a convenient explanation for a price decline. 15:16

His characterisation of the discourse is unsparing: "CT is very much a high school Travis area. It's monkey see, monkey do. Somebody copies somebody else's sentiments, and now it's everybody else's sentiments. Independent thinking is very rare" 15:30.

🌐 "Global Silicon Valley": Builder Flow As The Leading Indicator Price Cannot See

Mumtaz's positive case for Solana is not a metric — it is a claim about where startups choose to build, and about who can see that choice happening.

"Solana is very clearly the obvious choice. So, how come people aren't seeing it this way? It's because you're getting your source from CT, and CT is dominated by traders." 28:00

His two tracked indicators are developer numbers, visible because he runs a developer platform, and startup formation, visible because he is an angel investor. "The trader mind does not see those deals... they have no idea that there is tremendous builder interest" 25:05. He dates the uptick to mid-spring, heading into Consensus in Miami 25:28.

💡 Key InsightThis is an information-asymmetry argument rather than a valuation one. Pitch flow leads revenue by one to two years and leads price by more; the people who see it cannot prove it, and the people who can verify price cannot see it. That gap is precisely where he claims his edge sits. 25:05

The framing he wants to replace "L1 token" with is geographic: Solana as a global Silicon Valley — "the most usable, scalable, and battle-tested chain with the highest concentration of products, startups, and talent" 32:52. The meritocratic version is the strongest part of the pitch: an internet connection, a Helius plan, a Superteam membership, and "you can be from Bangladesh, you can be from Estonia, it does not matter what your name is, what your father was" 20:59.

His test of the process working is a survivorship argument: "let's look at the last kind of few giant companies in crypto. Axiom, Pump.fun, Magic Eden, Phantom... Jupiter. Now, they're not all on Solana, but compared to Solana's market cap versus the others, surely the highest per capita of greatest companies come from Solana" 27:33. He couples that with the portfolio maths that makes ecosystems work: "even if 90% of them fail, you just need 10% to succeed wildly" 47:11.

🌐 Consensus CheckThe market's 2026 objection to Solana is not that nobody builds there — it is that the activity is disproportionately speculative, and that memecoin volume is not the same as durable economic activity. Mumtaz does not engage that objection directly anywhere in the interview; he answers a different question, about whether builders are arriving at all. 27:33

The competitive elimination he walks through for a founder choosing a chain is worth reproducing because it shows how narrow he thinks the field is. Bitcoin: no. Base: "base was down three times last week. And also, do you trust Coinbase?" 26:12. Sui: "still quite early and it's also going down a lot cuz scaling is hard as Solana found out years ago" 26:35. Hyperliquid: only if you are building an exchange. Which leaves Solana as the default for anything general-purpose 26:42.

⚔️ "Solana Doesn't Compete With Hyperliquid" — And Hypercore Is A Distribution Play, Not A Chain

The question Mumtaz was asked most during the drawdown was what Solana should do about Hyperliquid. His answer is that the question is wrong.

"Solana doesn't compete with Hyperliquid. What would happen is Solana needs to organize the chain such that builders on top of Solana have everything they need to then compete with Hyperliquid." 22:43

The reasoning is about core competence. The Solana Foundation "is certainly not a quant mastermind like Jeff" and cannot win a market-microstructure fight; Anatoly Yakovenko's competence is scaling distributed systems, so that is what the chain should optimise, leaving the exchange problem to teams like Phoenix 22:58.

His assessment of Hyperliquid itself is respectful and pointed: "I compare it much more to perhaps a much better Binance than a very successful blockchain" 21:48. On the chain layer specifically: "if you look at Hypercore, the blockchain itself, it doesn't really do too much. It's more about using the exchange to get front-end integrators to distribute via builder codes like Phantom" — which he immediately calls "perfectly valid, by the way" 22:07.

⚡ TensionAt 21:36 he says Hyperliquid "certainly could" replicate Solana's physics-and-incentives combination; at 21:48 he characterises it as an exchange rather than a blockchain. He is describing a snapshot rather than a ceiling, but the two statements pull in opposite directions on the only question that matters for a multi-year holder.

The most useful admission here is about Solana's own past inadequacy. Asked whether a team could have built a Hyperliquid competitor on Solana a year earlier, his answer is no — "because the chain didn't work well enough." A risk engine would have required "weird shortcuts such that the fundamental tech wouldn't be as competitive in the final stage" 23:37.

📋 Protocol Cheat Sheet
  • Screen an L1 on two binary questions first: does it confront scaling directly, and do ecosystem participants benefit from each other's success?
  • Then apply the market test: distressed, under-covered, asymmetric. Fundamentals without mispricing is not a trade.
  • Watch startup formation and developer counts as leading indicators; watch price as a lagging one.
  • For any "chain versus chain" narrative, ask whether the competitor is a general platform or a single application with a token — the answer changes what winning looks like.
  • For privacy assets, check whether the shielded pool has a turnstile bounding supply, and whether the circuit has been formally verified.

💰 Why Own SOL At All: Block Space Is Not A Commodity

Mumtaz concedes the bear case before answering it. Asked why anyone should hold the token even if Solana becomes the most-used chain, he says: "to give weight to the critics, I think that's a valid criticism" 33:33. He notes that Yakovenko's own answer — the token exists to price scarce resources and prevent spam — is "technically true, but that's not super interesting" 33:51, and characterises both Yakovenko and Vitalik Buterin as builders whose brains "operate on such different dimensions" that they do not care what an investment thesis looks like 34:20.

He also declines the loose comparables. Referencing a target from the trader Ansem — that Ethereum's all-time-high market cap of roughly $600B would imply SOL near $990 — Mumtaz calls it "probably reasonable" by that logic, then adds: "But there is no real things that connect the dots" 36:11.

His own argument has three legs.

Competitive execution requires the asset. Helius runs what he describes as the largest RPC platform and the largest trading infrastructure on Solana, giving him a direct view of professional flow. If Ken Griffin's firm wants to act on the same Bloomberg data before Jane Street, "you need to have some SOL and a good amount such that you actually do win that trade... those add up over the course of many different hours and days. That's how Robinhood makes money, how Citadel makes money" 37:43.

Block space is sticky, not fungible. This is the line that carries the section:

"Block space is not a commodity. I hate when people say this... Is it? Okay, so why don't you go do your trades on Cardano then? It's also a blockchain, it has block space. Why aren't you doing it there?" 38:51

The supporting evidence is Ethereum's persistence: stablecoins and activity remain there despite throughput he puts at roughly 12 TPS, because "liquidity is extremely sticky, state is impossible to replicate" 39:13. His broader framing is that "Solana is an economy," and economics is the study of allocating scarce resources — block space is never infinite, so the native asset is the allocation mechanism 38:27.

Costs and rewards both accrue in SOL. Storage is a real expense at scale — opening an account costs about 30 cents, trivial for a user but material if you are Circle opening a million USDC accounts 40:05. Meanwhile staking returns MEV, trading fees and inflation protection 40:22.

🔒 The Zcash Bug: A Real Four-Year Flaw, A Fake Outage, And One Concession

Kevin asks Mumtaz to clear the Zcash FUD "that happened a couple of weeks ago," noting that Tushar Jain of Multicoin called it a non-event 48:32. Mumtaz's account separates three things the market merged into one.

The audit programme came first, and it was deliberate. Zcash has multiple independent teams — he names Zcash Foundation, Shielded Labs and several others — and in anticipation of AI making bug discovery cheaper, they chose to "really ramp up security, auditing, and finding these things in the open before any bad actor can find them" 49:05. The bug was found by an established researcher he places among the top three people most familiar with Zcash, not by someone with a coding assistant 49:59.

The "Zcash is down" panic was false. After the patch, some block explorers had not updated their nodes. Mumtaz says he woke up, opened his wallets, sent a transaction that confirmed, and checked node and mining stats — all live. "The block explorer itself is not updated, but the chain is working fine" 51:03. He compares it to early Solana: "a bunch of trolls just saying [things] to milk a few Elon bucks" 51:45.

The bug itself was real, and the turnstile is what bounds it. It was a counterfeiting bug that in theory permitted minting shielded notes inside the Orchard pool. His correction to the panic version: "you cannot print infinite Zcash because there's a thing called a turnstile" 53:55. His analogy: the pool is a coffee cup, and only the liquid that went in can come out. What an attacker could do is print notes inside the pool, withdraw through the turnstile, and leave other depositors unable to withdraw — making the pool insolvent rather than inflating supply 54:01.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed in detail. A soundness vulnerability in Orchard's zero-knowledge circuit — in the elliptic-curve components of the Halo 2 proving system — was discovered in late May 2026 by independent researcher Taylor Hornby during analysis supported by Shielded Labs. It had sat in the proof circuit for four years and could theoretically have allowed undetectable creation of counterfeit notes inside the shielded pool. The fix, Ironwood (NU6.3), activated on 28 July 2026 at block 3,428,143, creating a new shielded pool and restricting Orchard largely to withdrawals, which must pass the turnstile. Roughly $1.7 billion of shielded value was sealed behind that boundary. Sources: The Block; CoinDesk; Crowdfund Insider.

His confidence that no exploit occurred is heuristic and he labels it as such — "I'm 99% confident that didn't happen because the price went up, price went down, the shielded pool kept increasing in size, which would not happen if somebody was draining it the whole time" 54:29. The definitive answer comes from Ironwood: as funds migrate through the turnstile, a discrepancy would reveal itself 55:12.

💡 Key InsightThe concession is the most valuable thing Mumtaz says about Zcash, and he volunteers it: "I think people when they invest in Zcash didn't actually understand what private money means. If the money is private, that by definition means it's private. Meaning there are some trade-offs you make in supply versus auditability" 53:11. Privacy and supply auditability are in genuine tension; the turnstile is the engineering compromise, not a way out of it. 53:11
🌐 Consensus CheckThe bear reading — that a privacy chain cannot prove its own supply and therefore cannot be trusted at scale — is not addressed by the turnstile so much as bounded by it. Mumtaz's rebuttal to the loudest version of the attack ("they paid influencers with infinite money") is a market-logic one: infinite selling pressure would not coexist with a rising price 54:50.

🚀 Formal Verification: "The Facebook Approach Versus The Space Flight Approach"

This is the section that generalises beyond Zcash, and it is the strongest intellectual contribution in the episode.

"There's two ways to build software, serious and unserious." 56:15

Unserious software is Facebook: ship a feature, find a bug, patch it, nobody loses money. Serious software is spaceflight or pacemakers, where "if you write bugs, either somebody dies or billions of dollars are lost" 56:23. His indictment: "crypto has been built, a large part of it at least, using the Facebook approach versus the space flight approach. And with AI, that just won't cut it anymore" 56:51.

The remedy is formal verification — machine-checked mathematical proof that a function behaves exactly as specified. Mumtaz claims that as of the conversation, Ironwood and the Orchard pool had already been formally verified, and additionally audited using advanced models, "to show that mathematically speaking, which is the highest possible guarantee you can get in software, that the same class of bugs cannot exist again" 57:01.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed, with a number he does not give. The Ironwood pool shipped with a machine-checked proof comprising more than 2,700 theorems, establishing that it cannot create undetectable counterfeit ZEC under its design assumptions. This is genuinely the strongest guarantee available in software engineering, and Zcash is among the first major consumer-facing crypto assets to apply it at this scope. Sources: The Block; Cryptonews coverage of Zcash formal verification.

His conclusion is that the episode left the asset stronger: "this has done — is bullet, made Zcash much more anti-fragile than before... while there's all these other bugs lurking in the shadows and all these different ecosystems, this is the ecosystem that took all of its resources and did the extremely difficult work of proving mathematically" 58:00. And he generalises it into a prediction: "fundamentally this is how all of crypto will need to evolve... all serious teams in crypto will have to do that" 58:39.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ His headline example does not support the argument. Mumtaz cites the Drift hack alongside "like 12 DeFi hacks" as evidence that AI is raising exploit frequency. Drift Protocol was drained of roughly $285–286 million on 1 April 2026 — the largest DeFi hack of 2026 and the second-largest Solana incident after Wormhole's $326M in 2022 — but the attack was social engineering and privileged access, not a code flaw found by a model. Attackers posed as a quantitative trading firm for months, then exploited Solana's durable-nonce mechanism to get Security Council members to blind-sign dormant transactions that transferred admin control. TRM Labs and Elliptic attribute it to DPRK-linked actors. Formal verification would not have prevented it. The general thesis about AI-assisted vulnerability discovery stands on its own; this particular case is the wrong evidence for it. Sources: Elliptic; TRM Labs; Chainalysis.
📜 AnalogThe spaceflight comparison has a real precedent worth knowing. Formal methods entered mainstream engineering through exactly the domains Mumtaz names — avionics, rail signalling, medical devices — where the cost of a defect is unbounded and unrecoverable. His argument is that crypto has the same loss profile ("you put a billion dollars in there, well if it's gone you might as well just die") while having adopted consumer-web engineering practice, and that this mismatch is finally being priced. 59:00

🎯 The Zcash Price Case: 10% Of Bitcoin As A Floor, Not A Target

Asked about the $10,000 figure he is associated with, Mumtaz starts from the commonly cited target — Zcash at 10% of Bitcoin — and argues it is too modest.

He sets the baseline himself: "currently it's at I think 6 to 7 billion market cap and I think it's about 450 in price" against Bitcoin at "like what 1.5 trillion so 150 billion" for the 10% mark, noting that ZEC was then smaller than Dogecoin and Tron 59:34.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ His own arithmetic is loose, though the direction is clear. On air he computes "a 10x for Zcash would be 70 billion, which would mean 4,000." But 10% of a $1.5T Bitcoin is $150B, which from $6–7B is closer to 21x, implying roughly $9,000–10,000 per ZEC — the number Kevin actually asked about. The 10x and the 10%-of-Bitcoin figures are two different targets stated as one. Today ZEC trades at $1,115 with an $18.9B market cap, roughly 1.2% of Bitcoin's $1.559T — up from about 0.45% at recording, with Bitcoin's own market cap essentially flat. Source: CoinGecko, 14 September 2026.

The qualitative argument is stronger than the arithmetic. Once provable soundness is established, he asks: "why would you not want your Bitcoin to be private? What do you gain by simply not having the option to shield your funds?" 60:03. The answer he reaches is that a transparent-only asset is "strictly inferior technologically speaking" — optionality that costs nothing to hold 60:18.

Two supporting arguments follow. Fungibility: "if a coin has history, then it's not fungible. It can be frozen. See the Canadian truckers" 61:12. And positional privacy: "why would you want to disclose your positions to people who just should not know them?" 61:20. His compressed political framing — "the antidote to AOC is ZEC" 61:36 — is the most rhetorical moment in the episode and signals that part of the thesis is a bet on rising state and fiscal intrusiveness rather than on technology alone.

🎯 Against ConsensusThe standard institutional objection to privacy coins is regulatory: delistings, travel-rule friction, and exchange risk. Mumtaz never addresses it directly, treating adoption as a storytelling and integration problem. The market has since answered him in an unexpected way — a US spot ETF for a privacy asset launched without the blocking he never argued against and never anticipated. 60:22
🔍 Source Verified✅ A catalyst he does not mention arrived and moved the asset. Grayscale launched the first US spot Zcash ETF, ticker ZCSH, on NYSE Arca on 25 August 2026 with Coinbase as custodian — the first spot ETP for a privacy asset anywhere. It opened holding roughly 387,000 ZEC worth about $260 million and reached $313 million within three days, at a 2.5% annual fee, roughly ten times a Bitcoin ETF's. ZEC crossed $1,000 for the first time in nearly a decade about two weeks later. Sources: GlobeNewswire (Grayscale, 25 Aug 2026); The Block; 24/7 Wall St.

He lists execution dependencies rather than treating the outcome as automatic: quantum recoverability and proving, a 5–10x scaling upgrade, a simpler proving pool, wallet and exchange integrations, and miner decentralisation 62:16. He closes with the use case that ties all three positions together: hold funds in a Zcash wallet, pay someone who only accepts USDC on Solana, routed through NEAR — "settles on Solana using Zcash as privacy at the core" 63:57.

🧪 NEAR: The Third Position He Flags As Undervalued While Holding Almost None

Kevin's question is direct — "I don't hold any NEAR coin. What am I failing to understand?" — and Mumtaz's answer is unusually well-hedged for a crypto interview.

"I don't think NEAR is there today, but I do think NEAR has a lot of potential. It's not as high of a conviction play that I have with Zcash and Solana." 65:00

He states plainly that he is "still forming my thesis around this" and that he does not have "a huge position or anything like this" 68:40. That candour is what makes the rest usable.

His evaluation criteria for any project, stated here for the first time explicitly, are "the pedigree and the determination and the resourcefulness of the founders" 65:25. On that test, Illia Polosukhin rates extremely highly: "the only guy I know in all of crypto that can go head-to-head with Anatoly in a technical battle," and a co-author of the paper underpinning modern AI 65:36.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed, with one label corrected. Illia Polosukhin is Ukrainian-born and one of the eight co-authors of "Attention Is All You Need" (2017), the paper that introduced the Transformer architecture — the group known as the "Transformer 8." Mumtaz calls it "the LLM paper"; it is more precisely the Transformer paper, which underpins today's LLMs. Polosukhin co-founded NEAR Protocol with Alexander Skidanov in 2018. Mumtaz's reference to co-founders "Bowen and Alex" is consistent with Bowen Wang and Alexander Skidanov. Sources: arXiv 1706.03762; Wikipedia; CNBC profile.

Mumtaz includes a self-critical anecdote that lends the assessment weight: Polosukhin was telling him about agentic AI in 2024 and "I just completely faded him... Oh, I probably should have trusted the author of the paper" 66:40.

On the technology, his claims are that NEAR scales, that it has an AI edge from both throughput and Polosukhin's domain knowledge, and that it delivered sharding — "which was Ethereum's original vision, by the way. Which Ethereum abandoned, but NEAR actually did it" 67:33. The use case he personally cares about is a genuinely on-chain, KYC-less cross-chain spot exchange, which is what makes NEAR the routing layer in his Zcash-to-Solana payment example 67:18.

The risk statement is unhedged: "I think they have a good amount of things to iron out. So it's a very high-risk play cuz it depends on them actually ironing those things out" 67:46.

💡 Key InsightHis actual reason for taking NEAR seriously is not technical at all — it is operational. "When I work with the NEAR guys, I'm like, these guys are pros. They're in there like a Navy SEAL team. They know what they want. They have deliverables. They work with you and then it gets done" 71:03. Kevin agrees that whether invoices get paid on time tells you more about a team than any pitch. Operational excellence as an investment signal is underweighted precisely because only counterparties can observe it. 71:03

The valuation argument is pure asymmetry and he states it as such: downside is roughly halving, upside is not a 2x "cuz look at his market cap. It's tiny. Look at what's above it" 69:19. His shortlist of chains that are actually used — Solana, Ethereum, Bitcoin, Zcash "to an extent," Hyperliquid, and NEAR — is the frame he wants that comparison made within 68:19.

🌙 The Operator Behind The Calls: Spiky Attention, Night Shifts, And An Accidental Distribution Advantage

Two threads from the opening are relevant to reading the rest.

First, how he allocates attention. He works nights, to bed at 4 or 5am, splitting work by cognitive type: writing, strategy and marketing at night; "for super analytical stuff, like actually looking at the numbers, it's better to do in the morning" 03:36. He describes his attention as "very spiky in how it's directed" 05:11, and names specific stakes — if a page goes unanswered, "90% of the Solana ecosystem is going to be in trouble, and as a result, so will my team, and as a result, so will my revenue" 05:25. Most of his actual work, he says, is invisible: "behind the scenes of unblocking things, getting things listed" 04:57.

Second, an honest answer about distribution that most people with large followings would dodge. Asked about roughly six-fold follower growth to 1.2 million on X and accusations of being an "industry plant," he explains the actual mechanism: X's starter packs. New accounts selecting crypto as an interest are shown a short recommended list that includes him alongside Anatoly Yakovenko, Vitalik Buterin and Robert Leshner. "It's a very good top of funnel" 07:54.

💡 Key InsightThe starter-pack disclosure is a small but genuinely useful media-literacy point. A large share of one prominent commentator's audience growth came from a platform default rather than from being right about anything — which is a reason to weight his arguments on their merits and discount follower count as a signal entirely. 08:21

His closing invitation frames how he sees his own role in the ecosystem: "if you're building a business in crypto, shoot me a DM on Twitter" 69:59 — consistent with the thesis that his edge comes from seeing builders before the market does.

Phase 3: Specific Item Breakdown

🟣 Solana (SOL) — High Conviction: A Whole Economy, Now With Monetary Policy To Match

Mumtaz's core position — the only general-purpose chain that passes both his physics and incentives tests, with a token thesis built on non-commoditised block space and a two-part monetary fix now half-delivered.

The Thesis: Solana is a "global Silicon Valley": the most usable, scalable and battle-tested chain with the highest concentration of products, startups and talent. The token captures that because block space is scarce and non-substitutable — competitive execution, drops, and account storage all require SOL, while staking returns MEV, trading fees and inflation protection. As the economy grows, the currency of that economy cannot fail to benefit.

The Evidence: A sharp uptick in Solana startup pitches from mid-spring 2026 that price did not reflect; the highest per-capita concentration of large crypto companies (Axiom, Pump.fun, Magic Eden, Phantom, Jupiter); an all-time high in transaction activity; and a chain now technically good enough to host a serious risk engine, which Mumtaz says was not true a year earlier. Independently: over 10 billion transactions in Q1 2026, and a record 216 million non-vote transactions in a day by August 2026.

The Catalyst: Doubled disinflation, which passed governance in late August 2026 at 67% of participating stake, pulling terminal 1.5% inflation forward to roughly 2029. A compute-proportional burn is the stated next step and is now a live proposal. Slot times are being cut in stages from 400ms toward 200ms — 300ms landed 28 August 2026 — with the Alpenglow consensus redesign targeting roughly 150ms finality at a mainnet date of October 2026. Product launches he names: World (on-chain prediction markets without collateral), Phoenix on-chain perps, JTX on-chain trading, Backpack listings, and Helius privacy tooling for institutions.

The Stated Risk: Macro, and explicitly outside Solana's control: "a lot of the smart people I talk to that are not in crypto... think that there will be an AI bubble pop heading into perhaps a Q4 or early next year. Now, if that happens, it doesn't matter if Solana is doing three trillion TPS." Beyond that, the thesis is an execution bet on an ecosystem of teams where he expects 90% to fail.

🟡 Zcash (ZEC) — High Conviction: Private Money With A Mathematical Supply Guarantee

The asset he found at $18 when it was outside the top 100, held through a genuine counterfeiting bug, and argues should be worth substantially more than the commonly cited 10% of Bitcoin.

The Thesis: Private money is a simple, legible product — "with Zcash, it's extremely simple. It's private money. That's it." Once supply soundness is provable, transparent-only assets are strictly inferior because holders give up an option that costs nothing to have. Fungibility (a coin with history can be frozen or surveilled) and positional privacy do the rest of the work, in a world he expects to become more fiscally and politically intrusive.

The Evidence: The scaling roadmap that first attracted him in late 2024 / early 2025; multiple independent core teams rather than a single foundation; a turnstile mechanism that bounds the worst case at pool insolvency rather than supply inflation; and formal verification of the new Ironwood pool — more than 2,700 machine-checked theorems establishing that the same class of bug cannot recur. He describes the whole episode as leaving Zcash more anti-fragile than before.

The Catalyst: Ironwood (NU6.3) activated 28 July 2026, sealing roughly $1.7 billion of shielded value and migrating funds through the turnstile — which is also the proof that no exploit occurred. He also lists quantum recoverability and proving, a 5–10x scaling upgrade, a simpler proving pool, and wallet and exchange integration. The catalyst he did not anticipate: Grayscale's ZCSH spot ETF, launched on NYSE Arca 25 August 2026, the first spot privacy-asset ETP anywhere, after which ZEC crossed $1,000 for the first time in almost a decade.

The Stated Risk: He concedes the fair criticism — investors bought private money without understanding the supply-versus-auditability trade-off it necessarily entails. His confidence that the bug was never exploited rests on heuristics at 99%, not proof, until migration completes. And he frames the remaining work as a storytelling and integration problem: "can we tell the story effectively enough?" He never addresses the regulatory delisting risk that is the standard institutional objection.

🟢 NEAR — Low Conviction, High Asymmetry: A Bet On Founders And Operational Excellence

The only one of the three where he openly says the thesis is unfinished and the position is small — recommended on founder pedigree, working sharding, and how the team behaves in practice rather than on any current metric.

The Thesis: NEAR passes both the physics test (it genuinely shipped sharding, which Ethereum abandoned) and the incentives test, and it is "one of the only things that stands out to me for how undervalued it is compared to the things above it." Its distinctive edge is AI — from throughput and from Polosukhin's domain knowledge — plus a cross-chain, KYC-less on-chain spot venue that makes it the routing layer in Mumtaz's own payments example.

The Evidence: Illia Polosukhin as one of eight co-authors of the Transformer paper and, in Mumtaz's assessment, the only person in crypto who can debate Anatoly Yakovenko on technical ground; co-founders including Alexander Skidanov and Bowen Wang; and NEAR's inclusion in his shortlist of chains that are actually used. The decisive evidence for him is behavioural: "these guys are pros. They're in there like a Navy SEAL team. They know what they want. They have deliverables."

The Catalyst: Attracting builders. "Now it's about can they attract builders to help them solve the solvable problems" — which he considers "a very doable job" relative to bootstrapping another rollup. He notes Helius does not currently work on NEAR, and jokes about Yakovenko watching.

The Stated Risk: Stated more plainly than for either other asset: "a very high-risk play cuz it depends on them actually ironing those things out," with the thesis still forming and no meaningful position held. He also concedes the obvious counter — that the assets above NEAR may simply be overvalued rather than NEAR being cheap.

🔵 Hyperliquid — Rated As A Superior Exchange, Not A Competing Blockchain

He credits it with genuinely winning attention and enabling new speculative markets, then argues the comparison to Solana is a category error that the market keeps making anyway.

The Thesis: "I compare it much more to perhaps a much better Binance than a very successful blockchain." Hypercore does comparatively little as a chain; the model is using the exchange to attract front-end integrators who distribute via builder codes — which he calls perfectly valid. The strategic consequence is that Solana should not try to compete; it should make itself a place where someone can build a competitor.

The Evidence: Its rise coincided with two new speculative surfaces: geopolitics-driven commodity speculation with leverage and no KYC, and pre-IPO price discovery for AI companies — with Cerebras price discovery appearing on Trade.xyz ahead of the Nasdaq listing as a case where the crypto venue arguably led. Both are exchange use cases, not general-platform ones.

The Catalyst: Not discussed as a position. The relevant catalyst is on the Solana side: whether a team — Phoenix on-chain perps and JTX on-chain trading are the ones he names — can now build competitively, which he says was not technically possible on Solana a year earlier.

The Stated Risk: He concedes directly that Hyperliquid "certainly could" acquire the physics-and-incentives combination he treats as Solana's moat. That is the single largest unhedged risk in his Solana thesis and he leaves it unresolved.

⚫ The Rest Of The Field — Base, Sui, Ethereum And Bitcoin, Eliminated In Turn

His founder's-eye elimination round, which does more work in the argument than any of his positive claims about Solana.

The Thesis: For anyone building a general on-chain business, the practical options collapse quickly. Bitcoin: not a platform. Base: reliability and trust concerns — "base was down three times last week. And also, do you trust Coinbase?" Sui: "still quite early and it's also going down a lot cuz scaling is hard as Solana found out years ago." Ethereum: the L2 approach fights physics and splits incentives.

The Evidence: Ethereum's persistence is used as evidence for his own case rather than against it — stablecoins and activity remain there despite throughput he puts at roughly 12 TPS, which he offers as proof that block space is sticky and non-fungible rather than a commodity.

The Catalyst: None offered; this is an elimination argument, not a set of positions.

The Stated Risk: A one-sided assessment from an interested party. Every claim here is a judgement call rather than a measurement, and the Base and Sui dismissals rest on point-in-time observations.
