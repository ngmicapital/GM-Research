What is the CLARITY Act? A Complete Explainer

Source Law of Code · 2026-05-18 · 114 min
Domain Law, Policy & Financial Regulation
Reliability Expert explainer — the host reads from the actual Senate text and section numbers, and eight named practitioners who worked on the bill supply the commentary.
Content Type Explainer
Full Mode

⚡ The Bottom Line

  • CLARITY does not overrule Howey — it borrows Howey's own language, lowers the threshold, and uses it to sort tokens rather than to impose liability. Congress could have rewritten the 1946 investment contract test. Lewis Cohen's view, which the drafters seem to share, is that the complexity was not worth it.
  • The central invention is a token that sits inside the securities disclosure regime without being a security. An "ancillary asset" is a network token whose value still depends on a founding team. The asset stays a CFTC-regulated digital commodity; only the originator's own sales are treated as an investment contract distribution, and only the originator carries disclosure duties.
  • The bill answers the question ninety years of case law never reached: what happens to a non-security after it is sold in a securities transaction. Orange groves and chinchillas had no secondary markets, so no appellate court ever had to decide. Tokens trade continuously among strangers.
  • Certification is the exit door, and its procedure matters more than its standard. Deemed approval after 20 business days, with denial requiring a non-delegable vote of all five SEC commissioners, reverses the default that made the last decade so painful.
  • The bill failed on the Senate floor four months after this episode. On 15 September 2026 cloture on the motion to proceed failed 49–50 against a 60-vote threshold, with ethics provisions the decisive rift. The statutory analysis here remains accurate; the timing optimism did not survive the summer.

🔑 Key Claims (Ranked)

1. The gap was jurisdictional, not interpretive — nobody had authority over spot digital asset markets ↳ Evidence: The CFTC regulates derivatives on commodities, not the commodities themselves; the SEC's exchange rules assume securities. A spot venue had no federal registration door. 17:04 ↳ Caveat: The default in that gap was not deregulation but a fifty-state patchwork, which Dugan Bliss says the draft preempts. 32:55 ↳ Context: Coinbase petitioned the SEC for rulemaking in July 2022 and was refused; the Third Circuit later found the denial's reasoning insufficient.

2. A network token can pay its holders without becoming a security ↳ Evidence: Section 105 lists four circumstances that are not "disqualifying financial rights," including protocol-level fee accrual and governance capability. 44:00 ↳ Caveat: An enforceable contractual claim still disqualifies; Sarah Brennan's right versus interest line is the operative test. 41:46 ↳ Context: This is the provision that makes fee switches legal and ends the era of deliberately inert tokens.

3. Lowering the efforts threshold pulls more tokens into the regime, deliberately ↳ Evidence: Miles Jennings notes the bill uses "nominal efforts" where Howey used "efforts," so nearly any startup still building falls inside the perimeter. 66:59 ↳ Context: The organising principle is that ongoing effort creates information asymmetry, and asymmetry — not asset type — is what triggers regulation.

4. Disclosure is semi-annual, front-loaded, and cannot be cured after the fact ↳ Evidence: New Securities Act § 4(b) requires filings before any triggering offer or sale, then every six months. Four triggers, including the first post-effective-date secondary market sale. 76:43 ↳ Caveat: A Category C catch-all makes the enumerated list a floor, not a ceiling. 79:00 ↳ Context: Exchanges may file on a token's behalf — which solves the orphaned-token problem and makes venues partial gatekeepers.

5. Certification's deemed-approval default is the most consequential drafting choice in the bill ↳ Evidence: SEC silence for 20 business days approves; denial requires a public vote of the full Commission and cannot be delegated to staff. 83:40 ↳ Context: Under the status quo an agency could stall indefinitely and silence meant no. CLARITY inverts that and makes refusal institutionally expensive.

6. Agency rulemaking is not a substitute for a statute, and the vulnerability runs both ways ↳ Evidence: Bliss notes that the SEC's own crypto rulemaking rests on a Howey reading that the exchanges' litigators spent years attacking — so the same argument could unwind a friendly Commission's rules. 36:45 ↳ Context: Statutes survive administrations; rules face Administrative Procedure Act challenge on any significant action.

7. The Senate architecture replaced the House's countable decentralisation test with a behavioural one ↳ Evidence: H.R. 3633 as passed used "mature blockchain system" with a sub-20-percent insider ownership element. The Senate text uses network token plus ancillary asset plus certification. ↳ Caveat: The Senate approach is harder to game and harder to advise on — the recurring trade the whole bill makes.

8. Stablecoin yield was the four-month blockage, and the compromise is in tension with itself ↳ Evidence: Section 404 extends the GENIUS Act ban from issuers to exchanges and platforms, then permits activity-based rewards calculated by balance, duration or tenure. 96:27 ↳ Caveat: Balance-and-duration is the definition of deposit interest; the line depends entirely on rulemaking that does not yet exist.

9. A US token sale is taxable income; an equity raise is not ↳ Evidence: Jason Schwartz — the buyer gets no claim on the company and the company incurs no liability, so proceeds are simply income. "You think you're raising a hundred bucks, you're actually raising 80." 107:00 ↳ Context: CLARITY removes the securities-law reason to go offshore and leaves the tax reason fully intact.

10. Ethics, not token taxonomy, decided the bill's fate ↳ Evidence: Senator Gallego voted it out of committee while saying his vote did not guarantee a floor vote, with ethics guardrails the "toughest and most critical" open item. 98:38 ↳ Context: Cloture failed 49–50 on 15 September 2026, with reporting identifying enforcement independence in the ethics language as the unbridgeable gap.
