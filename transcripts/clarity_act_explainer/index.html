What is the CLARITY Act? A Complete Explainer

⚡ The Bottom Line

  • CLARITY does not overrule Howey — it borrows Howey's own language, lowers the threshold, and uses it to sort tokens rather than to impose liability. Congress could have rewritten the 1946 investment contract test. Lewis Cohen's view, which the drafters seem to share, is that the complexity was not worth it.
  • The central invention is a token that sits inside the securities disclosure regime without being a security. An "ancillary asset" is a network token whose value still depends on a founding team. The asset stays a CFTC-regulated digital commodity; only the originator's own sales are treated as an investment contract distribution, and only the originator carries disclosure duties.
  • The bill answers the question ninety years of case law never reached: what happens to a non-security after it is sold in a securities transaction. Orange groves and chinchillas had no secondary markets, so no appellate court ever had to decide. Tokens trade continuously among strangers.
  • Certification is the exit door, and its procedure matters more than its standard. Deemed approval after 20 business days, with denial requiring a non-delegable vote of all five SEC commissioners, reverses the default that made the last decade so painful.
  • The bill failed on the Senate floor four months after this episode. On 15 September 2026 cloture on the motion to proceed failed 49–50 against a 60-vote threshold, with ethics provisions the decisive rift. The statutory analysis here remains accurate; the timing optimism did not survive the summer.

Executive Summary

  • CLARITY does not overrule Howey; it builds a parallel track around it and then routes most tokens onto that track. Congress could have amended the 1946 investment contract test outright. Lewis Cohen's view, which the drafters appear to have shared, is that the juice was not worth the squeeze — so the bill instead borrows Howey's own "entrepreneurial or managerial efforts" language, lowers its threshold, and uses it as a sorting mechanism rather than a liability trigger. 47:56–48:38
  • The load-bearing innovation is that a token can be inside the securities disclosure regime without being a security. An "ancillary asset" is a network token whose value still depends on a founding team's efforts. The asset stays a digital commodity under CFTC jurisdiction; only the originator's own offers and sales get treated as an investment contract distribution, and only the originator carries disclosure and anti-fraud duties. Cohen repeats the point twice for emphasis: "still not a security." 54:26
  • The bill answers the question the case law never reached: what happens after a non-security is sold in a securities transaction. Ninety years of appellate Howey litigation involved orange groves, chinchillas and whiskey warehouse receipts — assets with no secondary market. Digital assets trade continuously among strangers, and the existing doctrine simply has no answer for the fifth buyer down the chain. CLARITY's answer is to put the burden on the person who raised the money, not on downstream holders. (51:15, 52:20)
  • Certification is the exit door, and its procedural design does more work than its substantive standard. An originator certifies that no covered party has engaged in "more than nominal" entrepreneurial or managerial efforts in the preceding 180 days and that all material information is public. The SEC then has 20 business days to object — by a vote of the full five-member Commission, which cannot be delegated to staff. Silence equals approval. (81:22, 83:40, 84:00)
  • Two committees, two bills, one floor vehicle — and that structure is a jurisdictional fact, not a drafting choice. The Senate Banking Committee owns the SEC; the Senate Agriculture Committee owns the CFTC. Because digital assets implicate both, the market structure package has to be assembled from two separately marked-up texts under Senate Rule 14 before it can be voted on as one bill. (89:22, 91:55–92:10)
  • The bill's fate was never really about token taxonomy. By May 2026 the classification architecture was substantially settled and the remaining fights were about stablecoin yield, developer liability, and — decisively — ethics rules for elected officials and their families. Senator Ruben Gallego voted the bill out of committee while stating plainly that his vote did not guarantee a floor vote. Four months later, that turned out to be the whole story. 98:38

Dating note. This episode was recorded across the weekend of 16–17 May 2026 and published on 18 May, days after the Senate Banking Committee reported the bill. Everything the speakers say is framed as of that moment, when the bill looked closer to enactment than any market structure bill before it. It is not the moment the bill is in now. On 15 September 2026 the Senate failed to invoke cloture on the motion to proceed, 49–50, against a 60-vote threshold. The analysis of the statute below is unaffected — the text is the text — but read the optimism about timing as a snapshot, and see the final themed section for where things actually stand.

⚖️ The Gap Was Not Ambiguity — It Was That Nobody Had Jurisdiction Over Spot Markets

Robinson frames the problem as a question of missing rules rather than hostile ones, and the mechanism is worth getting exactly right.

"The laws don't allow these products to exist. And that might sound extreme, but it's true."

The United States has two market regulators with two statutes. The Securities and Exchange Commission administers the Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, which cover securities. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission administers the Commodity Exchange Act, which covers derivatives on commodities — futures, swaps, options 16:11–16:24.

Robinson makes a distinction that a surprising number of commentators get backwards, and it is the hinge of the entire jurisdictional problem:

"The CFTC doesn't regulate the commodities themselves. They wouldn't regulate Bitcoin itself, but they regulate the financial contracts for future delivery that derive their value from the asset." 17:04

That is correct and it matters enormously. If Bitcoin is a commodity, the CFTC gets the Bitcoin futures market. It does not get the Bitcoin spot market, because the Commodity Exchange Act gives the Commission general anti-fraud and anti-manipulation authority over spot commodities but no registration regime for spot venues. So an exchange that custodies and matches actual Bitcoin trades sat in a gap: too commodity-like for the SEC's securities-exchange rules to fit, outside the CFTC's registration perimeter entirely.

🔍 Source Verified✅ The CFTC's exclusive jurisdiction under Commodity Exchange Act § 2(a)(1)(A) runs to contracts for future delivery, swaps and commodity options traded on registered venues. Its spot-market authority is enforcement-only — fraud and manipulation under CEA § 6(c)(1) and Rule 180.1 — with no licensing or registration hook. This is why a spot digital-commodity exchange had no federal registration door to knock on, and why the CLARITY architecture has to create new registrant categories rather than just reassign existing ones. Source: Commodity Exchange Act, 7 U.S.C. § 1 et seq.

Dugan Bliss adds the layer people forget: in the absence of a federal regime, the default is not "no regulation," it is fifty regulations.

"First of all, what you have in the US right now is without clarity a patchwork of state regulation first and foremost." 32:55

Some states run comprehensive digital-asset regimes; most do not; and states diverge on whether a licensed exchange may offer staking at all. Bliss notes that the draft he reviewed contains federal preemption to collapse that patchwork, so that a business is not subject to inconsistent rules depending on whether a customer drives twenty miles across a state line 33:32–33:46.

The industry's own conduct undercuts the "crypto wants a free pass" framing, and Robinson is visibly irritated by it. Coinbase petitioned the SEC for rulemaking in July 2022, having already published a policy proposal a year earlier, arguing that the existing market rules were structurally incompatible with the technology rather than merely burdensome 29:15–29:47.

🌐 Consensus CheckCoinbase's petition for rulemaking was filed on 21 July 2022 and asked the Commission to propose rules for digital asset securities. The SEC took nearly a year to respond; Coinbase then sought a writ of mandamus in the Third Circuit to compel an answer, and in December 2023 the Commission formally denied the petition. The Third Circuit later held that the denial's stated reasoning was insufficient and remanded for a fuller explanation. The procedural history is the strongest available evidence for Robinson's claim that the industry asked for rules and was refused — it is documented on the SEC's own docket, not an industry talking point. 29:15

Miles Jennings describes the second-order consequence: when the perimeter is unclear, the competitive advantage flows to whoever is most willing to sit outside it.

"Everyone should be playing by the same rules." 08:18

His fuller argument is that the firms taking the most legal risk were the ones winning share — FTX out-competing Coinbase, SushiSwap draining volume from Uniswap — because risk tolerance, not product quality, was the differentiating variable 07:48–08:18. He also names a subtler distortion: the SEC's own guidance keys off whether builders make representations to buyers, so the rational response is to say nothing, which produces more opacity rather than less 07:01–07:19.

💡 Key InsightJennings is describing a regulatory scheme that selects against transparency. If liability attaches to promises, and promises are what create the "expectation of profits" prong, then the compliance-optimal behaviour for a token team is silence — no roadmap, no financials, no named contributors. The information asymmetry that securities law exists to eliminate is thereby manufactured by securities law as applied. CLARITY's disclosure regime is a direct attempt to invert that incentive: disclosure becomes the price of the safe harbour rather than the evidence against you. 07:19

📜 Howey Is the Hinge, and the Bill Is Built to Route Around It Rather Than Repeal It

This is the intellectual core of the episode and Robinson spends roughly fifteen minutes on it before touching the bill text.

The statutory starting point is the definition of "security" in the Securities Act of 1933. Robinson refers to it as "section 21A," which is a transcription artifact of § 2(a)(1) — the definitional subsection 20:31. It enumerates notes, stocks, treasury stock, bonds, debentures, evidences of indebtedness, certificates of interest or participation in profit-sharing agreements and a long tail of other instruments, and it was drafted deliberately broadly, in the aftermath of the 1920s margin boom and the Crash, to catch "nearly any sort of scheme that people could come up with to raise money" 21:15–21:26.

One term in that list is broader than all the others and carries no statutory definition at all: investment contract.

"Investment contract is not defined by any statute, by any law. That was a deliberate step by Congress to capture arrangements that didn't fit neatly into named categories like stock." 22:21

Courts therefore had to build the content. The pre-Howey formulation, which Robinson correctly attributes to the Gopher Tire line of cases, defined an investment contract as a scheme for "the placing of capital or laying out of money in any way intended to secure income or profit from its employment" 22:44.

🔍 Source Verified✅ State v. Gopher Tire & Rubber Co., 146 Minn. 52, 177 N.W. 937 (Minn. 1920), decided 28 May 1920, is a Minnesota Blue Sky Law prosecution. The court held that certificates entitling holders to a share of profits in exchange for promotional activity were "investment contracts," reasoning that "the placing of capital or laying out of money in a way intended to secure income or profit from its employment is an investment." Congress is generally understood to have imported the term from state blue sky statutes precisely because this body of case law already existed. Robinson's account is accurate, including that this standard governed until 1946.

Then comes the part of the story Robinson is right to say nobody tells. Howey reached the Supreme Court because the Fifth Circuit and the district court had held that

"an investment contract is necessarily missing where the enterprise is not speculative or promotional in character and where the tangible interest which is sold has intrinsic value independent of the success of the enterprise as a whole." 23:34

In other words, the lower courts held that a thing with independent value could not be the subject of an investment contract. The Supreme Court rejected exactly that proposition, and rejected it in terms:

"The test is whether the scheme involves an investment of money in a common enterprise with profits to come solely from the efforts of others. If that test be satisfied, it is immaterial whether the enterprise is speculative or non-speculative or whether there is a sale of property with or without intrinsic value." 24:28
🔗 ConnectionThe rejected Fifth Circuit rule → the entire modern "the token is not the security" argument. Robinson spots this and it is the most intellectually interesting observation in the episode: the intrinsic-value theory that digital-asset advocates rely on is the theory the Supreme Court expressly refused to adopt in 1946. Oranges had intrinsic value. Chinchillas had intrinsic value. Whiskey in a warehouse had intrinsic value. None of that saved the sellers, because Howey looks at the transaction, not the object. This is precisely why the industry's position had to evolve from "tokens aren't securities" to the more defensible "the asset is not the security, but the transaction may be an investment contract" — and why a statute was needed to resolve what happens to the asset afterwards. 23:53

Robinson also correctly notes that lower courts softened "solely" to "predominantly" or "substantially," which is what keeps the test workable when a buyer contributes nominal effort 25:14.

Cohen's Ineluctable Modality project — an exhaustive review of nearly a century of federal appellate investment-contract case law, co-authored with Greg Strong, Freeman Lewin and Sarah Chen — produced two findings that the bill's architecture directly reflects.

🔍 Source Verified✅ The Ineluctable Modality of Securities Law: Why Fungible Crypto Assets Are Not Securities was published 10 November 2022 by Lewis Rinaudo Cohen, Gregory Strong, Freeman Lewin and Sarah Chen, then of DLx Law. It runs roughly 180 pages and surveys the federal appellate investment-contract corpus. Its central claim is that the asset sold in an investment contract transaction is analytically distinct from the investment contract itself. The authors released it without paywall; Robinson's description of the scale of the undertaking is accurate, and its influence on the Senate text's originator/asset separation is visible in the drafting.

The first finding is doctrinal: a thing that carries no legal relationship or obligation is not itself a security, but it can perfectly well be sold in an investment contract transaction 25:58–26:16. The second is a negative finding, and it is the one that makes legislation necessary:

"None of the cases, at least the appellate level, have ever addressed secondary transactions." 27:35

Cohen's explanation of why is elegant. Howey is a remedial doctrine — it applies where parties structured something as a commercial arrangement and a court later looks back and imposes securities treatment because the economic reality was otherwise 26:26–26:59. Remedial doctrines run between the original parties. And as a practical matter there were no secondary markets in orange groves or chinchillas, so the question of what happens when the asset passes from A to B, C, D and E simply never presented itself.

💡 Key InsightThis is the strongest structural argument in the episode and it does not depend on any policy preference. The gap is not that Howey was decided wrongly; it is that Howey was decided in a factual world without continuous anonymous secondary markets, and courts cannot manufacture the missing doctrine without a vehicle. Cohen's framing — "it's the best you can kind of do until Congress acts" — is a genuinely generous reading of the SEC's March 2026 guidance, treating it as a good-faith attempt at a problem that agency interpretation structurally cannot solve. 51:42

Cohen's policy conclusion follows directly, and it is the sentence that most cleanly states the bill's allocation of risk:

"The right person to bear the risk of those ambiguities is the entity or person that raised the money." 52:20

Bliss supplies the litigator's version of the same point, and this may be the most underrated argument in the whole episode. Agency rulemaking is not a substitute for a statute, for two reasons.

"Rules are not as durable as statutes. Statutes are much much more durable, and the durability is a critical benefit of them." 34:07

And second, rules get sued.

"There's an almost inevitable litigation morass awaiting any significant rulemaking in this country." 35:18

Bliss's sharpest observation is that the vulnerability is bidirectional. A chunk of the SEC's current crypto rulemaking rests on a Howey analysis keyed to promises and reasonable expectations. But the argument the exchanges advanced in the Gensler-era litigations was that an investment contract requires an actual economic right — a legal claim on profits or assets — and that promises and expectations are not enough 35:36–36:45. Bliss advanced that argument himself and believes it is correct. Which means the same argument could be turned around and used to attack the authority of a friendly SEC's pro-industry rulemaking.

🎯 Against ConsensusThe prevailing industry mood in 2026 is that the SEC's new guidance has largely solved the problem and legislation is a nice-to-have. Bliss's position is that the guidance is structurally fragile precisely because it is good — it rests on a contestable reading of Howey that the industry's own best litigators have spent years attacking, and an Administrative Procedure Act challenge from either direction could unwind it. On his account, the durability argument for a statute is strongest exactly when the agency is most sympathetic. 36:45

🧬 Four Buckets, and the Entire Fight Lives Inside One of Them

Jennings gives the taxonomy in about forty seconds and it is the cleanest summary in the episode.

"There's really kind of four categories within the taxonomy that Clarity creates." 15:08

The four are digital commodities (digital assets that are commodities and not securities); digital securities (tokenised securities); payment stablecoins (already covered by the GENIUS Act); and a residual bucket of non-commodity, non-security digital assets, into which Jennings puts NFTs — for which, he notes, CLARITY contains a safe harbour 15:12–15:40.

Everything contentious is inside the first bucket. Robinson turns to Title I, which he describes as substantially the 2026 descendant of the Lummis–Gillibrand Responsible Financial Innovation Act, and identifies the two sub-categories the bill creates: ancillary assets, the first definition in the list, and network tokens, the seventh 40:11–40:16.

🌐 Consensus CheckThe lineage Robinson traces is real and unusually clean. The Responsible Financial Innovation Act (S. 4356) was introduced by Senators Cynthia Lummis and Kirsten Gillibrand on 7 June 2022 — the episode says 10 June, a three-day slip of no consequence — and was at the time the most comprehensive digital asset bill ever put to Congress. The Digital Commodity Consumer Protection Act followed that summer from the Senate Agriculture side, backed publicly by Sam Bankman-Fried, whose collapse in November 2022 made the association toxic. FIT21 (H.R. 4763) was introduced 20 July 2023 and passed the House 279–136 in May 2024. The House CLARITY Act (H.R. 3633) was introduced 29 May 2025 and passed 294–134 on 17 July 2025. The Senate Banking text discussed in this episode is the fusion of the RFIA drafting tradition with the House-passed CLARITY structure. 10:42–13:22

A network token is a digital asset intrinsically linked to a distributed ledger system, expected to derive its value from the use and functioning of that system, and carrying no disqualifying financial right — meaning nothing that looks like debt, equity or a profit-sharing claim 45:54–46:09. Clear that bar and the token is a digital commodity: CFTC jurisdiction, and secondary market transactions in it are not securities transactions.

The critical drafting move is in § 105, and Robinson is right to slow down for it. The bill enumerates four circumstances that expressly do not create a disqualifying financial right 44:00–45:14:

  1. Where the distributed ledger system itself collects, receives, accrues or distributes consideration — protocol-level fee flows back to token holders.
  2. Where the token confers governance capabilities over the blockchain or its governance system — an explicit shield for governance tokens.
  3. Where the token's value may appreciate or depreciate in response to the use, efforts, operations or financial performance of the blockchain or its decentralised governance system.
  4. Where the value appreciates or depreciates depending on the efforts of the ancillary asset originator or a related person.

That fourth item is the one to notice. It says that dependence on a founding team does not, by itself, convert a network token into a security. It routes that dependence into the ancillary asset disclosure regime instead.

Sarah Brennan identifies the distinction that carries the weight:

"An economic interest is not necessarily disqualifying. That means that you can have value accrual to the token across these models, which is super important to these tokens not being junk." 41:46

A right is a contract — an enforceable claim. An interest is an economic exposure without an enforceable claim. Under the bill, the former disqualifies and the latter does not.

💡 Key InsightThis is the provision that decides whether tokens can have fundamentals. Under the prior regime the safest token was an economically inert one, because any value accrual mechanism looked like a profit-sharing arrangement and therefore like a security. That is how the industry ended up with governance tokens that governed nothing and fee switches that stayed permanently off. Section 105 legalises the fee switch. Brennan's phrase — "these tokens not being junk" — is the whole policy argument in four words. 41:52

Brennan also flags the provision most likely to disappoint token holders who read the bill as a promotion: an express clause preserving the fiduciary duties an entity owes its shareholders.

"There is an express acknowledgement and structurally kind of keeping this tension between shareholders as a superior class and token holders as a junior class." 42:12

That is a meaningful limit. CLARITY creates a federal disclosure regime for ancillary assets; it does not create state-law fiduciary duties running to token holders. Corporate law still puts equity first, and the statute says so rather than leaving it to be litigated.

🔗 ConnectionThe Senate architecture → the House architecture it replaced. H.R. 3633 as passed by the House organised the same problem around a "mature blockchain system," defined as a blockchain system and its related digital commodity "not controlled by any person or group of persons under common control," with a bright-line element limiting insider ownership to less than 20 percent of outstanding units. The Senate Banking text abandons the maturity/20-percent framing in favour of network token plus ancillary asset plus certification. The trade is explicit: the House chose a countable threshold that could be gamed by distributing supply across nominally unrelated wallets; the Senate chose a facts-and-circumstances standard policed by a filing with liability attached. Cohen defends that trade directly when he says the ambiguity is doing "important work for protecting people." 49:38

Cohen's broader defence of keeping an open-textured standard is worth quoting because it is the honest version of the argument:

"The world of investment fraud is wide broad and deep. And the reason some of this is vague is because it really depends on specific facts and circumstances. And people will do everything they can to work around fixed rules." 49:32

Robinson presses him on the obvious objection — if you lift Howey's language, are you not importing Howey's ambiguity? Cohen concedes that to some extent you are, and reframes the question: not whether ambiguity exists, but who bears it. That reframing is the through-line of the entire statute.

🪜 Ancillary Assets: The Statute's Answer to "What Happens Next"

Brennan's characterisation is the most intuitive one offered:

"I guess I would describe ancillary assets as they're either like immature network tokens or they stay that way, but they are sort of not what you would deem to be a traditional security. But it's got that dependence on the efforts of the team kind of flavor to it." 46:34

The statutory definition, which Robinson reads from § 102:

"The term ancillary asset means a network token, the value of which is dependent upon the entrepreneurial or managerial efforts of an ancillary asset originator or a related person, as those concepts are further specified by the commission by regulation." 56:02

Note what this does structurally. The test starts from network token status. You first ask whether the asset is intrinsically linked to a distributed ledger system and free of disqualifying financial rights. Only once it clears that threshold do you ask whose efforts the value depends on. If the answer is market forces — Bitcoin is the paradigm case — it stays a plain network token. If the answer is a specific team, it is an ancillary asset 54:52–55:31.

The phrase "entrepreneurial or managerial efforts" is lifted nearly verbatim from the Howey progeny, and Robinson flags it as deliberate 48:48–48:56. But Jennings points out that the threshold has been deliberately lowered:

"I think it's nominal efforts in in Clarity as compared to just efforts under Howie." 66:59

That inversion is counter-intuitive and worth stating plainly. Lowering the bar means more tokens fall inside the ancillary asset regime, not fewer. Jennings wants that outcome:

"The vast vast majority of network tokens, basically anyone where there's still a startup that is kind of building that system, is going to fall within that regulatory perimeter. And as I was saying earlier, that's exactly what we want because we want a very level playing field." 67:04–67:19

His justification is information asymmetry rather than investor protection in the abstract: if someone is working on the system, that someone knows things the market does not, and the response to asymmetry is disclosure 66:24–66:47.

🧵 Running ThesisThe bill's organising principle is that ongoing effort creates information asymmetry, and information asymmetry — not asset type — is what triggers regulation. This is a genuine departure from the classificatory instinct that dominated the previous decade, where everyone argued about whether a token "is" a security. CLARITY makes the question dynamic and relational: not what the thing is, but whether anyone currently knows more about it than the market does. Status: confirmed across Cohen, Jennings and Brennan, who arrive at it from three different starting points.

Two defined terms do the work of identifying whose efforts count.

An ancillary asset originator is the person who originally offered, sold or distributed the network token, or caused it to be offered, sold or distributed — in practice the founding team, company or foundation 56:39–56:48. Cohen flags the word choice as significant and calls it a personal hobby horse:

"It does not use the term issuer... Securities have issuers. In any kind of legal sense they are an originator, and if they take back a bunch of the tokens they can and probably should have legal responsibility, but they don't have the same kind of responsibility that a securities issuer has." 56:57–57:27

This is not pedantry. "Issuer" is a term of art under Securities Act § 2(a)(4) that drags a body of registration, liability and reporting doctrine with it. Choosing "originator" instead is a deliberate refusal to import that doctrine wholesale — the same design philosophy that produces the "still not a security" conclusion.

The May draft added joint-and-several liability so that an originator cannot escape status by routing the issuance through an offshore vehicle and transferring the bulk allocation onward. If the original offeror transferred the largest allocation to another person, that recipient can be pulled in as a co-originator 57:40–58:09.

A related person is defined through a lattice of ownership thresholds and lookback windows, and the asymmetry between them is itself informative 58:19–59:51:

CategoryOwnership thresholdLookback
Founder or person serving in a similar capacityBeneficial owner of at least 4% of total outstanding units3 years (36 months)
Executive officer, director, trustee, general partner, or holder of more than 10% of any class of equity of the originatorCurrent or within the preceding 12 months12 months
Person or group under coordinated controlBeneficially owned 10% or more of outstanding units at any point6 months
Person or group under coordinated controlBeneficially owns covered tokens equal to at least 2% of total outstanding units6 months

The design logic is that founders are gripped hardest — the lowest personal threshold, 4%, with the longest lookback, three years — while the corporate and coordinated-control categories get higher thresholds and shorter windows. The statute is chasing the individuals whose ongoing work actually drives value, not merely the cap table.

Cohen connects this to the other half of the bargain, which is the fundraising on-ramp. He traces the lineage back to Commissioner Hester Peirce's token safe harbour proposal, and Robinson looks up the dates mid-conversation 60:59–61:24.

🌐 Consensus CheckCommissioner Hester Peirce first proposed a token safe harbour on 6 February 2020 and released Safe Harbor 2.0 on 13 April 2021. Robinson's real-time correction — "2021, yeah, that was 2.0, so I think the first one maybe even February 2020, so six years ago" — is accurate on both dates. The original proposal offered a three-year grace period for token networks to decentralise before securities registration attached, with disclosure conditions attached. CLARITY's Regulation Crypto plus the certification exit is recognisably that idea, given statutory force and paired with holding requirements Peirce's version did not have. 61:22

Cohen's account of why the lockups exist is the most candid passage in the episode:

"It's really easy to create some hype, sell something that's basically just a little magic ticket jelly bean, you know, nothing yet. Get money in and just walk away." 62:01

The design answer: if you take the fast-track crowdfunding route, "you're going to have to stay holding those assets until you achieve some important functional decentralization hurdles" 63:01. Nobody is forced to stay — but the economic upside is conditioned on delivery.

📜 AnalogThis is the underwriter lockup imported into token land. Post-IPO lockups, typically 90 to 180 days, exist for the same reason: to prevent insiders from converting information advantage and promotional momentum into an immediate exit at retail's expense. CLARITY's version is more demanding, because the release condition is not the passage of time but the achievement of a decentralisation standard. Time-based lockups can be waited out; a standard-based lockup cannot. 63:01

📄 Disclosure Without Registration: The New Securities Act § 4(b)

Here is the mechanism that makes "regulated but not a security" coherent.

The asset remains a digital commodity under CFTC jurisdiction. But the offer, sale or distribution of an ancillary asset by an originator is treated as a distribution of an investment contract for securities law purposes — which puts those specific sales, and only those, under the SEC and the Securities Act disclosure framework 68:11–71:29.

Section 102 of the bill inserts a new § 4(b) into the Securities Act of 1933. The disclosure obligations live in § 4(b)(C) and § 4(b)(D) of the amended Act 71:37–71:50.

Section 4(b)(C) sets out four triggering events. An originator becomes subject to initial and periodic disclosure on any of the following 72:03–72:32:

  1. Filing an offering statement under Regulation A.
  2. Conducting an offering under the crowdfunding exemption.
  3. Making the first secondary market offer, sale or distribution of an ancillary asset in the United States after the Act's effective date — which the bill deems a public offering.
  4. Conducting an offering under Regulation Crypto, the new exemption created by § 103.

Trigger three deserves attention. It means an originator who never conducts a formal offering at all still lands inside the regime on its first post-effective-date secondary market sale. There is no "we launched quietly" path out.

The timing has two phases, and Robinson is emphatic about the first:

"You cannot sell first and disclose later." 76:43

Disclosures must be filed and current before any triggering offer or sale. After that, the obligation is semi-annual — twice a year, for as long as ancillary asset status persists — covering financials, personnel, activities undertaken and projected, token holdings, insider transactions and development timelines 76:29–77:17.

The materiality standard is that information must be material and known or reasonably knowable to the originator or to the digital asset intermediary filing on its behalf 77:23–77:35. That intermediary-filing option is a genuine structural novelty: an exchange can file on a token's behalf, which Robinson flags as "a really interesting future and dynamic."

💡 Key InsightIntermediary substitution quietly solves the hardest practical problem in the whole scheme. Many network tokens have no originator left to file — the team dissolved, the foundation wound down, the assets trade on. Under a pure originator-obligation model those tokens would be permanently un-listable. Letting an exchange file instead converts a dead-end into a compliance service, and creates a commercial incentive for venues to do diligence they would otherwise skip. It also, less comfortably, makes exchanges partial gatekeepers of which tokens can legally trade in the US. 77:35

The disclosure content is organised into three tiers beginning at page 45 of the Senate draft 78:00–79:14:

  • Category A — mandatory. Basic corporate information about the originator: its experience developing ancillary assets, its experience with distributed ledger systems and technology. The text uses "shall furnish."
  • Category B — discretionary, subject to SEC rulemaking. Economic and technical information: how much of the asset the originator itself holds, a plain-English description of how the distributed ledger functions. The text uses "may include."
  • Category C — catch-all. Any other material information not captured by A or B.

Robinson's read on Category C is correct and worth making explicit: it converts the enumerated list from a ceiling into a floor. An originator who discloses everything in A and B and withholds something material has still failed.

The SEC's rules on disclosure content must be "reasonably tailored" by reference to enumerated factors including the aggregate amount of ancillary assets offered or sold to the public in the United States, and whether the asset and its distributed ledger system are subject to coordinated control 74:12–74:36.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Regulation Crypto is the bill's purpose-built public fundraising exemption, modelled on the Regulation A / Regulation D family. Under the Senate Banking text an ancillary asset originator may raise, per calendar year, the greater of $50 million for a period of four years or 10 percent of the total dollar value of ancillary assets outstanding, subject to a $200 million aggregate ceiling. Those caps are what make it a genuine on-ramp rather than a loophole — comparable in scale to Regulation A+ Tier 2, which caps at $75 million per year, but calibrated to token supply rather than corporate revenue. Source: Senate Banking Committee CLARITY Act text, May 2026; Hodder Law analysis of the committee draft.

Robinson's summary of what Regulation Crypto achieves is accurate and worth stating plainly: comply with it, file under § 4(b), and there is for the first time a lawful path to raise capital from the American retail public by selling tokens 73:22–73:52.

Coordinated control is the term to watch. Earlier drafts used common control, a settled corporate-law concept meaning two entities controlled by the same person or parent. The May Senate draft renamed it. Brennan explains the drafting instinct:

"We're going to use the normal term for what we normally conceptualize this as and we're gonna create a new definition that picks up all of these sort of broader relationships that happen in this ecosystem." 75:10

The rename captures the crypto-specific reality that formal ownership structures may not exist while people nonetheless act in concert to control a network or its token supply. The precise definition is left to SEC rulemaking under § 104, with guiding considerations on page 87 of the May text — whether the system is open, permissionless, credibly neutral, distributed, autonomous and economically independent 75:23–75:58.

🔗 ConnectionCoordinated control → the House bill's 20 percent bright line. These are two answers to the same question: how do you stop a team from claiming decentralisation while retaining effective control? The House answer was countable — hold less than 20 percent. The Senate answer is behavioural — are you acting in concert, whatever the cap table says. The Senate answer is harder to game and harder to advise on, which is the recurring trade the whole bill makes. It is also why so much turns on an SEC rulemaking that does not yet exist. 74:47

🚪 Certification Is the Exit Door, and Its Procedure Matters More Than Its Standard

Ancillary asset status is not permanent. Disclosure continues until either a certification terminates the obligation or the SEC denies, suspends or revokes it 79:27–79:47.

Who can file. A "certification covered party," defined in § 4(a)(3), covering four categories: the originator itself; a subsidiary of the originator; a related person; or any entity that directly or indirectly controls, is controlled by, or is under common control with the originator 80:22–80:50. The May draft added the ability for a digital asset intermediary — an exchange listing the token — to file on the originator's behalf in certain circumstances 81:01–81:09.

What it contains. Two substantive prongs, each of which must be based on knowledge and supported by reasonable evidence 81:16–82:29:

  1. That during the 180-day period preceding submission, and as of the submission date, no certification covered party has engaged in more than nominal entrepreneurial or managerial efforts with respect to the ancillary asset or its distributed ledger system — and that no covered party is likely to engage in such efforts after certification.
  2. That all material information reasonably expected to contribute to the value of the ancillary asset is, and is reasonably expected to remain, publicly available.

Robinson notes the 180-day window was shortened from one year in earlier drafts 81:25. And the "more than nominal" formulation is the deliberate softening of Howey's efforts prong — some minimal ongoing involvement does not automatically block the exit 82:00–82:07. What counts as nominal is left to SEC rulemaking under § 104.

There is no third prong. Robinson says so explicitly, which is a useful thing for a reader to know, because the natural expectation would be some additional decentralisation metric.

What happens after filing. The SEC has 20 business days to notify the originator of an intent to deny. Robinson's unpacking of this is the sharpest procedural analysis in the episode 83:40–84:39:

  • The decision cannot be delegated to staff or to an individual commissioner. Denial requires a vote of the full Commission — all five commissioners.
  • That creates a high institutional cost for denial: it consumes Commission meeting time and requires five political appointees to go on the public record with reasons.
  • A full Commission vote is a public act producing a public record, which makes quiet or arbitrary denial materially harder than a staff-level decision would be.
  • If the SEC does not deny within the 20-business-day window, the certification is automatically deemed approved.
💡 Key InsightThis is the single most consequential drafting decision in the bill and it has nothing to do with token classification. Deemed-approval plus a non-delegable full-Commission denial inverts the default. Under the status quo, an agency can stall indefinitely and silence means no — the Coinbase petition took eighteen months to produce a denial. Under CLARITY, silence means yes, and saying no costs the Commission scarce institutional capital. The substantive standard is vague, but the procedure is not, and in administrative practice the procedure usually decides the outcome. 84:32

Certification is revocable, in both directions. If, after certification takes effect, any covered party engages in efforts that would prevent them from meeting the standard, the certification ceases to be effective as of that date, and the party undertaking those efforts must recommence disclosure — including a description of the change in circumstances 85:16–85:44.

Robinson reads this as a feature rather than a trap, and his example is a good one. Suppose quantum computing starts breaking cryptographic assumptions. You want the original team to be able to come back and upgrade the protocol. A regime where re-engagement is permanently penalised would freeze networks in whatever state they were in on the day they certified out 85:44–86:16.

One more provision from the May draft: a project can submit a certification before launching a public offering, giving it pre-clearance on decentralisation status from day one rather than running the ancillary asset regime first and certifying out later 86:16–86:36.

🌐 Consensus CheckLaw firm analyses of the committee text describe the same mechanism with a framing worth adding: the bill establishes a rebuttable presumption that a network token is an ancillary asset, which the originator or a digital asset intermediary rebuts by certification supported by reasonable evidence. Where an intermediary files, it must conduct a reasonable inquiry into the originator's recent and anticipated efforts. That presumption framing is the mirror image of Robinson's description — he explains the test affirmatively, the statute allocates the burden defensively — and both are accurate descriptions of the same provision. The practical consequence is that a token is presumptively inside the regime until someone files to get it out, which is why the 20-business-day deemed approval matters so much. 79:47

Robinson also handles the anti-circumvention point briefly: § 505 provides that a security does not cease to be a security solely because it is recorded on a blockchain, and confirms that existing rules apply to tokenised securities, with a study directed at how best to regulate them 63:46–64:04. This closes the obvious arbitrage — you cannot tokenise equity and claim digital commodity treatment.

💵 The Two Compromises That Unblocked the Markup

By 2026 the classification architecture was largely settled and the remaining fights were adjacent. Two of them nearly ate the bill.

Stablecoin yield. Robinson identifies this as the reason a January 2026 draft took until May to reach markup 94:53–94:58.

The baseline is the GENIUS Act, which prohibits a permitted payment stablecoin issuer from paying holders interest or yield. Robinson describes the resulting situation as issuers being able to pay yield "indirectly" but not directly — which is a fair shorthand for the three-party structure where an issuer shares reserve income with an exchange, and the exchange pays a reward to the customer.

🔍 Source Verified✅ GENIUS Act § 4(a)(11) prohibits permitted payment stablecoin issuers and foreign payment stablecoin issuers from paying a holder "any form of interest or yield (whether in cash, tokens, or other consideration) solely in connection with the holding, use, or retention" of a payment stablecoin. The prohibition binds issuers, not distributors — which is exactly the gap the three-party exchange model exploited and the bank lobby wanted closed. Regulators have been working the same problem: the OCC's proposed GENIUS implementing rules introduce a rebuttable presumption that an arrangement where an issuer pays an affiliate or third party who then routes yield to holders violates the statutory prohibition. Source: GENIUS Act § 4(a)(11); OCC notice of proposed rulemaking on GENIUS Act implementation.

CLARITY § 404, titled "Prohibiting Interest and Yield on Payment Stablecoins," extends the prohibition to covered parties — exchanges, brokers and platforms — closing the distributor gap that GENIUS left open 95:24–95:40.

The compromise is the carve-out. The prohibition does not apply to activities that are not economically or functionally equivalent to paying yield on an interest-bearing bank deposit. Three categories qualify 95:56–96:27:

  1. Payments in connection with transactions, payments, transfers, conversions, remittances or settlement activity, including rebates tied to use of a payment stablecoin.
  2. Providing liquidity for market making, posting collateral, or otherwise putting assets at credit or investment risk.
  3. Use of any product or service, including participation in governance, validation, staking, or a loyalty, promotional, subscription or incentive programme.

And critically, permissible rewards may be calculated by reference to balance, duration, tenure, or any combination 96:27–96:36. Robinson reads this correctly: a reward that looks like interest — scaled to how much you hold and for how long — is not automatically prohibited, provided it is structured as activity-based rather than pure passive yield.

⚡ TensionThe carve-out and the prohibition are in genuine tension on the statute's own terms. Section 404 bars anything economically or functionally equivalent to deposit interest, then expressly permits rewards calculated by balance and duration — which is the definition of deposit interest. The reconciling principle has to be that some qualifying activity must occur, with balance and tenure merely setting the size of the reward. That is a workable line, but it is a line drawn by SEC and banking-agency rulemaking rather than by the text, and the September floor negotiations show it was never fully accepted. Robinson's own verdict — "that should be settled, that was a huge hurdle and I think that's done" — did not survive the summer.

Developer protection. The Blockchain Regulatory Certainty Act is the other side battle, and Miller Whitehouse-Levine describes the markup outcome as bittersweet:

"What they ended up doing was stripping a reference to the blockchain regulatory certainty act from the section 301 decentralized in name only rulemaking. So that is of course one step back but then it bounced out of committee on a bipartisan basis which is two step forward." 92:49–93:07

Robinson explains what the BRCA actually does, and the precision here is worth preserving. Its purpose is to clarify how 18 U.S.C. § 1960 — unlicensed money transmitting — applies to developers of non-custodial smart contract software who are not themselves transmitting money. It does not touch 18 U.S.C. § 1956, the money laundering statute, so a developer who conspires to launder money remains fully exposed 93:25–93:47.

🌐 Consensus CheckThe distinction Robinson draws is the operative one in the recent enforcement record. Section 1960 criminalises operating an unlicensed money transmitting business and, unlike § 1956, requires no proof of intent to conceal criminal proceeds — which is why it has been the charge of choice against software developers who never took custody of user funds. Section 1956 requires knowledge that the funds are criminal proceeds and intent to promote or conceal. The BRCA narrows the former without touching the latter. Framing it as a free pass, as several critics have, misdescribes which statute is being amended. 93:42

What survived in § 301 is the substantive distinction. Robinson quotes Coin Center's reading: the section remains premised on a meaningful difference between decentralised protocols and non-decentralised protocols, where in the latter "a group of persons under common control have the ability to alter the protocol or restrict access." Actors who do more than develop and publish permissionless, neutral software are captured 94:00–94:32.

💡 Key InsightThe "decentralized in name only" rulemaking is where the bill's decentralisation question actually gets decided for DeFi, and it is a separate track from the ancillary asset certification. Certification asks whether anyone is working on the network. The DINO test asks whether anyone can alter or restrict it. A protocol can be fully abandoned by its founders — certifying out cleanly — while a multisig still holds upgrade keys. Two tests, two different failure modes, and the bill needs both. 94:24

🏛️ Two Committees, One Floor Vehicle, and the Provision That Decided It

Bill Hughes gives the clearest available explanation of why market structure requires two bills. It is a jurisdictional fact rather than a drafting preference.

"Agriculture committee deals with farming. What's farming about? It's about growing and selling commodities of all sorts." 90:13

The Agriculture Committees in both chambers oversee the CFTC, because futures markets began as hedging instruments for farmers. The SEC sits with House Financial Services and Senate Banking. Because digital assets implicate both regimes, the package needs both committees' work product 90:13–92:10.

Kyle Bligen lays out the procedural runway as it stood after the Banking markup 88:46–89:41:

"There needs to be a committee report on the bill to bring it out of the banking committee and then we need Senate leadership to actually schedule this bill to go to the floor."

Then Rule 14 — the mechanism for placing a measure directly on the Senate calendar, bypassing further committee referral — is used to join the Banking text with the Agriculture text into a single comprehensive market structure package 89:22–89:41.

🔍 Source Verified✅ The two committee tracks are documented and their votes diverged sharply. The Senate Agriculture Committee advanced S. 3755, the Digital Commodity Intermediaries Act, on 29 January 2026 by a party-line 12–11 vote; every amendment offered during that markup — on ethics rules, illicit finance safeguards, and a fully staffed bipartisan CFTC — failed 12–11. The Senate Banking Committee advanced the CLARITY Act on 14 May 2026 by 15–9, with Democratic Senators Ruben Gallego and Angela Alsobrooks crossing over. Bligen's account of a partisan Agriculture markup followed by a bipartisan Banking markup is exactly right, and the asymmetry explains why so much negotiating weight fell on the Banking text.

The remaining hurdle, as every guest identifies it, is ethics. Bligen frames it as two separable problems 100:00–103:22:

"There's a political issue to be quite frank and then there's a public interest concern."

The design questions are genuinely hard. Which activities are prohibited — holding assets, or allowing a memecoin to be issued in your name and benefiting from it? Who is the watchdog, and can an office staffed by Senate-confirmed appointees credibly investigate the administration that nominated them? And who is covered: civil servants and senior staff are easy, members of Congress follow the existing insider-trading debate, but extending coverage to the offices of the President and Vice President is where the friction is 100:02–102:28.

"Public servants should not be leveraging their office or their position for personal or financial gain." 103:14

Gerald Gallagher relays the White House position from Patrick Witt, executive director of the President's Council of Advisers on Digital Assets: any ethics provision has to be constitutional — Congress cannot simply legislate limits on the President's ability to do business — and has to apply uniformly rather than singling out one office 97:33–98:13.

Senator Gallego voted the bill out while stating the limit of what that vote meant:

"I want to be clear my vote here does not guarantee a vote on the floor. We have many outstanding issues still to resolve. Toughest and most critical of all is coming to an agreement on ethics guard rails for elected officials." 98:38

Robinson closes on Gallego's own football metaphor — the ethics deal at the 99-yard line, needing a short-yardage back to punch it in 104:01.

🌐 Consensus CheckIt did not get punched in. On 15 September 2026 the Senate voted on a motion to proceed to the CLARITY Act and failed to invoke cloture, 49–50, well short of the 60 votes required to open debate. Reporting on the failed vote identifies the ethics provisions as the decisive rift — negotiators had produced language requiring divestiture or blind trusts and giving state attorneys general an enforcement role, but opponents argued the enforcement mechanism lacked sufficient independence. Stablecoin yield and law enforcement provisions remained unresolved alongside it. The bill had been eligible for a floor vote since 1 June 2026; the vote was pushed from summer to September and then lost. Prediction market pricing on enactment during 2026 collapsed from above 70 percent earlier in the year to roughly 5 percent after the vote. Negotiations are expected to continue, but the calendar before the midterms is short.
🧵 Running ThesisEverything the guests say about the substance held up; everything they say about timing did not. Whitehouse-Levine's read — "this train is out of the station at this point and whatever version ends up on the Senate floor and the House floor will become law" — assumed that clearing committee with bipartisan votes meant clearing the floor. What the September vote demonstrates is that the 60-vote cloture threshold is a different political instrument entirely: two Democratic crossovers are sufficient for a 15–9 committee report and nowhere near sufficient for cloture, which needs at least seven. Status: the substantive analysis is confirmed and durable; the procedural optimism is falsified.

Two sentences from the episode deserve to be read against that outcome. Whitehouse-Levine's:

"For the first time in my career in Washington working on crypto policy, it feels like this bill is actually going to become law." 13:55

And, immediately after, his own hedge — that there would be further changes and that they would have to be fought 14:10–14:16. That instinct was the right one.

💸 The Tax Problem Nobody Was Pricing

Robinson saves this for last and is right that it is underdiscussed. Jason Schwartz had used the word "nonviable" on X about US token launches under CLARITY, Jennings pushed back, and Schwartz refined the claim.

"It's certainly viable. It's just that companies that view token sales as a capital raise might be disappointed to learn that they can raise capital for free by issuing debt or equity in the US, but they're taxed on the sale of tokens in the US." 107:00

The mechanism is clean first-principles tax law and Schwartz states it precisely. Issuing equity is not a taxable event because the buyer receives a claim on the company's assets. Issuing debt is not taxable because the company incurs an offsetting obligation. A token sale is neither: the buyer gets no claim on the company and the company incurs no liability, so the proceeds are simply income 107:14–107:46.

"The company has this cash basically just free to use in whatever manner it wants. And so under sort of first principles of taxation, that really should be a taxable event." 107:34

Schwartz's own view is that this is defensible policy, not a drafting error. But the practical consequence for founders is blunt: "you think you're raising a hundred bucks, you're actually raising, let's say, 80 bucks" 107:53.

💡 Key InsightThis is the gap that will keep offshore structuring alive even if CLARITY passes. The bill fixes securities law and does nothing to the Internal Revenue Code. Schwartz describes the current offshore workaround elsewhere in the episode: to keep token proceeds from being pulled back into the US company's income, founders must genuinely give up control and transfer IP to a protocol steward governed by a different team — a structure he calls "a huge pain in the ass" and "a perverse policy incentive" 30:57–31:37. CLARITY removes the securities-law reason to go offshore while leaving the tax reason fully intact, so a bill sold partly on ending legal gamesmanship would end only one kind of it. Jennings hopes clarity ends "the tax gamesmanship" too 30:31; on the text as drafted, it does not. 107:46
🔗 ConnectionThe tax point → the ancillary asset regime. There is an unremarked interaction here. Regulation Crypto's whole purpose is to make US public token fundraising lawful. But if a compliant US raise under Regulation Crypto is taxed as ordinary income while an equivalent equity raise is not, the tax code applies a haircut to precisely the behaviour the securities title is trying to encourage. The two halves of the incentive structure point in opposite directions, and nothing in the nine titles reconciles them. 108:02

Phase 3: Specific Item Breakdown

🟣 Network Token — The Statute's Clean Non-Security

A digital asset intrinsically linked to a distributed ledger system, expected to derive value from that system's use and functioning, and conferring no disqualifying financial right.

What It Covers: Defined in § 102 as the seventh definition in the amended list. Clearing the definition makes the asset a digital commodity: CFTC jurisdiction applies, and secondary market transactions in it are not securities transactions. Bitcoin is the paradigm case. Jennings's list also includes Ethereum, Uniswap and Hyperliquid — every token deriving value from an underlying blockchain system.

Why It Matters: This is the category that closes the spot-market gap. An exchange listing network tokens registers with and is regulated by the CFTC rather than the SEC. Lockup rules that attach to securities and to ancillary assets do not apply. Crucially, the obligations that do exist here sit at the intermediary level — on exchanges and custodians — not at the issuer level on the project or its founders.

🟣 Ancillary Asset — Regulated, but Still Not a Security

A network token whose value depends on the entrepreneurial or managerial efforts of an ancillary asset originator or a related person.

What It Covers: A subset of network tokens, not a separate asset class. The asset itself stays a digital commodity under CFTC jurisdiction. Only the originator's and related persons' own offers, sales and distributions are treated as distributions of an investment contract, pulling those transactions under the SEC and triggering § 4(b) disclosure. The bill establishes this as a rebuttable presumption, rebutted by certification.

Why It Matters: It is the bridge category that lets a project raise money and keep building without its token becoming a security — the middle ground that neither Howey nor the pre-CLARITY SEC had any way to construct. Cohen's insistence that it is "still not a security" is not rhetoric; it determines whether downstream holders are transacting in securities, which determines whether every exchange, custodian and broker in the chain needs securities registration.

🟣 Disqualifying Financial Right — The Line Between Commodity and Security

Any debt, equity or profit-sharing claim whose presence prevents a token from being a network token at all.

What It Covers: Enumerated in § 102(7). Section 105(a) then carves out four circumstances that are expressly not disqualifying: protocol-level fee accrual and distribution; governance rights over the blockchain or its governance system; value changes driven by the system's use or performance; and value changes driven by the efforts of the originator or a related person.

Why It Matters: This is the provision that makes economically meaningful tokens legal. Brennan's right versus interest distinction is the operative test: an enforceable contractual claim disqualifies, an economic exposure does not. Without § 105, fee switches and revenue-sharing designs would remain securities-by-construction, and the industry's decade of deliberately inert tokens would continue.

🟣 Regulation Crypto — The Public Fundraising On-Ramp

A new exemption from securities registration, created by § 103, letting an ancillary asset originator sell tokens to the American public subject to conditions set by SEC rule.

What It Covers: Deliberately named as a cousin to Regulation A (streamlined small-company public offerings) and Regulation D (private placements to accredited investors), but tailored to token mechanics. The Senate text caps a raise at the greater of $50 million per calendar year for four years or 10 percent of the total dollar value of ancillary assets outstanding, with a $200 million aggregate ceiling. Conducting an offering under it is one of the four § 4(b)(C) disclosure triggers.

Why It Matters: It is the first lawful route to retail token fundraising in the United States. Cohen's point is that blockchain's native strength is crowdfunding — letting the people who understand a project best contribute — and that private placements limited to accredited investors and venture funds are the exact opposite of that. The caps are the guardrail that keeps it an on-ramp rather than an unregistered IPO.

🟣 Certification — The Exit from Ancillary Asset Status

A filing that terminates disclosure obligations by attesting to no more than nominal ongoing efforts and full public availability of material information.

What It Covers: Filed by a certification covered party under § 4(a)(3) — the originator, a subsidiary, a related person, or an entity under common control — or by a digital asset intermediary on the originator's behalf. Two prongs, a 180-day lookback, SEC review within 20 business days, denial only by non-delegable full-Commission vote, and automatic approval on agency silence. Revocable if covered parties resume more-than-nominal efforts. Available pre-launch.

Why It Matters: Procedurally this is the most powerful provision in the bill. Deemed approval reverses the default that made the previous era so painful, and requiring five commissioners to go on the public record makes denial institutionally expensive. It also turns decentralisation from a defence raised after enforcement into a status you can file for in advance.

🟣 Coordinated Control — The Renamed Anti-Circumvention Test

A crypto-native replacement for "common control," aimed at people acting in concert without any formal ownership link.

What It Covers: Renamed from common control in the May Senate draft. Definition left to SEC rulemaking under § 104, with guiding considerations on page 87 of the text: whether the system is open, permissionless, credibly neutral, distributed, autonomous and economically independent. Feeds into both the related-person thresholds and the tailoring of disclosure obligations.

Why It Matters: Formal corporate control concepts do not capture a group of pseudonymous holders coordinating through a Discord and a multisig. The rename is an admission that the existing corporate-law vocabulary does not reach the conduct. It is also the bill's largest undefined term, which makes the § 104 rulemaking a gating item for the whole regime.

🟣 Section 404 — Prohibiting Interest and Yield on Payment Stablecoins

Extends the GENIUS Act's yield ban from issuers to the exchanges, brokers and platforms that distribute stablecoins, with a three-category activity carve-out.

What It Covers: Applies to covered parties rather than issuers, because issuers are already covered by GENIUS § 4(a)(11). Exempts activity that is not economically or functionally equivalent to deposit interest: transaction-linked payments and rebates; liquidity provision, collateral posting and other at-risk deployment; and use of products or services including governance, validation, staking and loyalty or incentive programmes. Permissible rewards may be calculated by balance, duration or tenure.

Why It Matters: This was the four-month blockage between the January and May drafts, resolved as the Tillis–Alsobrooks compromise. It is also the clearest instance in the bill of an incumbent industry — banks — successfully defending a deposit franchise against a competing payment rail. The carve-out is broad enough to preserve most existing reward programmes while denying the label of interest.

🟣 Blockchain Regulatory Certainty Act — The Developer Carve-Out

Clarifies that 18 U.S.C. § 1960's unlicensed money transmitting offence does not reach developers of non-custodial software who never control user funds.

What It Covers: Carried in Title VI on protecting software developers. Touches § 1960 only; § 1956 money laundering liability, which requires knowledge and intent, is untouched. During the May markup a cross-reference to the BRCA was stripped from the § 301 decentralised-in-name-only rulemaking as part of the deal that produced bipartisan votes.

Why It Matters: Section 1960 requires no proof of intent to conceal criminal proceeds, which is why it became the charge of choice against non-custodial developers. Narrowing it is the difference between writing open-source software being a compliance question and being a criminal-exposure question. The stripped cross-reference is a real loss of belt-and-braces protection, which Whitehouse-Levine scores as one step back against two steps forward.
