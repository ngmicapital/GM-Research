Executive Summary

  • x402 is an HTTP extension that turns a dormant status code into a payment handshake. HTTP has always reserved 402 for "Payment Required" and never defined what to do with it. x402 fills that gap: the server answers an unpaid request with 402 plus a machine-readable list of acceptable payment terms, and the client re-sends the request carrying a signed payment. 12:45–13:48
  • The design goal is that neither side needs to know anything about blockchains. A third role — the facilitator — verifies the signed payment and broadcasts settlement, so the resource server never runs a node, holds gas, or manages RPC. That separation is why non-crypto companies can adopt it. 15:42–16:08
  • Reppel's business argument is more interesting than his technical one: charge the agent, not the reader. If AI agents are becoming the dominant way people consume content, they disintermediate publishers from their audience. x402 lets a site keep ads and subscriptions for humans and price access per-request for machines — turning agents from a threat into a revenue line. 07:24–08:43
  • Agents turn out to be good at budgets, which is the part that makes autonomous payment tractable. Reppel describes what a colleague calls "economic reasoning": give an agent a spending limit and a price list and it reliably picks the 2-cent API over the 20-cent one. Wallet-level guardrails plus an escalation path to a human do the rest. 22:04–23:40
  • Coinbase gave the standard away deliberately, and the adoption numbers now sit outside it. x402 was donated to the Linux Foundation and development moved to the x402 Foundation; Visa, Mastercard and American Express joined rather than resisted, because card networks stay the better rail for large, disputable purchases. (14:29–14:45, 26:06–26:53)

Recording note: this is a booth interview — Reppel refers to being in Miami 02:17 and to a trading competition running at the CoinDesk booth beside them 19:01. Automatic captions render the protocol name inconsistently as "X402", "XRO 2", "XPRT" and "X 402"; it is written x402 throughout below. A sponsor segment at 12:20–12:42 is excluded.

🔌 The Internet Can Describe Everything Except Money

Reppel's framing of the problem is the cleanest thing in the interview, and he opens with it twice — once in the cold open and once in full.

"But they have no way of saying, here's money." 05:03 — Erik Reppel on what HTTP can and cannot express.

Computers exchange structured instructions constantly: render this data this way, here is an image, here is audio. What they have never had is a standardised representation of value. His formulation of the fix:

"What x402 does is it allows you to code against money and treat money as something that you can share as easily as you could content." 05:11

The analogy he reaches for is the browser. You do not download Chrome to visit Google sites; Safari, Chrome and any other browser all work with any website because the web runs on shared protocols 05:47–06:08. Payments have no equivalent. His one-line version of the status quo, prompted by the host:

"You can only do Venmo on Venmo, but with x402, anyone can build Venmo." 06:34

Two clients on two different x402-speaking apps can pay each other because the value itself moves over stablecoin rails, and the protocol only standardises how the request for payment and the proof of payment are expressed 06:46.

💡 Key InsightThe claim is narrower and stronger than "crypto payments for the web." x402 does not define a currency, a chain, or a settlement mechanism — it defines the negotiation. The server publishes what it will accept; the client picks one. That indirection is why the protocol can add card networks later without changing shape, and it is also why the facilitator exists. 06:15

Reppel is explicit that the problem is old, not new. Building payments into the internet "traces its roots back to the '80s" 04:01; what changed is that blockchains got fast enough to move at the speed of an HTTP request — "you can actually send money in under a second for under a cent on modern blockchains" 03:33 — at the same moment AI agents arrived needing to pay for things.

📜 AnalogThe lineage he gestures at is real and specific. David Chaum's blind-signature work in the early 1980s and the DigiCash venture that followed were the first serious attempts at native internet money, and they failed for reasons of adoption and settlement infrastructure rather than cryptography. The 402 status code was reserved during the same era of optimism and then sat unused for roughly three decades. 04:01

🧾 402 Was Always There, Reserved and Empty

Asked what the name means, Reppel explains that every HTTP request gets a three-digit status code back — 200 for OK, 404 for not found — and 402 was set aside for "payment required" 12:52–13:19. Then he offers a party trick: reciting the entire IETF specification for it.

"402 Payment Required Reserved for Future Use. That's it." 13:32 — Erik Reppel, reciting the whole spec.

That is not a joke at the IETF's expense; it is the whole argument. The code exists because the web's designers assumed payments would be needed, and it stayed empty because "we didn't have a way of actually transmitting the value natively within the internet" 13:37. x402, he says, is an extension of the 402 status code rather than a replacement for it 13:45.

🔍 Source Verified✅ The recitation is exact. RFC 9110 (HTTP Semantics, June 2022), the current specification that obsoletes RFC 7231 and its predecessors, defines the code at §15.5.3 in a single sentence: "The 402 (Payment Required) status code is reserved for future use." The definition has carried forward essentially unchanged from the HTTP/1.1 work of the late 1990s. Source: rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html.
🔗 ConnectionReserved status code → zero adoption cost. Because 402 already exists in every HTTP client, server, proxy and CDN on earth, x402 needs no new port, no new transport and no negotiation with infrastructure vendors. A server that returns 402 is doing something the spec always permitted. This is the single largest structural advantage x402 has over any payment scheme that requires new plumbing. 13:45

⚙️ How the Handshake Actually Works

Reppel does not walk the wire protocol on air, so here it is, because the mechanism is where the design decisions live.

A client requests a protected resource. The server, seeing no payment attached, replies HTTP 402 Payment Required with a JSON body containing x402Version, a human-readable error, and an accepts array. Each entry in accepts is a set of PaymentRequirements — the scheme (currently "exact"), the network (e.g. a Base or Solana identifier), maxAmountRequired in atomic token units, the asset contract address, the payTo recipient address, the resource URL, a description, and maxTimeoutSeconds.

The client picks one, signs a payment authorisation, base64-encodes it, and re-sends the same request with an X-PAYMENT header. The server hands that payload to a facilitator — POST /verify — which checks the signature, the payer's balance, the amount, the time window, that the parameters match what was demanded, and finally simulates the transaction. Only after verification does the server do the work. It then calls POST /settle, the facilitator broadcasts, and the server returns the resource with an X-PAYMENT-RESPONSE header carrying the transaction hash.

🔍 Source Verified✅ On EVM chains the "exact" scheme uses EIP-3009 transferWithAuthorization, an ERC-20 extension that lets a holder authorise a transfer by signature so a third party can submit it. Two consequences follow. The payer never broadcasts a transaction and pays no gas; and replay is prevented by a 32-byte nonce inside the signed authorisation, enforced at the contract level. On Solana the same scheme is implemented with SPL TransferChecked under a strict instruction layout. Source: x402 Protocol Specification v1, §6 and §7, github.com/x402-foundation/x402.
💡 Key InsightThe facilitator is the load-bearing abstraction and it was there from the beginning — the earliest commit in the x402 repository, dated 16 February 2025, is titled "signing and facilitating transfer works." Its job is to absorb every piece of blockchain-specific knowledge: gas, RPC endpoints, chain selection, simulation. A publisher adding x402 installs one middleware function and never learns what a nonce is. That is the design choice that makes Reppel's claim about non-crypto adopters credible. 15:56
🌐 Consensus CheckThe protocol has since been generalised beyond HTTP. The specification separates types, scheme logic and representation, with transport bindings for HTTP, MCP (the Model Context Protocol agents use to call tools) and A2A (agent-to-agent). The same payment payload can therefore ride a tool call rather than a web request — which matters considerably more for agent commerce than the web case Reppel describes on air. Source: x402 specs, transports-v1. 04:48

💸 Charge the Agent, Not the Reader

The use case Reppel is most animated about is not payments as a vertical but the business model it unlocks.

His premise: AI agents are quickly becoming a dominant way people consume content, which disintermediates publishers and API providers from their audience "in a big way" 07:35–07:42. The existing economic engine is ads and subscriptions, and neither works on a machine. His alternative is explicitly additive, not a replacement:

"If an AI comes to you, you charge them 5 cents or 10 cents or a dollar or however much you want to price your content at." 08:08 — Erik Reppel.

The framing he draws from it — "AI isn't something that's a defend against, it's something that you can actually monetize as a publisher" 08:15 — is the strategic case for the whole protocol, and he connects it to something larger: a positive-sum incentive for the internet to stay as vibrant as it is, which he calls humanity's greatest achievement 08:24–08:33.

Asked whether this is vision or reality, he names live examples: API providers including CoinGecko and CoinMarketCap; Messari making research and data available over x402; and an AI-native newsletter launched by an a16z partner where agents pay a dollar to read each weekly article 08:48–09:26. His own characterisation is honest — "we're kind of in the experimental phase where it's kind of ramping up, but this is a thing you can do today" 09:27.

🔗 ConnectionAgent token costs → willingness to pay. Users have already been conditioned by LLM pricing: your agent costs a couple of dollars a day to run anyway, so an extra 30 cents for better data is a line item, not a new category of spending 09:59–10:52. Micropayments failed for thirty years partly because asking a human to approve a 5-cent charge costs more attention than the charge is worth. An agent with a budget has no attention to spend.
💡 Key InsightThe per-request model inverts the subscription problem for the buyer too. Reppel's research example — a recruiter or VC whose agent reads everything written about a company — replaces "maintain n subscriptions that sometimes don't have the thing you're looking for" with paying only for what is retrieved 17:16–18:29. Subscriptions price access to a corpus; x402 prices consumption of a document.

🤖 What Agents Do With a Wallet

The host presses on the obvious sceptical case: people give agents $100 to trade and the agent loses it. Reppel does not defend agentic trading — he redirects.

"The thing that agents absolutely can do is pay for stuff using x402. We see this literally tens of thousands of times a day." 20:19 — Erik Reppel.

Giving an AI a wallet with no instruction and expecting it to find alpha "feels a little bit like wishful thinking" 19:42–19:52, while paying for things is both achievable today and more valuable, because it compounds: an agent that can buy better context, data and tools is a better agent 20:33–20:40. He notes pointedly that the Bitcoin white paper's title is about peer-to-peer electronic cash — paying for things was the original premise before trading absorbed the industry's attention 20:02–20:16.

On whether agents grasp value at all, he cites a colleague's observation:

"Something that my colleague Kevin refers to as economic reasoning." 23:22 — Erik Reppel.
"This API costs 2 cents, this API costs 20 cents. They claim to do the same thing. I'm going to try the 2 cent API." 23:34 — Erik Reppel on what that looks like in practice.

The control model he wants is budgets plus escalation, enforced at the wallet rather than in the prompt:

"You can spend up to five bucks a day. Don't spend more than $3 per thing you're trying to do." 22:12 — Erik Reppel describing agentic wallet guardrails.

The crucial detail is where those limits live. "The really nice property of blockchains and crypto wallets is that they're really easy to program," so the rules are enforced rather than interpreted — the agent must ask permission rather than be trusted to follow instructions, and the human gets a push notification when it tries to exceed its limit 22:48–23:12.

💡 Key InsightThis is the substantive answer to the prompt-injection objection nobody raises in the interview. If spending limits are instructions in a system prompt, they are advisory and an adversarial input can talk past them. If they are conditions on a programmable wallet, no amount of persuasion moves money. Reppel is describing a policy layer that sits below the model, which is the only place it can safely sit.
🌐 Consensus CheckHe is candid that the category is immature — Coinbase shipped one of the first agentic wallets in February, the properties that make a good agentic wallet differ meaningfully from those of a human wallet, and "the tech works, it's just how good is the UX" 20:49–21:35. That maps to how the wider field has developed: the payment rail settled quickly, while authorisation, delegation and spend policy remain the contested layer.

🏦 Rails, Not Rivals: Why the Card Networks Joined

The host asks the natural question — would Visa and Mastercard want anything to do with a protocol that could undercut their fees? Reppel's answer is that they are already inside it: Visa, Mastercard and American Express are founding members of the x402 Foundation, which launched on April 2nd, a date chosen for the joke 14:29–14:43.

His explanation is a rails argument. x402 is currently stablecoin-only in practice — most volume on Base, Solana and Polygon, with support extending across roughly fifty chains — but the protocol is payment-method agnostic and card support is intended 11:05–11:32. The differentiator is transaction size. Stablecoins are uniquely good at low-value transfers; cards remain better for a flight or a hotel. He sketches the mixed flow: an agent pays 10 cents to read an article, 5 cents to query a flight API, and then puts the actual booking on a credit card — all expressed through x402 11:40–12:00.

"You can as a merchant say I support all of these ways of paying me and you consumer choose how you want to pay." 12:10 — Erik Reppel on his own analogy: x402 as the placard of accepted cards at a cash register.
💡 Key InsightThe placard analogy explains why the card networks joined instead of fighting. x402 does not disintermediate them; it is the layer at which a merchant advertises that it accepts them. The accepts array is literally the placard — a machine-readable list of tenders, of which stablecoins are currently the only populated entries. A protocol that standardises acceptance is worth more to an incumbent network than a protocol it has to compete with. 12:02
🎯 Against ConsensusReppel's position is notably un-maximalist for a Coinbase engineer: "consumers still want to use credit cards for stuff. I think that's the thing we take for granted in the crypto industry" 14:46–14:53. He treats card payment as the correct answer for a large class of transactions rather than a legacy system to be displaced, and grounds it in dispute resolution and chargeback expectations rather than ideology.

🏛️ Governance: Given Away on Purpose

Asked about Coinbase's long-term role, Reppel makes the structural point directly.

"Coinbase has donated x402 to the Linux Foundation and spun up the x402 Foundation." 26:10 — Erik Reppel.

His reasoning: "you can't have one company controlling it. It needs to be kind of a group of industry peers making the decisions" 26:16–26:27. Development proceeds through the Foundation; Coinbase builds products and services that complement the standard, but so can anyone — "Coinbase actually doesn't have the ability to build anything anyone else can't go and build. We just want to be the best at it" 26:47–26:53.

🔍 Source Verified✅ The governance move is visible in the primary record. The x402-foundation GitHub organisation was created on 1 April 2026, matching Reppel's April 2nd launch date; the canonical repository now lives there and the original coinbase/x402 repository carries a notice that it is a development fork with all issues and pull requests transferred. The project itself dates to February 2025. Source: github.com/x402-foundation/x402.
🌐 Consensus CheckAdoption has moved well past the "experimental phase" Reppel describes. The x402 site currently reports roughly 75 million transactions and $24 million in volume over a trailing 30-day window, across about 94,000 buyers and 22,000 sellers, and lists Alchemy, AWS, Cloudflare, Stripe and Vercel among adopters. The agentic.market directory Reppel points listeners to now indexes around 2,900 x402-enabled endpoints across inference, data, search, media, trading, storage and travel. Note the implied average: at those figures a typical transaction is worth well under a cent, which is precisely the range the protocol was built for. Sources: x402.org; agentic.market. 16:54

🧭 Success Is When Nobody Talks About It

Reppel's definition of winning is the opposite of a token launch.

"I will know x402 has succeeded when it is both ubiquitous and untalked about." 24:58 — Erik Reppel.

The model is HTTP itself: normal people have no idea it exists, and that is exactly why it is powerful — "uncontroversial technology that kind of underpins our daily lives at this point, that there's no debate. You just use it" 25:12–25:23. He wants neutrality, value-accretion and non-bias, so the protocol can fade into the background as agents and humans transact 25:36–25:50. Asked for a timeline he laughs it off — "three months, I don't know" 25:53.

🧵 Running ThesisReppel is arguing that payment infrastructure only succeeds by becoming invisible, and that invisibility requires neutrality, which requires giving up control of the standard. The Linux Foundation donation and the card networks' membership are the same move made twice: remove every reason for a participant to build a competing protocol. Status: developing — the adoption numbers support it; whether it holds once fee-bearing intermediaries have real volume to defend is untested.

His closing advice generalises the instinct: "Your career is best spent playing positive sum games with positive people" 28:10, against a crypto default of zero-sum framings — PvP trading, chains competing — when the interesting work is building what was not possible before 28:19–28:56.

Phase 3: Specific Item Breakdown

🟣 HTTP 402 Payment Required — The Code That Waited Thirty Years

A status code the web reserved for payments and never defined, now carrying the entire x402 handshake.

What It Covers: RFC 9110 §15.5.3 defines it in one sentence: reserved for future use. No semantics, no required headers, no body format. x402 supplies all three without amending the RFC, because a reserved code imposes no constraints on what an extension may do with it.

Why It Matters: Every HTTP client, proxy and CDN already handles a 402 gracefully. That is the difference between a protocol that needs infrastructure adoption and one that needs only application adoption.

🟣 The x402 Handshake — Nine Steps, Two Headers

Request, 402 with terms, signed re-request, verify, work, settle, deliver.

What It Covers: The 402 body carries x402Version, error and an accepts array of PaymentRequirements (scheme, network, amount, asset, payTo, resource, timeout). The client returns the same request with a base64 X-PAYMENT header. The server confirms with X-PAYMENT-RESPONSE containing the settlement result and transaction hash.

Why It Matters: Payment is folded into the existing request/response cycle rather than bolted on as a separate flow. No redirect, no checkout page, no session — which is what makes it usable by a program with no browser.

🟣 The Facilitator — Where the Blockchain Goes

The third-party service that verifies signatures and broadcasts settlement so neither client nor server touches a chain.

What It Covers: POST /verify checks signature validity, payer balance, amount, time window, parameter match and simulates the transfer. POST /settle broadcasts it. GET /supported advertises the schemes and networks it can handle. Trust is bounded by design: a facilitator cannot move funds other than in accordance with the client's signed intent.

Why It Matters: This is the abstraction that makes the protocol adoptable by companies with no crypto team. Reppel's examples — Exa, Fireblocks and others who "just want to do business" 15:56–16:23 — are only possible because gas, RPC and chain selection are somebody else's problem.

🟣 The "exact" Scheme — Gasless, Signature-Based Transfer

EIP-3009 transferWithAuthorization on EVM chains, SPL TransferChecked on Solana.

What It Covers: The payer signs an EIP-712 authorisation specifying from, to, value, validity window and a 32-byte nonce. The facilitator submits it. Replay is blocked by the nonce at the contract level. The payer spends no gas and holds no native token.

Why It Matters: "Signature, not transaction" is the property that makes sub-cent payments viable. A scheme requiring the payer to broadcast would need the payer to hold gas on every chain they transact on, which would defeat the whole premise.

🟣 The x402 Foundation — Deliberate Loss of Control

Coinbase donated the standard to the Linux Foundation and moved development to a neutral organisation, with the card networks inside it.

What It Covers: Launched 2 April 2026 (the GitHub organisation was created 1 April). Visa, Mastercard and American Express are founding members per Reppel. The canonical repository moved to x402-foundation/x402; Coinbase's copy is now a development fork.

Why It Matters: Neutrality is the stated precondition for ubiquity. It also removes the strongest objection an incumbent payment network could have to adopting it — that it would be ratifying a competitor's rail.

🟣 L402 — The Lightning Predecessor

The earlier attempt to make HTTP 402 real, using Lightning invoices and macaroon tokens.

What It Covers: A server returns 402 with a WWW-Authenticate header containing a macaroon token and a Lightning invoice. The client pays, obtains the preimage as proof, and presents token plus preimage in the Authorization header. The macaroon commits to the invoice's payment hash, enabling stateless verification, and can be attenuated and delegated by the bearer.

Why It Matters: It is the closest prior art and the comparison clarifies x402's bet. L402 requires both sides to touch the Lightning Network — a node, channels, inbound liquidity. x402 pushes that burden onto an optional facilitator and settles on general-purpose stablecoin rails, so a server integrating it needs no payments infrastructure at all. The macaroon design does, however, give L402 native delegation semantics that x402's signed-authorisation model handles differently.
