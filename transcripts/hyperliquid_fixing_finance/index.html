Jeff Yan's Deadline: Build A Financial System Humans Own Before AI Renders Us Obsolete

Source When Shift Happens · Date n/a · 89 min
Domain Crypto & Markets ·
Reliability Mixed — a founder describing his own protocol, but unusually checkable: the mechanism claims verify against Hyperliquid's own documentation and DefiLlama, while one supporting analogy (how Ethereum burns fees) is factually wrong and one flagship statistic is presented with an unstated denominator.
Content Type Founder Interview
Full Mode

⚡ The Bottom Line

  • Yan's closing argument reframes the entire company: Hyperliquid is a race against AI, not against exchanges. His thesis is that once intelligence is machine-driven, value transfer will be too — and AI cannot plug into legacy financial rails, so humans have a narrowing window to build a programmable financial system in which they retain a stake.
  • The buyback criticism is answered by denying the premise. Yan's response to "you should buy back more when the price is low" is that Hyperliquid has no discretionary buyback at all — fee conversion and burn are baked into the chain's execution logic, the way a TWAP's slicing is part of the state machine rather than a person's decision.
  • His supporting analogy is wrong in a checkable way. Yan says Ethereum "burns its priority fees" and that they "used to go to the miners." EIP-1559 burns the base fee; priority fees still go to validators. The structural point about non-discretionary protocol burns survives; the fact does not.
  • Eleven people, zero VC, and roughly $700m of annualised protocol revenue. Yan's explanation for the headcount is that Hyperliquid outsources the expensive parts — tokenisation, spot, lending, stablecoins, liquid staking — to independent teams via permissionless primitives, so the "team" is the ecosystem.
  • The 10 October 2025 crash is his case study in a perverse incentive: transparency gets punished. Hyperliquid publishes every order, cancel and liquidation onchain; centralised venues publish one liquidation per second by documented policy. Comparing the totals made Hyperliquid look like the epicentre of a crash it merely reported honestly.

🤖 The Deadline Is AI, And Yan Thinks Crypto Is Building The Escape Hatch

The most consequential minutes of the episode are the last five, where Follonier asks a question shaped as morale-boosting and gets an answer shaped as an ultimatum.

The setup is the state of the industry: founders and investors burnt out, crypto markets disappointing, little innovation in years, and an AI brain drain pulling talent out of the space. Why should anyone stay 85:24–85:50?

"There's a ticking time bomb because AI is going to come and basically supplant human intelligence in the near future."

Yan's argument runs in four steps 86:04–87:32. First, AI will eventually render human intelligence obsolete — he treats this as a when, not an if. Second, once intelligence is largely machine-driven, value transfer becomes largely machine-driven as well, because the two follow each other. Third, AI cannot plug into the legacy financial system: "code and value are not on the same playing field" there, which is a compact way of saying that legacy finance is a permissioned, identity-gated, human-mediated system with no programmable interface at the settlement layer. Fourth, therefore: humans need to finish building a programmable, accessible financial system in which they hold a stake before the handoff, or they will simply be routed around.

💡 Key InsightThe argument inverts the usual crypto-versus-AI framing. The standard version treats AI as a competitor for talent and capital. Yan's version treats AI as the customer — and argues that whoever owns the rails machine intelligence will eventually transact on has the only durable claim on human participation in the economy that follows. Whether or not you accept the premise, it is a materially different reason to build. 87:16

He is careful about the ownership framing. Hyperliquid, he says, is "our best shot," and immediately extends the claim to every builder in crypto, since Hyperliquid "is not built by one team" — he describes the goal as a collective effort to build a family of primitives and companies that interoperate, which the eventual "AI overlords" will adopt because it is autonomous, fair, open and permissionless 87:35–88:06.

⚡ TensionAt 86:09 Yan calls AI a "ticking time bomb" driving urgency for the whole project. Nine seconds later he says "it's actually not as close as people think. I think AI cannot write good code yet" and that important code should not be AI-written 86:20–86:29. Both can be true — a distant deadline still binds — but the urgency he uses to motivate builders sits awkwardly against his own estimate of the timeline, and he does not reconcile them.
🧵 Running ThesisEvery design decision Yan defends in this episode — non-discretionary burns, permissionless deployment, no insider allocation, no discretionary credit extension — is downstream of one requirement: the system has to run correctly without a human in the loop, because eventually there will not be one. Status: confirmed. It is the only frame under which the whole interview is internally consistent.

When Follonier asks what Yan wants Hyperliquid remembered for, the answer is the same thesis compressed: he does not want it remembered for anything, he wants it used forever, in the way he hopes Bitcoin survives as a store of value outside any single power's control 85:05–85:22. "I hope Hyperliquid becomes the internet for money."

🔥 "Hyperliquid Does Not Have A Discretionary Buyback Program"

Follonier puts the criticism directly: smart people in crypto say Hyperliquid should stop buying back when HYPE is expensive and buy more when it is cheap 35:05–35:22. Yan's answer is that the criticism presupposes something that does not exist.

"Hyperliquid is a protocol. It has — it's a rules-based protocol."

His explanation 35:25–37:19 is that protocol fees are converted to HYPE and burned by automated logic, and that the conversion strategy "is in fact baked into the execution logic of the chain itself." His comparison is to an onchain TWAP: nobody sits there deciding when to release the next slice into the book, because the slicing is part of the state machine. Fee conversion works the same way.

He then names the reason the criticism is so sticky. Most venues with tokens are companies with discretionary treasury programmes — centralised exchanges and CeFi/DeFi hybrids buy their own tokens, and for them the timing advice is fair feedback. People reach for the frame they know. Yan's objection is that "Hyperliquid is not a company," and that Hyperliquid Labs is a small group responsible for a critical but limited component of a larger ecosystem 37:26–37:49.

🎯 Against ConsensusThe market treats HYPE buybacks as capital allocation and grades them on execution quality. Yan is arguing they are not allocation at all — they are a supply rule, closer to a monetary policy parameter than a corporate action. If he is right, the entire genre of "Hyperliquid should time its buybacks better" commentary is mis-specified. 35:25

Hyperliquid's own documentation states that "HYPE in the assistance fund is burned, removing the tokens permanently from the circulating and total supply," and that fees are "entirely directed to the community (HLP, the assistance fund, and deployers)." The docs also reference over $1bn in annualised fees flowing toward programmatic HYPE buybacks. The auditable consequence: HYPE total supply had fallen to roughly 955m against the 1bn genesis cap by September 2026 — approximately 45m tokens permanently destroyed. Source: Hyperliquid documentation; CoinGecko supply data.

His structural point — that a protocol-level, non-discretionary burn is not a corporate buyback and nobody asks Ethereum's developers to time it — is entirely correct and does not depend on the error. The closing move in this section is a reframing of what Hyperliquid is. Yan concedes it is good that the system drives value to its native token, but insists this "is not the same way that any company drives value to its shareholders" — Hyperliquid is a neutral platform on which finance is built, and the better analogy is the internet: what the internet did for information, Hyperliquid is trying to do for finance 38:28–39:00.

📜 AnalogYan reaches for AWS when describing neutrality later in the episode — "nobody says hey, why are you building on AWS, that's such an opinionated choice" 52:47–53:01. It is a revealing choice because AWS is corporate-controlled, and he says so. His point is about the felt experience of infrastructure: successful infrastructure disappears into the background, and he wants that property without the corporate control that currently accompanies it. 52:47

🧮 Eleven People Works Because The Expensive Work Is Deliberately Not Theirs

Follonier presses on headcount from two angles — why so few, and why comparable decentralised protocols have far more 17:16–17:40.

Yan's answer has a structural half and a personal half, and the structural half is the interesting one 17:41–20:10. At a centralised exchange, tokenisation alone is an organisational tree: a team for crypto asset onboarding, a team integrating traditional finance price feeds, teams managing the multisigs, each with a lead, engineers and coordination overhead. You scale horizontally under one umbrella. Hyperliquid "doesn't actually do any of these things." It is a set of primitives by which anyone can tokenise anything in different ways — so the real team includes every builder carrying responsibility for a building block others plug into.

The personal half is straightforward preference: he enjoys working with a small number of very good, very committed people rather than a larger set each handling a small slice 20:10–20:20.

The operating rule he states is unambiguous, and he admits it is hard to apply 20:24–22:02:

"The basic answer is if it can be built externally, it should be."

His example of following the rule under pressure is HIP-3 — a mechanism nobody had attempted, which he says more people expected to fail than succeed. The complaints were not answerable on their own terms: a user saying "I trade on this other platform and they just do everything in house and it works exactly like they say it should" is stating something true. Yan's position is that you cannot argue with it; you have to believe the principle. "Maybe you get there faster if you do it in a fully centralised way, but you build a much more resilient and robust and ultimately globally scalable system if you build it the hard way."

🔗 ConnectionHeadcount → the AI thesis. A system meant to keep running correctly without human discretion cannot have a large human organisation at its centre making calls; the small team is not an efficiency story, it is the same design constraint that produces the non-discretionary burn and the permissionless deployment model. (19:46 → 87:16)
🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed on the structural facts. Hyperliquid took no venture capital — Jeff Yan self-funded it from the profits of the market-making firm he had built, and the November 2024 genesis distributed 310m HYPE, 31% of the 1bn fixed supply, to more than 90,000 users with no VC allocation. The eleven-person figure is consistently reported. For scale: DefiLlama's trailing-year figures put Hyperliquid at roughly $933m in fees and $701m in protocol revenue. Source: DefiLlama; multiple contemporaneous profiles of Hyperliquid's funding history.

It is also true that HIP-3 open interest has been dominated by a single deployer, TradeXYZ — permissionless in design, highly concentrated in practice. 🔦 10/10: The Crash Where Transparency Became A Liability

Asked how FUD affects him personally, Yan gives a specific and unusually candid answer about the October 2025 crash 32:41–35:00.

His framing: the last half year in crypto had been volatile in the bad direction, and "10/10 was a good example where there was a lot of damage caused, and because Hyperliquid was the only transparent venue, people kind of latched on to it." He describes spending that week on what he calls a mission to educate people, and he is not neutral about why the narrative spread — he says competitors from several angles were mispainting Hyperliquid's virtues with negativity "to deflect from real problems on other platforms," and that it is "very obvious that they know what they're doing."

The mechanism he explains is the part worth keeping, because it generalises to any transparency comparison 33:58–34:43. Analytics sites published lists showing Hyperliquid with more liquidations than centralised exchanges. But Hyperliquid puts every order, cancel and liquidation onchain, while — per those exchanges' own documentation, which he notes nobody reads — several of them "only push the first liquidation in every second," with no explanation attached. Adding up two differently-constructed numbers produces a ratio that means nothing, and can make the honest venue look catastrophically worse.

💡 Key InsightThis is a general hazard, not a crypto one: when one participant reports at full resolution and its competitors report a sampled subset, naive aggregation systematically penalises the transparent party. Every cross-venue comparison table needs the reporting methodology checked before the numbers are compared — and almost none of them do it. 34:26
🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed on the event and Yan's characterisation of the data problem. On 10 October 2025, more than $19bn of crypto leverage was liquidated in roughly a day — the largest single-day liquidation event on record, triggered by a sudden tariff announcement against a market sitting at record open interest. Hyperliquid recorded roughly $10.31bn of that, over half the market total, precisely because it reports every liquidation while several centralised venues throttle reporting. Subsequent analysis also found that realised trader losses were a small fraction of the headline $19bn. Source: CoinDesk Research; FTI Consulting analysis; contemporaneous reporting on exchange liquidation reporting policies.
⚡ TensionAt 08:03 Yan says Hyperliquid "is known for not really caring about what the meta is or what people think," and describes the approach as leading by example. At 31:47–32:26 he says the opposite has become policy: his instinct was that "this is clearly false, the truth will come to light," and "I've learned that that is not always the right mentality" — so they now actively correct falsehoods and he defers to people better at communications. This is a real change of position within the same conversation, and it is to his credit that he states it plainly rather than pretending the posture never moved.

🏗️ Perps Are Not A Crypto Instrument — HIP-3 And The Migration Bet

Yan's framing of perpetual futures is the most economically interesting claim in the episode, and it deliberately strips the instrument of its crypto identity 44:39–46:11.

Perps, he notes, existed in academic literature before crypto; BitMEX demonstrated the practical application — a more efficient means of price discovery that concentrates liquidity in a single instrument that never expires. His conclusion follows directly: "there is nothing unique about crypto when it comes to perps." Therefore perps should exist on most liquid instruments for which futures and options already exist. He is careful not to overclaim — not that perps are strictly better than everything else, but that users should have the option — and then makes the prediction: if perps were widely adopted across asset classes in traditional finance, "a large percentage of the flow would migrate to perps," because perps let users express the view they actually want to express.

HIP-3 is the implementation: anyone can deploy a perp market on Hyperliquid. Yan is clear that the one-sentence description hides the work 46:11–47:07. Open questions included whether a protocol could really cede something as safety-critical as real-time oracle updates to a permissionless deployer system, and how permissionless deployment interacts with margining across a long tail of edge cases. He adds a second, non-technical cost: builders had to commit to learning something entirely new with no precedent.

Asked why it matters versus what everyone else does, his answer is about specialisation 48:28–51:31. Every non-crypto perp effort he has seen is an exchange bolting new price feeds onto an existing perp operation. His counter-model observes that finance is deeply specialised for reasons that are not all inefficiency: different countries, different asset classes, different venues, different rules, different interfaces — "finance is just too big for one team to build." He expects the biggest financial companies to pursue the same unified-financial-life goal through acquisitions, partnerships and in-house building, and calls that "a very brittle setup," because blowups tend to happen when too much power sits with one entity.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Directionally right, denominator unstated. Yan says HIP-3 markets, predominantly those run by TradeXYZ as the first deployer, "have reached approximately 2% of global volume on silver" 55:08–55:24. Public reporting on the same markets puts HIP-3's gold and silver volume at roughly 1% of COMEX volume — COMEX being the world's largest metals derivatives venue but not "global volume." Separately, TradeXYZ's silver perps did $4.09bn on a single day in early February 2026, about 68% of all HIP-3 activity that day, with HIP-3 open interest peaking above $1bn. The scale is real and the achievement is genuine given the markets launched months earlier; the specific percentage depends entirely on a denominator he does not name. Source: The Block; KuCoin market analysis; public HIP-3 dashboards.
🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed on HIP-3's design. Hyperliquid's documentation states that "the deployer of a perp market is responsible for market definition... and market operation, including setting oracle prices," with a 500,000 HYPE staking requirement to deploy and the ability for deployers to retain up to 50% of trading fees from their markets. This substantiates Yan's claim that the protocol genuinely ceded oracle responsibility to permissionless deployers rather than retaining a veto. Source: Hyperliquid documentation.

Yan's credit allocation in this section is worth noting because it cuts against his own interest. Asked for numbers that prove Hyperliquid is doing perps right, he first refuses the frame — "I'm still going to say that it is early," and "the numbers today don't necessarily reflect whether long-term it is going to be successful" — then gives the metals example and immediately assigns the credit elsewhere: "this is nothing to do with the core team... how that liquidity infrastructure is used is entirely up to the builders" 53:57–56:14.

🔗 ConnectionHIP-3 → outcome markets → portfolio margin. All three follow the same template: identify a primitive that multiple independent builders keep asking for, build it as small and general as possible at the protocol layer, and move the opinionated decisions out to the deployers. Yan states this design rule explicitly at 58:19 — be very selective about what the protocol does versus what happens on the general-purpose EVM. (46:11 → 64:42 → 79:24)

🧿 HyperEVM Is A Portal Into HyperCore, And Yan Admits The Story Has Not Landed

Asked for the simple version, Yan gives it: HyperEVM lets people deploy contracts they are used to deploying on Ethereum, ported over and working — "a blank slate on which smart contracts can execute" 39:15–39:57.

Asked for his honest take on its success, he gives the more interesting version, including the concession 40:47–42:02. He rejects the premise that it has failed, then drills down: HyperEVM is actually a way for smart contracts, for the first time, to tap into native primitives built onchain. The correct evaluation frame is that it is "a portal into Hyperliquid," which has no analogy on other EVM chains — and therefore comparing it to self-contained EVM ecosystems produces the wrong verdict. He concedes directly that this "needs to be kind of packaged a bit more as a digestible narrative."

His concrete example is the best argument in the section 42:12–43:20. A long-running criticism was that Circle had not integrated native USDC. Yan's explanation: HyperCore is a completely native implementation of primitives like balance ledgers, and it is very hard for any company with existing general-purpose-chain infrastructure to interface with something like that. HyperEVM solves it by letting native USDC be minted fully within Circle's own hub-and-spoke network of supported minting environments — burn on any chain, mint on any other, with HyperEVM as one of those chains.

Then the honest close 43:26–44:04: the reason HyperEVM has no visible success stories is partly that "most of them are yet to be built," and partly that the good ones "feel native" — a well-executed integration hides the machinery that made it possible, which is exactly the property that makes the narrative hard to tell.

💡 Key InsightYan has identified a structural marketing problem, not a product problem: infrastructure whose value is seamlessness cannot demonstrate its value through user-visible evidence, because the evidence of success is the absence of friction. Every integration that works perfectly removes a data point from his own argument. 43:47

📐 Outcome Markets: Adding Convexity To A Linear System

The cleanest piece of reasoning in the interview arrives when Yan explains why Hyperliquid needs a third primitive 64:42–66:25.

Spot and perps look very different — perps allow leverage and capital efficiency, spot represents tokenised ownership of an actual asset — but they share a limiting property: both can only express linear beliefs. "If you hold perps, no matter whether you're at 1x or 100x leverage... when the price moves one unit you make n units of profit or loss." Leverage scales n; it does not change the shape of the payoff.

He then identifies the demand that shape leaves unserved. Some users want convexity: something worth one dollar or zero depending on an event, or something bounded. His worked example is protective: you hold Bitcoin, you are broadly happy holding it, but below some price you want downside protection. "You cannot express these beliefs through any combination of spot and perp."

Outcome markets are his answer, and he defines them plainly: fully collateralised contracts where both sides post capital and settlement exchanges collateral somewhere in between — binary or continuous 66:32–66:59. Options and prediction markets are the obvious instances; he also gestures at markets that aggregate opinions where there is no objective outcome, such as surfacing which issues matter.

The user-facing property he emphasises is the absence of liquidation risk 67:04–67:22. Someone who wants to put up capital, walk away and check back later, without funding mechanics or the possibility of being liquidated on a wick, cannot get that from perps. He is blunt that perps are "a relatively advanced feature" and observes that people who use perp leverage are often really reaching for an options payoff they cannot otherwise access.

🎯 Against ConsensusThe prevailing view treats prediction markets as a consumer category — election betting, sports, culture. Yan treats them as a special case of a general derivatives primitive that belongs in the protocol layer next to spot and perps. That reframing makes prediction markets a piece of financial plumbing rather than a product, which is a materially larger claim than the one most prediction-market advocates make. 66:32

🏦 "Housing All Of Finance" Means Composability Without A Landlord

Follonier asks Yan to define a phrase he uses often, and the definition has two independent halves 69:05–70:50.

The first half is not about crypto at all: the financial system should not be fragmented. A user should not have to plug into a bank account, transfer funds to another account to buy one thing, and to a third for something else. Having a person's financial life composable in one place is, in Yan's word, "a huge unlock" — and he explicitly concedes that centralised institutions can deliver this.

The second half is the crypto-specific requirement: the platform on which this happens should not be controlled by a central corporation. Many teams should build, anything one team builds should automatically compose with what others build, and it is those network effects that "really make it a financial system."

He describes the resulting design problem as a spectrum. At one end, fragmentation with composability. At the other, a more limited, centrally operated, efficient system. Yan's claim is that done right you can capture the advantages of both ends, and that this tension "drives a lot of the design philosophy behind what we do."

The same structure appears in his answer about whether Hyperliquid is a crypto company 71:06–72:22. He says DeFi values are in the DNA, but rejects the identity of "building the best crypto exchange" — "finance is finance, and crypto has the potential to upgrade the rails on which people conduct their financial lives." He is relaxed about how success would be interpreted: some would call it crypto vindicated, others would call it a new thing that borrowed some ideas, and he considers both readings fine.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed on the market-position claim implicit in this section. Hyperliquid's share of onchain perpetuals volume ran in the 32–44% range across 2026 depending on the measurement window, with trailing 30-day volume of $172bn–$245bn — several multiples of the second-ranked venue. The claim that Hyperliquid is the reference venue for onchain derivatives is supported by third-party data, not just by the founder. Source: DefiLlama derivatives tracker; contemporaneous market analyses.

When Follonier revives a panel quote — "we have no competition" — Yan neither disowns nor doubles down 80:59–83:38. He clarifies that the question was probably about who exactly competes with Hyperliquid, and that there are many competitors "in some sense," because Hyperliquid sits at the intersection of many things. His substantive point is that much of what looks like competition should be cooperation: protocols are increasingly adding Hyperliquid as a chain, and he reports builders telling him a product launched on Hyperliquid became their best-performing launch of the year.

The part he does defend is narrower and more defensible: Hyperliquid is building something nobody else is precisely trying to build, and the reason is path dependence. He notes he has worked on this for more than three years, that many community members have been there from day one, and that "the fact that Hyperliquid got here with a no-insider principle from day one" shaped what could be built afterwards. The specific thing nobody else attempts, in his telling: when others launch perps, nobody says they trust the community and outside builders to execute the markets themselves.

🧊 The Culture: No Champagne, Full-Day Interviews, And A Single Veto

Several short exchanges assemble into an unusually concrete picture of how Hyperliquid Labs actually operates.

Hiring screens for integrity, and the test is duration. Yan says there is no perfect method, so candidates work with the team for at least a full day — his reasoning is that a human being outside "a super time constrained simulated environment" emits soft signals a technical interview cannot capture 26:51–27:26.

The decision rule is a veto, not a vote. Everyone on the team must feel good about a hire. There is nothing formal, but if anyone holds a medium-to-strong "no" or higher, that is usually enough to end it. Follonier compresses it accurately — "if it's not a hell yeah, it's a no" — and Yan agrees 27:39–28:29.

Motivation after the airdrop was left to sort itself out. Asked how he keeps people motivated after they get rich overnight, Yan says there is nothing he can do about it, and that the integrity bar they treat as non-negotiable naturally selects for people not optimising for a quick financial outcome — "if they wanted to do that, and they're so smart, then they can definitely do it in other ways" 22:28–23:28. He reports nobody had left at the time of recording, that he did not spend much time thinking about it beforehand, and that if anything people appear more excited and feel more ownership. His read: "people are motivated not by the amount of value you can extract by the system, but by the amount of value you can produce."

There was no celebration, and he thinks that is a little sad. Follonier raises the story of a company that had champagne waiting at an IPO and whose team drank water and went back to work until midnight. Yan cannot recall any Hyperliquid celebration at all, and volunteers that "it is a bit sad," because there were many things worth celebrating and there is never a good moment to pause. He suggests maybe decades from now, when the system is fully autonomous and complete 24:39–26:44.

Sleep is not managed on the input side. Yan says pushing people on inputs is "a recipe for disaster" — if someone needs sleep and is not sleeping, anything they produce is worse than if they had slept. He notes the quality bar is high enough that good engineering requires being fully awake, and he is openly dismissive of all-nighter-flexing culture, while noting he personally works more hours than most people who flex it 28:36–30:01.

💡 Key InsightThe culture Yan describes has a single organising property: it optimises for output correctness rather than observable effort. The full-day interview, the unanimity veto, the refusal to manage sleep, and the absence of celebration all follow from a belief that in safety-critical financial infrastructure the only thing that matters is whether the work is right. It is also, notably, not a culture that scales to hundreds of people — which is consistent with an architecture designed never to need them. 29:52

🔒 Total Transparency For The Protocol, Total Privacy For The Contributor

The episode's hardest question gets its weakest answer, and the gap is worth marking precisely because everything else in the interview holds up.

Follonier raises the FUD directly: community fear that roughly $300m of employee-vested tokens would hit the market every month for a long time, and asks what has actually happened 30:29–30:43.

Yan declines, and grounds the refusal in principle rather than convenience 30:45–31:40. He argues financial privacy is a right and part of why people are in crypto; that you would not demand a community member disclose everything they do with tokens they bought; and that the line is drawn at personal privacy. He then states the other half sharply, and it is a genuinely high standard: "everything should be very transparent on how the protocol works — every dollar needs to be accounted for," and anything in the system belonging to a person "needs to actually belong to that person." What any contributor does with their own tokens, he says, is not his business.

🌐 Consensus CheckThe position is internally consistent and matches how Hyperliquid operates elsewhere. It also leaves a real gap: without aggregate disclosure — team wallet clusters, net flows, or even a periodic statement of whether insider selling is occurring at scale — holders cannot falsify either the rumour or the denial. Several protocols publish aggregate insider flow without identifying individuals, which would satisfy both of Yan's stated principles at once. He does not address why Hyperliquid does not. 31:02

The same tension surfaces in his answer on fairness, which is more satisfying. Asked where fairness has cost the most, Yan says he does not know concretely, because it has never been a decision framed as a cost-benefit trade-off — "that's not how we think." Then the concession that makes it credible 84:06–84:47:

"If you are willing to compromise on integrity and fairness and you cut the corners then you can grow a lot faster. I think that's just undeniable."

He points at FTX as the illustration — hyper-scaled, and had they not been caught, "they would have gotten away with it all." His conclusion is that for some people this is a cost-benefit analysis; for Hyperliquid it is not, because the cost of being wrong "could be infinite."

🕰️ The 2018 Prediction Market That Failed Because Nobody Wanted Anything Onchain

Follonier surfaces old footage of Yan launching prediction markets in 2018, at roughly the same time as Kalshi, and asks why it did not work 61:32–61:46.

Yan's answer is a founder post-mortem without excuses 61:49–63:26. The best explanation is that "we weren't ready." He believes the infrastructure thinking was largely right — offchain matching with onchain settlement was the correct architecture for 2018. But "there's a lot more that goes into building something successful than simply having the right ideas, and the ideas are actually a very very small part of it."

The binding constraint was demand, and the timing was brutal. It was not obvious to him then that anyone wanted onchain products at all. The idea started when prices were up and everyone was happy; by the end of that year prices were far down. His note on what that does to a first-time founder is the human centre of the episode: when you talk to users and literally nobody will try your product, "it can be very disheartening."

He is gracious about who did succeed — genuinely pleased for the teams that started around the same time and are now seeing mass adoption after years of work.

🔗 Connection2018 → outcome markets. Yan is returning to prediction markets eight years later, but not as a product. Having failed to build a standalone prediction market into a market that did not exist, he is now building the primitive — outcome markets — beneath a venue that already has the users. It is the same idea rebuilt at a different layer of the stack. (63:04 → 64:42)
🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed on the comparison. Kalshi — the "Kali" in the transcript — was founded in 2018 by Tarek Mansour and Luana Lopes Lara, who met at MIT, and became a CFTC-regulated event-contract exchange. Yan's framing of contemporaries who "did have what it takes" and are now seeing mass adoption is accurate: Kalshi reached a multi-billion-dollar valuation and mainstream traction in 2025–26. Source: Kalshi company history; CNBC and Britannica profiles.

His related observation about spot trading closes the philosophical loop 59:47–61:30. Spot, he argues, is the closest thing to Satoshi's peer-to-peer vision, because trading spot really is two parties transferring assets on a ledger and the order book is only a mechanism to intermediate those transfers. He is "very confident" Satoshi would have said trading should happen fully onchain, though not necessarily on Bitcoin itself. And his evidence that this matters to users is behavioural: several 2025 launches saw Hyperliquid become the leading source of spot price discovery, which he believes was a first for a decentralised venue — people trading spot crypto tend to be believers in the vision, and being able to transact consistently with that belief without sacrificing UX "feels a bit like going full circle."

Phase 3: Specific Item Breakdown

🟣 HYPE And The Assistance Fund — A Supply Rule, Not A Treasury Programme

Protocol fees are converted to HYPE and burned by chain logic that nobody operates, which is why Yan says buyback-timing advice is addressed to a programme that does not exist.

What It Includes: Automated conversion of protocol fees into HYPE, executed as part of the chain's state machine, with the purchased tokens burned from circulating and total supply. Genesis distributed 310m HYPE (31% of a 1bn fixed supply) to more than 90,000 users in November 2024, with no venture allocation.

The Evidence: Yan's analogy is an onchain TWAP — nobody decides when the next slice hits the book, because the slicing is the execution 36:52–37:19. Hyperliquid's documentation confirms the burn and states fees are "entirely directed to the community." The auditable proof is supply: roughly 955m total HYPE against the 1bn cap by September 2026. The one defect is his Ethereum comparison: EIP-1559 burns the base fee, not the priority fee, which still goes to validators. The structure of his argument is unaffected; the fact is wrong.

🟣 HIP-3 — Permissionless Perps On Anything Liquid

Hyperliquid ceded oracle operation and market definition to permissionless deployers, betting that perps are a general financial instrument rather than a crypto one.

What It Includes: Any deployer meeting a 500,000 HYPE staking requirement can define and operate a perp market, including setting oracle prices, and retain up to 50% of that market's trading fees. Deployers can list up to three assets without auction participation.

The Evidence: Yan says more people expected HIP-3 to fail than succeed, and that the hard part was not the sentence but the edge cases — whether a protocol could safely cede real-time oracle updates, and how permissionless deployment intertwines with margining 46:11–47:07. The results he cites: HIP-3 metals markets, predominantly TradeXYZ's, reaching what he calls ~2% of global silver volume. Public data puts HIP-3 gold and silver near 1% of COMEX volume, with TradeXYZ's silver alone doing $4.09bn on a single day in early 2026 and HIP-3 open interest peaking above $1bn. Real scale, unstated denominator, and heavy concentration in one deployer.

🟣 HyperEVM — A Portal Into HyperCore, Not A Standalone Chain

An EVM environment whose actual purpose is giving smart contracts access to HyperCore's native primitives — a framing Yan concedes has failed to reach the public narrative.

What It Includes: Standard EVM contract deployment, plus precompiles and core-writer primitives that let contracts read from and write to HyperCore's native ledgers, order book and staking system.

The Evidence: Yan's strongest case is native USDC: HyperCore's native balance-ledger implementation is very hard for a company with general-purpose-chain infrastructure to interface with, and HyperEVM lets Circle mint natively inside its own burn-and-mint network with HyperEVM as one supported chain 42:12–43:20. His concession is equally clear — most success stories "are yet to be built," and the good ones "feel native," which is precisely why they generate no visible evidence. He also says the framing "needs to be kind of packaged a bit more as a digestible narrative."

🟣 Outcome Markets — The Convexity Primitive

Fully collateralised binary or continuous contracts that let users express nonlinear beliefs which no combination of spot and perps can produce.

What It Includes: Both sides post collateral; settlement exchanges collateral somewhere between the bounds. Binary and continuous variants. Options and prediction markets are the obvious instances; Yan also gestures at opinion-aggregation markets with no objective resolution.

The Evidence: The reasoning is a derivatives identity rather than a claim: at any leverage, a perp pays n units per unit of price move, so leverage scales the payoff without bending it 64:52–65:18. His motivating case is downside protection on a spot Bitcoin holding below a chosen floor. The secondary benefit he emphasises is the absence of liquidation risk and funding mechanics — post capital, check back later 67:04–67:22. He also notes many perp users are reaching for options exposure they cannot otherwise get.

🟣 USDH And The Aligned-Stablecoin Design

A protocol-level stablecoin standard where treasury yield is split between the protocol and the issuer, encoded so HYPE holders and stablecoin users are aligned without anyone's discretion.

What It Includes: A "protocolised" aligned-stablecoin concept in which reserve yield flows to a mixture of the protocol and the deployer, traders using the stablecoin pay lower fees, and the entire arrangement is encoded at protocol level. USDH itself is operated by Native Markets.

The Evidence: Yan situates it in the post-GENIUS Act stablecoin wave and says the design emerged from extended back-and-forth with ecosystem participants 72:34–74:38. His framing: "the protocol earns yield and in that way the holders of hype are aligned with the stable coin," with traders paying lower fees — "a purely objective system." Externally: Native Markets won the USDH ticker on 14 September 2025 through a competitive governance vote against Paxos, Ethena, Frax, Sky and BitGo, proposing off-chain reserves managed by BlackRock and on-chain assets via Superstate; USDH launched 24 September 2025. The GENIUS Act he references was signed into law on 18 July 2025.

🟣 Kinetiq — Liquid Staking Built Entirely Onchain

The largest liquid staking protocol on Hyperliquid, and Yan's proof that HyperEVM's core-writer primitives eliminate the offchain component every other LST depends on.

What It Includes: A smart contract that tokenises a pool of staked HYPE while performing staking, unstaking and accounting entirely within the system, using precompiles and core-writer primitives to read from and write to HyperCore.

The Evidence: Yan's technical point is that staking normally sits a layer below the application layer, so even the premier liquid staking token on Ethereum has an offchain component in its tokenisation 74:39–76:12. On Hyperliquid the entire loop closes onchain, which he says "eliminates all sort of bridging and trust risk." Externally, Kinetiq's kHYPE is the dominant LST in the Hyperliquid staking ecosystem — reported at over 80% share, with TVL peaking in the billions — which supports Yan's "biggest liquid staking token" claim within Hyperliquid.

🟣 HyperLend And Portfolio Margin — The Hardest Feature Built The Hard Way

Portfolio margin routed through real lending markets rather than protocol-minted credit, so that demand spikes raise borrow rates instead of creating solvency risk.

What It Includes: HyperLend as the leading borrow-lend market on HyperEVM, positioned to back portfolio margin on HyperCore — the feature letting traders post any liquid asset as collateral and trade anything against it.

The Evidence: Yan's explanation of why this is hard is the most technically substantive passage in the episode 77:32–80:41. On a centralised venue, portfolio margin works because the exchange can mint balances from nothing against sitting collateral — usually fine, and the source of bad debt in black swans. He credits the exchanges for managing it well while arguing the model is unacceptable in DeFi, because it means everyone with funds on the protocol carries solvency risk and "every dollar is accounted for" stops being true. Hyperliquid's alternative: lending markets fully back the margin operations, so a demand spike produces a brief borrow-rate increase that suppliers compete back down. He explicitly compares the difficulty to HIP-3 — a much harder way to build a feature users will never notice. Portfolio margin was pre-alpha at recording.

🟣 Unit And Onchain Spot Price Discovery

Hyperliquid's spot layer, built by an outside team, which in 2025 made a decentralised venue the leading source of spot price discovery for the first time.

What It Includes: Tokenisation and spot markets on Hyperliquid, built by the Unit team rather than by Hyperliquid Labs — the clearest single instance of Yan's "if it can be built externally, it should be" rule.

The Evidence: Yan points to several 2025 launches where Hyperliquid was the leading source of spot price discovery, naming XPL as one, and says he believes it was the first time this happened on a decentralised venue 59:20–59:45. His philosophical case: spot trading is closest to Satoshi's peer-to-peer vision because trading spot is literally two parties transferring assets on a ledger, with the order book as intermediation mechanism 59:47–60:23. His behavioural case: people trading spot crypto are usually believers in the vision, and being able to do it onchain without sacrificing UX "feels a bit like going full circle."
