Jeff Yan's Deadline: Build A Financial System Humans Own Before AI Renders Us Obsolete

Source When Shift Happens · Date n/a · 89 min
Domain Crypto & Markets ·
Reliability Mixed — a founder describing his own protocol, but unusually checkable: the mechanism claims verify against Hyperliquid's own documentation and DefiLlama, while one supporting analogy (how Ethereum burns fees) is factually wrong and one flagship statistic is presented with an unstated denominator.
Content Type Founder Interview
Full Mode

⚡ The Bottom Line

  • Yan's closing argument reframes the entire company: Hyperliquid is a race against AI, not against exchanges. His thesis is that once intelligence is machine-driven, value transfer will be too — and AI cannot plug into legacy financial rails, so humans have a narrowing window to build a programmable financial system in which they retain a stake.
  • The buyback criticism is answered by denying the premise. Yan's response to "you should buy back more when the price is low" is that Hyperliquid has no discretionary buyback at all — fee conversion and burn are baked into the chain's execution logic, the way a TWAP's slicing is part of the state machine rather than a person's decision.
  • His supporting analogy is wrong in a checkable way. Yan says Ethereum "burns its priority fees" and that they "used to go to the miners." EIP-1559 burns the base fee; priority fees still go to validators. The structural point about non-discretionary protocol burns survives; the fact does not.
  • Eleven people, zero VC, and roughly $700m of annualised protocol revenue. Yan's explanation for the headcount is that Hyperliquid outsources the expensive parts — tokenisation, spot, lending, stablecoins, liquid staking — to independent teams via permissionless primitives, so the "team" is the ecosystem.
  • The 10 October 2025 crash is his case study in a perverse incentive: transparency gets punished. Hyperliquid publishes every order, cancel and liquidation onchain; centralised venues publish one liquidation per second by documented policy. Comparing the totals made Hyperliquid look like the epicentre of a crash it merely reported honestly.

🔑 Key Claims (Ranked)

1. There is a deadline on building a human-owned financial system, and AI sets it ↳ Evidence: Yan argues that once AI self-improves past human intelligence, value transfer becomes machine-driven, and that AI cannot interface with legacy finance because "code and value are not on the same playing field" there. 86:04–88:06 ↳ Caveat: He also says AI is further away than people think and cannot yet write code important enough to trust — which weakens the urgency of his own deadline. ↳ Consensus: Contrarian — the standard crypto-versus-AI framing is competition for talent and capital, not a handoff window.

2. Hyperliquid has no discretionary buyback, so buyback-timing advice does not apply to it ↳ Evidence: Fee-to-HYPE conversion and burn are encoded in the chain's execution logic, not executed by a treasury desk; Hyperliquid's docs confirm HYPE in the Assistance Fund is burned from circulating and total supply. 35:25–37:19 ↳ Caveat: The Ethereum comparison he reaches for is wrong on the specifics, and "not discretionary today" is a property of current code, not a constitutional guarantee. ↳ Consensus: Aligned with the protocol's documentation; contrarian against how most of the market discusses it.

3. Eleven people works because the expensive parts are deliberately built by other teams ↳ Evidence: Yan's rule is "if it can be built externally, it should be" — spot by Unit, stablecoin by Native Markets, liquid staking by Kinetiq, lending by HyperLend, HIP-3 markets by deployers like TradeXYZ. (17:41–21:58, 24:31) ↳ Caveat: He concedes the centralised path is faster and often delivers a better initial user experience. ↳ Consensus: Contrarian — every comparable venue scales headcount horizontally.

4. Perps are not a crypto instrument, and most TradFi flow would migrate to them if offered ↳ Evidence: Yan traces perps to pre-crypto academic literature and credits BitMEX with the practical demonstration; HIP-3 exists to let anyone deploy perps on any liquid instrument. 44:39–46:11 ↳ Caveat: "A large percentage of the flow would migrate" is a prediction with no evidence attached beyond early metals volume. ↳ Consensus: Emerging — TradFi perp interest is real but nascent.

5. Transparency is a competitive liability in the short run and the moat in the long run ↳ Evidence: On 10/10 Hyperliquid published every liquidation while rivals publish "the first liquidation in every second" per their own docs; the resulting comparison was, in Yan's word, misinformation. 32:51–35:00 ↳ Consensus: Aligned — the mechanism is documented; the dispute is about who amplified it.

6. HyperEVM is being judged as an L2-style ecosystem when it is a portal into HyperCore ↳ Evidence: Yan's point is that the EVM's purpose is giving smart contracts access to native onchain primitives — his example is native USDC minting, which Circle could not do against HyperCore directly. 40:47–43:51 ↳ Caveat: He concedes the narrative has not landed and that most success stories "are yet to be built." ↳ Consensus: Contrarian, and he admits it is losing the argument publicly.

7. Outcome markets are the missing primitive: spot and perps can only express linear beliefs ↳ Evidence: Whether at 1x or 100x, a perp pays n units per unit of price move; convexity — a payoff bounded at zero and one, or downside protection above a floor — cannot be constructed from any combination of spot and perps. 64:42–66:25 ↳ Consensus: Aligned as a matter of derivatives mathematics; contrarian as a chain-level primitive.

8. Portfolio margin is only safe in DeFi if a real lending market backs it ↳ Evidence: Centralised venues extend credit against collateral by minting balances from nothing, which is usually fine and produces bad debt in black swans; Hyperliquid routes the same feature through borrow-lend markets so demand spikes hit rates rather than protocol solvency. 76:58–80:41 ↳ Caveat: Portfolio margin was still pre-alpha at the time of recording. ↳ Consensus: Emerging — the clearest technical argument in the episode.

9. Employee vesting disclosure is refused on principle, not convenience ↳ Evidence: Yan declines to discuss the roughly $300m/month unlock FUD, arguing protocol transparency is non-negotiable but individual balances are private. 30:29–31:40 ↳ Caveat: He offers no aggregate alternative — no team-wallet dashboard, no disclosure of net flows — so holders cannot verify either the FUD or the denial. ↳ Consensus: Contrarian, and the weakest answer in the episode.
