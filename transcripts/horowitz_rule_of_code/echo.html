Rule Of Code As Exit From A Politicised Bench — And The Two Cases That Undercut The Diagnosis

Source Network State Podcast · Date n/a · 40 min
Domain Law, Governance & Crypto
Reliability Expert opinion — two unusually well-placed practitioners, but the two legal cases carrying the argument's weight are characterised inaccurately, and both were substantially reversed on appeal after the likely recording date
Content Type Conference Interview (Balaji Srinivasan × Ben Horowitz)
Full Mode

⚡ The Bottom Line

  • Horowitz's case for "rule of code" is not utopian — it is a claimed response to judicial discretion, and a16z has already acted on it by reincorporating in Nevada. His diagnosis: the US advantage was never the law itself but its predictability, and that "presumed that you didn't have people who were above the law who were judges and prosecutors." 25:53–26:52
  • Both cases he cites were substantially reversed on appeal — which is evidence that appellate review works slowly and expensively, not that it has stopped working. The Delaware Supreme Court reversed the Tesla rescission on 19 December 2025 ($1 nominal damages, fee award cut from $345m to ~$55m); the New York Appellate Division vacated the ~$500m Trump penalty on 21 August 2025 as an excessive fine. Neither reversal appears in the conversation.
  • The Delaware Supreme Court declined to hold what Horowitz asserts. It took the "narrower" of three available paths — rescission was an improper remedy — and expressly did not decide whether the pay plan was entirely fair or whether stockholders had ratified it. Chancellor McCormick's entire-fairness framework was left undisturbed, which is the opposite of "made up her own law."
  • Balaji's "a smart contract is rule of law" collapses rule by rule into rule of law. Smart contracts deliver Fuller's congruence limb perfectly — the declared rule is the executed rule — while abolishing the institutional limbs every canonical account requires: an independent judiciary, accessible courts, natural justice, and equitable escape valves for mistake, duress and unconscionability.
  • Horowitz imposes a sequencing constraint that most rule-of-code advocacy skips: no law on chain until keys are as common as browsers. "We have to get the infrastructure deployed more broadly first... not everybody has a wallet, not everybody has keys." He wants money distribution and voting on that rail before law follows. 33:48–34:55

🔑 Key Claims (Ranked)

1. Rule of law in the US has been politicised to the point where predictability — its actual economic value — is gone ↳ Evidence (Horowitz): "A huge strength of the US has been rule of law and the amount of case law and the predictability of the law. But that presumed that you didn't have people who were above the law who were judges and prosecutors." 26:21–26:52 ↳ Caveat: Both supporting cases were reversed on appeal within months, and Delaware's legislature separately enacted SB 21 in March 2025 to narrow controlling-stockholder liability. The corrective mechanisms fired. ↳ Consensus: Contrarian in framing, aligned in substance — the DExit trend is real and measurable, with Delaware's share of IPO incorporations falling to ~62% in 2025 against Nevada's ~17%.

2. Delaware's Chancery Court "chose to completely ignore the law and make up her own law" in the Tesla compensation case ↳ Evidence (Horowitz): He adds that "the board of directors didn't have a say and then even the shareholders didn't have a say." 26:52–27:30 ↳ Caveat: Inaccurate as a description of the holding. The Chancery applied entire fairness — a Delaware standard of review with a forty-year pedigree — because it found Musk controlled Tesla. On appeal the Supreme Court reversed the remedy only and expressly avoided the fairness and ratification questions. ↳ Consensus: Contrarian — and notably stronger than a16z's own published position, which argued Chancery had introduced "an unprecedented level of subjectivity" and eroded the business judgment rule, not that it fabricated law.

3. A smart contract removes the third party, and therefore removes the discretion that made both cases possible ↳ Evidence (Horowitz): "If it was a smart contract, then there's no third party... a little bit by definition there's no room for a judge to say the contract was invalid." 30:04–30:38 ↳ Caveat: That is the feature and the defect. Contract law's escape valves — mistake, misrepresentation, duress, unconscionability, frustration, illegality, rectification — exist because parties routinely need relief from the literal text. Code has none of them. ↳ Consensus: Contrarian against legal scholarship; mainstream inside crypto.

4. Consensus protocols achieve something states no longer can: universal agreement on ownership across every culture and political faction ↳ Evidence (Horowitz): "Everybody in the world agrees to the penny who owns every bit of... Bitcoin market cap. That's a hell of a magic trick." He calls it "probably the only future that works." 31:33–32:19 ↳ Caveat: His "$3 trillion" figure overstates it — Bitcoin's market cap peaked near $2.40 trillion in October 2025. And consensus covers ledger state, not the underlying entitlement questions courts actually adjudicate. ↳ Consensus: Emerging — the legibility-across-cultures argument is stronger than the usual censorship-resistance framing.

5. The state's interest in tech is power, not safety — and it arrived the moment tech reached state-level power ↳ Evidence (Horowitz): "What the state cares about is power, and as tech kind of rose in power to the level of the state, then they got very very interested... it's always under the guise of safety but it's really power." He traces the causal chain: control the money → control the speech → control the people. 20:47–22:07 ↳ Consensus: Contrarian relative to mainstream policy discourse, aligned with public-choice theory.

6. Special economic zones are the tractable path because Deng proved reform can be geographically scoped ↳ Evidence (Balaji): Shenzhen as the template; a "special Elon zone" or "special founder zone" on uninhabited territory where OSHA/EPA-type rules are suspended and entrants sign a "new social smart contract." Horowitz calls it "super viable" and names Nevada. 10:52–14:03 ↳ Caveat: Shenzhen's admission control was a physical migration barrier — an 84.6km patrolled fence with 16 checkpoints and travel permits — not the ideological vetting Balaji describes. ↳ Consensus: Emerging — SEZ-based reform is well documented; the founder-specific variant is untested at scale.

7. Crypto infrastructure must be pervasive before law can move on chain — and it is not close ↳ Evidence (Horowitz): "Not everybody has a wallet, not everybody has keys... that needs to be the way that money gets distributed to citizens and the way people vote, and then there's a basis to move the law on chain." 33:48–34:55 ↳ Consensus: Aligned, and the most operationally honest claim in the conversation — it concedes the rule-of-code thesis is a decade-scale project, not a present option.

8. AI will subsidise crypto because AI makes everything fake and crypto makes it real again ↳ Evidence (Balaji): "AI is probabilistic. Crypto is deterministic" — the wallet supplies a signature, enabling content IDs and proof-of-human social networks. Horowitz's counterpoint: AI drained crypto's developer base. 36:26–37:26 ↳ Consensus: Emerging — the provenance argument is gaining ground; developer migration is the near-term constraint.

9. Small states can do a reverse merger with capital: political legitimacy for funding ↳ Evidence (Balaji): With 193 UN members, a small state is "like a reverse merger or digital asset treasury company" — one side has a symbol and legitimacy but no capital, the other capital but no legitimacy. 17:07–17:46 ↳ Caveat: The flagship instance has since failed — Network School was shut down by Malaysia in July 2026 and relocated to Kazakhstan, host-state discretion reasserting itself over exactly the arrangement the thesis depends on. ↳ Consensus: Contrarian, and now with a live counterexample attached.
