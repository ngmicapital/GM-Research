Rule Of Code As Exit From A Politicised Bench — And The Two Cases That Undercut The Diagnosis

Source Network State Podcast · Date n/a · 40 min
Domain Law, Governance & Crypto ·
Reliability Expert opinion — two unusually well-placed practitioners, but the two legal cases carrying the argument's weight are characterised inaccurately, and both were substantially reversed on appeal after the likely recording date
Content Type Conference Interview (Balaji Srinivasan × Ben Horowitz)
Full Mode

⚡ The Bottom Line

  • Horowitz's case for "rule of code" is not utopian — it is a claimed response to judicial discretion, and a16z has already acted on it by reincorporating in Nevada. His diagnosis: the US advantage was never the law itself but its predictability, and that "presumed that you didn't have people who were above the law who were judges and prosecutors." 25:53–26:52
  • Both cases he cites were substantially reversed on appeal — which is evidence that appellate review works slowly and expensively, not that it has stopped working. The Delaware Supreme Court reversed the Tesla rescission on 19 December 2025 ($1 nominal damages, fee award cut from $345m to ~$55m); the New York Appellate Division vacated the ~$500m Trump penalty on 21 August 2025 as an excessive fine. Neither reversal appears in the conversation.
  • The Delaware Supreme Court declined to hold what Horowitz asserts. It took the "narrower" of three available paths — rescission was an improper remedy — and expressly did not decide whether the pay plan was entirely fair or whether stockholders had ratified it. Chancellor McCormick's entire-fairness framework was left undisturbed, which is the opposite of "made up her own law."
  • Balaji's "a smart contract is rule of law" collapses rule by rule into rule of law. Smart contracts deliver Fuller's congruence limb perfectly — the declared rule is the executed rule — while abolishing the institutional limbs every canonical account requires: an independent judiciary, accessible courts, natural justice, and equitable escape valves for mistake, duress and unconscionability.
  • Horowitz imposes a sequencing constraint that most rule-of-code advocacy skips: no law on chain until keys are as common as browsers. "We have to get the infrastructure deployed more broadly first... not everybody has a wallet, not everybody has keys." He wants money distribution and voting on that rail before law follows. 33:48–34:55

Executive Summary

  • Horowitz's case for rule of code is defensive, not utopian: it is an exit strategy from judicial discretion, and a16z has already executed on it. His diagnosis is that the US advantage was never the content of the law but its predictability, and that predictability "presumed that you didn't have people who were above the law who were judges and prosecutors." The firm reincorporated from Delaware to Nevada on exactly that reasoning. (25:53–26:52, 25:41)
  • The two cases carrying the argument's weight were both substantially reversed on appeal — and neither reversal is mentioned. The Delaware Supreme Court reversed the Tesla rescission on 19 December 2025; the New York Appellate Division vacated the roughly $500m Trump penalty on 21 August 2025. That pattern is evidence that appellate review is slow and expensive, not that it has ceased to function.
  • Horowitz's characterisation of the Chancery decision does not survive contact with the holding. He says the chancellor "chose to completely ignore the law and make up her own law." The court applied entire fairness — a Delaware standard of review with a forty-year pedigree — after finding Musk controlled Tesla, and the Supreme Court on appeal reversed only the remedy while expressly declining to decide fairness or ratification. 26:52–27:30
  • "A smart contract is rule of law" is the conversation's load-bearing jurisprudential claim, and it conflates rule by rule with rule of law. Smart contracts satisfy Fuller's congruence requirement perfectly — declared rule equals executed rule — while abolishing the institutional limbs that Dicey, Fuller and Raz all treat as constitutive: independent adjudication, accessible courts, natural justice, and relief for mistake, duress and unconscionability. (25:35–25:38, 29:43–30:38)
  • Balaji's structural contribution is the integration thesis and the jurisdictional two-sided market. Network states are to community what Netscape was to the internet — all primitives already exist and nothing works until someone snaps them together — and small states can do a "reverse merger" swapping political legitimacy for capital. (00:53–02:33, 17:07–17:46)
  • Horowitz imposes the sequencing constraint most rule-of-code advocacy skips. Law cannot move on chain until keys are as pervasive as browsers: "not everybody has a wallet, not everybody has keys," and money distribution and voting must run on that rail first. That concession makes the thesis a decade-scale project rather than a present option. 33:48–34:55
  • The thesis has since taken a hard hit from reality. Network School — the flagship instance of the small-state-hosts-network-state model — was shut down by Malaysia in July 2026 and relocated to Kazakhstan. Host-state discretion reasserted itself over precisely the arrangement the argument depends on.

🧩 The Integration Thesis: Network States Are To Community What Netscape Was To The Internet

The conversation opens on structure rather than politics, and Horowitz supplies the frame that organises everything after it.

"You needed something to unify all the technology... it was a little deceptive in that a lot of people deep in the tech kind of saw it as well, what's new here? You're just putting an interface on it."

Before Netscape there was FTP, TCP/IP, Gopher — mature pieces, no product 00:57–01:29. Horowitz's claim is that integration is where the curve bends, and that it necessarily comes last: "that actually has to happen after all the other pieces are mature, because if any one piece of those is wiggly, then the whole thing doesn't work" 02:18–02:33. Balaji applies it directly — Discord, WhatsApp groups, Bitcoin, stablecoins, VR all exist; nobody has assembled them 01:29–02:00. His extension is that digital communities are now inspiring physical ones — Culdesac from micromobility, Próspera from biotech deregulation — and that the richer design space is a community at the intersection of many ideas rather than around one 03:42–05:07. Horowitz's diagnosis of why group chats stay primitive is blunt: "there's no money in them... there's no crypto in these communities. There's no physical dimension. There's no virtual reality dimension. All you can really do is chat" 07:02–07:21.

📜 AnalogBoth speakers reach for Bitcoin as the second instance of the same pattern — ECDSA, hash functions and hashcash all predated it, and the innovation was "a clever integration of existing things" rather than a cryptographic advance 02:47–03:20. The implication for network states is a testable prediction: the binding constraint is packaging, not invention. Horowitz repeats it later about privacy tech, where homomorphic encryption and secure multi-party computation exist but "that packaging has to be done." 38:00–38:26
💡 Key InsightThe friction they identify is social, not technical. Proposing a meetup inside a group chat is "a little more risky... you don't want people to go, nah" 08:35–08:42. Their fix — ambient location awareness surfacing "these three people are nearby" — is a coordination subsidy, and Balaji immediately names the constraint: "the people would have to also want to be location tracked. It has to be done in exactly the right way that they feel good about it" 10:10–10:18. That consent condition is the same one every network-state argument eventually meets at the level of the state.

⚖️ The Argument: Rule Of Code As Exit From A Politicised Bench

The core argument is Horowitz's, and it is narrower and better than the maximalist version usually attributed to this camp. He does not claim the law is bad. He claims it stopped being predictable.

"A huge strength of the US has been rule of law and the amount of case law and the predictability of the law. But that kind of presumed that you didn't have people who were above the law who were judges and prosecutors."

His two exhibits are the Delaware Chancery decision in the Tesla compensation case and the New York civil fraud prosecution of Donald Trump. On the first: the chancellor "just chose to completely ignore the law and make up her own law... the board of directors didn't have a say and then even the shareholders didn't have a say" 26:52–27:30. On the second: "forget about the law — I the prosecutor and I the judge will just go, the law about what it means to have an agreement with a bank to get a loan is no longer the law. It's what I believe" 27:43–28:17.

Balaji's structural gloss is that formerly neutral institutions have become contested territory — "if you imagine like a house and there's two factions and they're fighting over the territory, that house is now a battleground" 28:47–29:10. Horowitz agrees and adds the ratchet: "I think we've gone through a bit of a one-way door on this, because once one side does it, I don't think the other side is on morally higher ground. It's going to be the same kind of thing but in reverse" 29:10–29:31.

🔍 Source Verified❌ The Tesla characterisation is wrong on the law. Chancellor Kathaleen McCormick applied entire fairness — a Delaware standard of review dating to the 1980s, triggered here by her finding that Musk controlled Tesla — rather than inventing a rule. On appeal the Delaware Supreme Court reversed on 19 December 2025, but took what commentators called the "narrower" of three available paths: it held rescission "was an improper remedy," awarded $1 in nominal damages, and expressly avoided deciding whether the 2018 plan was entirely fair or whether stockholders had effectively ratified it. The trial court's legal framework was left standing. Sources: CNBC, 19 December 2025; Gibson Dunn client alert; Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance, February 2026.
🔍 Source Verified⚠️ The Trump characterisation is half-vindicated and half-wrong. On 21 August 2025 the New York Appellate Division, First Department, unanimously threw out the roughly $500m judgment as an excessive fine — two judges writing that "while harm certainly occurred, it was not the cataclysmic harm that can justify a nearly half billion-dollar award to the State." But the five-judge panel split on liability, with two judges holding Trump was properly found liable for business fraud. The penalty was excessive; the underlying fraud findings were not uniformly vacated. Source: People v. Trump, App. Div. 1st Dept, 21 August 2025 (Justia); NPR and CNBC contemporaneous reporting.
💡 Key InsightBoth exhibits were corrected — by appellate courts, within eighteen months, in Horowitz's favour on the remedy in each case. That is the rule of law operating exactly as designed, on a lag and at ruinous cost. The honest version of his complaint is therefore not "Delaware doesn't have rule of law anymore" but "first-instance error is now expensive enough and slow enough to correct that firms will pay a premium to avoid the forum." That is a real and serious claim — and it is the one a16z actually made in public.
🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed, with an important nuance. a16z did reincorporate its principal entity, AH Capital Management, from Delaware to Nevada, publishing a post by legal executives including chief legal officer Jai Ramaswamy urging others to follow. But the firm's stated reasoning was narrower and more precise than Horowitz's spoken version: that Chancery had eroded the business judgment rule and "introduced an unprecedented level of subjectivity into judicial decisions." Subjectivity is a different accusation from fabrication. Source: PitchBook; a16z's own published post.
⚡ TensionAt 11:49 Horowitz says a special founder zone with suspended OSHA and EPA rules is "super viable" — "there's so many states in the US that would want to do that." But at 20:47 his headline conclusion is that "what the state cares about is power," and that regulatory interest in technology is power wearing safety as a disguise. Both can hold only if the states willing to grant such a carve-out are precisely those with the least power to lose by it — which is also why the model keeps landing in Nevada, El Salvador and the Marshall Islands rather than California or Brussels.

🏛️ The Jurisprudential Problem: "Smart Contract Is Rule Of Law" Is A Category Error

Balaji's compression — "a smart contract is rule of law... Delaware is melting down but smart contracts are booting up" 25:35–25:41 — is the line the whole episode is titled after, and it is where precision matters most.

The mechanism he proposes is real: "the property can't be seized since the private keys — you have to break encryption in order to seize the property; to break the contract you have to break the smart contract" 29:55–30:03. Horowitz completes the thought: "then a little bit by definition there's no room for a judge to say the contract was invalid" 30:04–30:11.

That is an accurate description of what smart contracts do. It is not a description of rule of law.

🎯 Against ConsensusEvery canonical account of the rule of law requires institutions the smart contract removes. Dicey's formulation turns on the supremacy of regular law over arbitrary power and equality before it. Fuller's eight desiderata of legality culminate in congruence between declared rule and official action — the one limb code satisfies perfectly — but the other seven presuppose a human system capable of promulgating, interpreting and revising. Raz is the most direct: the rule of law requires an independent judiciary, accessible courts, observance of natural justice, and judicial review of implementing legislation. A contract that executes with no forum, no appeal and no equitable relief maximises determinacy and eliminates adjudication. That is rule by rule. Calling it rule of law borrows the legitimacy of a concept whose institutional content it discards. (25:35, 30:04)

The practical form of the objection is the escape-valve problem. Contract law developed mistake, misrepresentation, duress, undue influence, unconscionability, frustration and illegality — plus rectification and rescission — because parties routinely need relief from the literal text of a bargain they genuinely made. Code implements none of these. The very "no room for a judge" property Horowitz celebrates is the property that makes an exploited contract unfixable.

🌐 Consensus CheckThe strongest evidence against code-is-law comes from inside crypto, not outside it. In June 2016 an attacker drained roughly 3.6 million ETH — about $50m at the time — from The DAO through a reentrancy bug. The code executed exactly as written. The Ethereum community's response in July 2016 was a hard fork that rolled the chain back and returned the funds, splitting the network and creating Ethereum Classic for those who insisted on immutability. Faced with a pure code-execution outcome it found unacceptable, the ecosystem imported a discretionary, political override — which is precisely the institution the rule-of-code thesis says is dispensable. Source: CoinDesk retrospective; Gemini Cryptopedia.
🎯 Against ConsensusThe phrase "code is law" is routinely cited in this camp as intellectual lineage, and it inverts its source. Lawrence Lessig coined it in Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace (1999) as a warning: that architecture is a regulatory modality alongside law, norms and markets, and that commercially-built code would produce a more perfectly and invisibly controlled environment than statute ever could. Lessig's conclusion was that the absence of government regulation online does not mean the absence of regulation — an argument for constitutional attention to code, not for code as an escape from constitutions. Citing it as a liberation thesis reads the book backwards.

Balaji's better argument is the legibility one, and Horowitz states it well: consensus protocols achieve "mathematically strong consensus to the point where everybody in the world agrees to the penny who owns every bit" of the network's value, and "everybody from every culture can read code, can diligence code, can do the math, can determine for themselves it's correct — whether they're Pakistani or Indian or Chinese, Democrat or Republican" 31:33–32:34. That is a claim about verifiability across political difference, and it is the strongest thing in the conversation because it does not depend on the rule-of-law equivalence at all.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Figure overstated. Horowitz hedges — "whatever it is $3 trillion Bitcoin market cap" — but Bitcoin's market capitalisation peaked around $2.40 trillion in October 2025 at the $126,080 all-time high, and had fallen to roughly $2.2 trillion by month-end. The total crypto market cap did reach about $4.27 trillion on 6 October 2025, which is the likelier source of the number. Sources: StatMuse market-cap series; CoinLaw crypto market capitalisation statistics.
🧵 Running ThesisRule of code is a substitute for rule of law where the judiciary has become politically contested. Status: challenged. Supporting: the DExit data, a16z's own reincorporation, and the legibility-across-cultures argument. Against: both cited cases were reversed on appeal; Delaware's legislature enacted SB 21 in March 2025; smart contracts supply no equitable relief; the DAO fork is a standing empirical refutation; and Horowitz's own sequencing constraint pushes the thesis a decade out. The defensible residue is narrower and still substantial — rule of code as a hedge against forum risk, not as a replacement for legal systems.

🏭 Historical Context: What Deng Actually Did In Shenzhen

Balaji's model for scoped reform is Deng Xiaoping's special economic zones, and the mechanism he emphasises is admission control 12:52–13:32: the country was "all brainwashed total communist," Deng "couldn't reform it all at once," so he set up a few zones — "especially Shenzhen near Hong Kong" — and "actually he didn't allow everybody in." From it he derives the "special Elon zone," or more generally a "special founder zone" (detailed in Phase 3 below), which Horowitz rates "super viable" with Nevada as the obvious host 10:52–13:55.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Mechanism real, motive mischaracterised. Shenzhen SEZ was established in August 1980 as one of four zones, and an internal border — the "second line," a 84.6km, 2.8-metre barbed wire barrier with 16 checkpoints, constantly patrolled — physically separated it from the rest of Guangdong from 1982. Chinese nationals needed travel permits to cross into the zone and work permits to access social benefits, and the line was not formally scrapped until 2018. So Balaji is right that entry was controlled and documented. But the permit regime was migration and smuggling control, not the ideological screening he describes; there is no support for an admissions process selecting "the most capitalist sympathetic people" or excluding "diehard Maoists." Sources: Shenzhen SEZ historical record; South China Morning Post on the 2018 abolition of the border.
🔗 ConnectionShenzhen → the consent problem in network states. What actually made the Shenzhen model work was a coercive border maintained by a state with the capacity and will to maintain it. The network-state version substitutes voluntary opt-in — the "new social smart contract" — which is morally superior and materially weaker, because it supplies no answer to the case where the host state changes its mind. 13:42

🌍 Jurisdictional Arbitrage As A Two-Sided Market — And The Case That Just Broke It

Balaji's sharpest structural idea is that jurisdiction shopping is now a two-sided market with 193 possible counterparties 17:07–17:46: a small state has "a symbol and a sense of political legitimacy but not capital," a crypto project has "capital but not legitimacy," and the deal that joins them is structurally a reverse merger or a digital-asset treasury company — executable "at the level of the state, whether a sovereign state or like a US state." He proposes convening the supply side explicitly: "a conference of small countries and pro-growth jurisdictions" opposite founders and venture capitalists 18:46–19:12.

Horowitz's supporting observation is that the willing jurisdictions sort on growth appetite rather than ideology: in the US, "red states or purple states at least" — Wyoming, Nevada, Florida, Texas — and internationally Argentina, Malaysia and Dubai, against places that "are like, well, we're rich enough and now we need to divide up the pie" 15:05–16:38. He reports that a potential UK prime ministerial candidate named growth itself as her differentiator, which he found striking enough to comment on 18:10–18:29.

🔍 Source Verified❌ The Worldcoin figure is off by roughly an order of magnitude in framing. Horowitz says "half the citizens of Buenos Aires are active users on Worldcoin." Argentina was indeed World's strongest market — about 2.2 million verifications nationwide by October 2024, roughly 33% of global World ID verifications, up from 500,000 in April 2024 — but that is a country-wide figure covering Buenos Aires, Mar del Plata, Córdoba and Mendoza, not a Buenos Aires half-share, and a one-time iris verification is not an active user. The Malaysia claim checks out: World ID verification launched there in September 2024. Sources: Biometric Update; La Nación (October 2024); Bernama.
🔍 Source Verified❌ The model's flagship instance has since failed its first host-state test. Network School was shut down by the Malaysian government in July 2026 over alleged administrative violations, following controversy about Israeli citizens attending — Israelis are barred from entering Malaysia — and relocated to Kazakhstan. The two-sided market worked exactly as described until the sovereign side exercised discretion, which is the risk the thesis has no mechanism to price. Source: Fortune, 26 July 2026.
🌐 Consensus CheckThe DAO-law competition Balaji cites is real and correctly sequenced. Wyoming enacted the first DAO statute — SB 38, signed 21 April 2021, effective 1 July 2021 — recognising DAOs as a species of LLC. Tennessee followed in April 2022 as the second state, deliberately dropping "autonomous" and legislating for "Decentralized Organizations." Utah joined in 2023. Wyoming went further in 2024 with the Decentralized Unincorporated Nonprofit Association Act. Against that, Delaware's own response to DExit was legislative rather than passive: SB 21, signed 25 March 2025, limited controlling-stockholder and director liability for conflicted acts and tightened books-and-records demands. Sources: DeFi Education Fund; Norton Rose Fulbright.

The quantitative picture is genuinely mixed. In 2025 Delaware's share of IPO incorporations fell to approximately 62%, with Nevada at roughly 17% and Texas about 4%; of 18 companies that proposed leaving Delaware that year, 13 chose Nevada and two Texas. Tesla, Meta, Dropbox, Tripadvisor, Roblox and AMC are on the list. That is a meaningful shift and not yet a collapse.

⚡ TensionBalaji frames the jurisdictional opportunity as one of "rule of law integrated with rule of code" — "we've got a better DAO law and we're better than Delaware because we allow you to port your company" 26:09–26:18. But that formulation concedes the point the rest of the conversation resists: what makes Nevada attractive is better statutes and courts, not the absence of them. Nevada's own upgrade path runs through AB 239 and AJR 8 — jury-trial waivers and appointed business-court judges — which is a deliberate reconstruction of Chancery, not an escape from it.

🔌 Horowitz's Sequencing Constraint: No Law On Chain Until Keys Are As Common As Browsers

Asked how far the on-chain substitution can run — Balaji lists identity contracts, marriage and birth registries, and Kleros-style key-holding juries 32:51–33:42 — Horowitz declines the invitation and imposes an ordering.

"I think we have to get the infrastructure deployed more broadly first... crypto is not pervasive. Not everybody has a wallet, not everybody has keys."

His first argument is security rather than sovereignty: "having all of your PII in the cloud in 400 different websites is the most dangerous thing... much worse than driving without seat belts and smoking three packs of cigarettes a day" 34:13–34:34. Only once keys are the default for how "money gets distributed to citizens and the way people vote" is there "a basis to move the law on chain" 34:38–34:55.

🔗 ConnectionThe sequencing constraint → the credibility of the whole thesis. Horowitz's ordering makes rule of code contingent on universal self-custody, which is a far higher bar than the ~741 million global crypto owners recorded in 2025 (up from 659 million in 2024, with about 365 million holding Bitcoin). Those figures overwhelmingly count custodial exchange accounts, not self-held keys. By his own criterion the argument describes a decade-scale infrastructure project, not an available alternative to a court. 34:38

He diagnoses the current bottleneck precisely: when "the assault started," the two things crypto needed to fix were performance and usability. "We made a lot of progress on performance, not as much on usability" 38:31–38:45. Balaji's enumerated tailwinds — Solana and Base making transaction-blasting practical, USDC and stablecoins made legal, Apple relaxing App Store restrictions, the regulatory assault pulled back — are supply-side unlocks that still leave the usability gap open 35:52–36:26.

🤖 The AI–Crypto Trade: Talent Left, Determinism Is The Subsidy Back

Horowitz names a cost of the last cycle that rarely gets stated this plainly: "the combination of the assault from the administration and the powers that be, and then the rise of AI, kind of shifted a lot of the software development energy over to AI from crypto, and we need to get a lot of that back to rebuild" 36:33–36:54.

Balaji's response is the sharpest formulation in the episode: "AI is going to subsidise crypto in the sense that AI makes everything fake and crypto makes it real again" 36:54–37:00. The mechanism is signature, not sentiment — "AI is probabilistic, crypto is deterministic, and that wallet allows you to digitally sign something to show it came from you," enabling content IDs on everything and proof-of-human social networks 37:04–37:26.

💡 Key InsightThis reframes crypto's value proposition away from monetary policy and towards provenance infrastructure — and it is the one argument in the conversation that gets stronger, not weaker, as AI improves. It also sidesteps the rule-of-law dispute entirely: a signature proving human origin needs no theory of adjudication to be useful.

The second subsidy Balaji identifies is privacy tooling built for scaling being repurposed for confidentiality — zero-knowledge techniques, homomorphic encryption, secure multi-party computation — "now starting to be something that you might be able to use in traditional web apps with the right packaging of it" 37:49–38:15.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ Terminology drift, and Balaji hedges it himself. "HTTPZ" as a named concept comes from Zama, the FHE company, describing an encrypted-by-default internet analogous to HTTPS — and it is built on fully homomorphic encryption, not zero-knowledge proofs, so Balaji's "it's basically zero knowledge" gloss conflates two distinct primitives. He flags the uncertainty ("whether it's literally HTTPZ"). The adjacent institutional work is real: on 12 September 2025 the Ethereum Foundation rebranded Privacy & Scaling Explorations as the Privacy Stewards of Ethereum and published a roadmap covering private writes, private reads and private proving, with the Kohaku privacy wallet targeted for mid-2026. Sources: Zama; The Block; Ethereum Foundation roadmap coverage.

Phase 3: Specific Item Breakdown

🟣 Tornetta v. Musk — The Case Horowitz Builds On, Reversed On Remedy Not On Doctrine

Horowitz treats the Delaware Chancery's rescission of Musk's 2018 Tesla pay package as proof the court abandoned law for personal preference; the appellate outcome corrected the remedy while leaving the legal framework intact.

What Horowitz Says: The chancellor "just chose to completely ignore the law and make up her own law... the board of directors didn't have a say and then even the shareholders didn't have a say" — followed by Balaji's interjection, "whatever she imagined would be the right thing" 26:52–27:30. His conclusion: "Delaware doesn't have rule of law anymore" 27:43.

Why The Distinction Matters: Chancellor McCormick found Musk "controlled Tesla" and the approval process "deeply flawed," which triggered entire fairness — Delaware's most demanding standard and long-settled doctrine for conflicted controller transactions. The Supreme Court then chose the narrowest of three available paths: rescission was an improper remedy, with fairness and ratification expressly left undecided. A court that invented law would have been reversed on the standard of review. This one was reversed on proportionality of relief — which supports "the remedy was disproportionate," not "there is no rule of law."

🔵 People v. Trump — Penalty Vacated As Excessive, Liability Split

Horowitz's second exhibit for a politicised bench; the Appellate Division unanimously agreed the penalty was excessive but divided on whether the fraud findings stood.

What Horowitz Says: That the prosecutor and judge decided "the law about what it means to have an agreement with a bank to get a loan is no longer the law. It's what I believe" — and that ordinary secured borrowing, where a lender evaluates disclosed collateral, "was not legal in New York" for that case 27:43–28:30. His summary: "the law has been very politicized... we're in uncharted territory a bit. So I do think that creates an opening" 28:32–28:44.

The Honest Reading: His specific complaint about the remedy was vindicated — the 323-page First Department opinion was unanimous that the penalty was an excessive fine. His broader claim, that the substantive legal standard was abolished for one defendant, was not: the panel could not agree to vacate liability, and two judges held the fraud finding proper. The New York statute in play does not require proof of victim reliance or loss, which is a real and unusual feature — but one the legislature wrote, not one the judge invented.

🟢 The Special Elon Zone — A Founder-Scoped SEZ On Uninhabited Land

Balaji's proposal for a Western SEZ analogue where a named builder gets to "move at the speed of physics rather than permits," which Horowitz rates as immediately achievable in Nevada.

What It Includes: A state or country carves out "essentially an uninhabited territory as close to Burning Man as possible," imports existing rules for mundane matters — "regulations on, I don't know, graffiti and stuff. You don't have to rewrite every single law" — allows rapid amendment, and suspends the OSHA/EPA layer for manufacturing and mining. Entrants sign "a new social smart contract" acknowledging they came because of the new rules 10:52–13:55.

The Evidence: Horowitz: "I think it's very doable. There's so many states in the US that would want to do that. I know Nevada would want to do that... they have Burning Man. They would certainly rather have something that was productive" 11:49–12:09. Balaji extends the pattern to a drone zone for Palmer Luckey, zones for manufacturing and mining, and parallel paths abroad — El Salvador, Singapore — generating a template importable back to the US 12:31–14:24.

Unaddressed: Nothing in the discussion prices the risk that the granting jurisdiction revokes the carve-out. That is not hypothetical for this specific pairing — see the Network School card below.

🟠 Shenzhen SEZ — The Template, With The Border Balaji Understates

Deng's geographically-scoped reform is the historical proof that a state can run a parallel legal regime without changing the whole country — but its admission control was a fence, not a philosophy test.

What Balaji Says: When Deng took over, the country was "all brainwashed total communist" and could not be reformed at once, so he set up zones "especially Shenzhen near Hong Kong," with "a membership process or an admissions process where only the most capitalist sympathetic people were allowed in. They weren't going to make trouble. They weren't still diehard Maoists" 12:52–13:32.

The Correction: Entry was genuinely controlled and documented — the 84.6km "second line" with its 16 checkpoints and permit requirement is real, and Balaji's core point survives. But the permit regime was a migration and smuggling control typical of the hukou system, not ideological vetting. No source supports selection for capitalist sympathies or exclusion of Maoists. The gap matters, because the mechanism Balaji admires was coercive state capacity, and the network-state version substitutes voluntary opt-in.

🟡 Wyoming, Tennessee And Utah DAO Statutes — The Rule-Of-Code Legal Wrapper

The concrete jurisdictional competition Balaji points to, and the clearest instance of "rule of law integrated with rule of code" actually shipping.

What It Includes: Wyoming SB 38, signed 21 April 2021 and effective 1 July 2021, made Wyoming the first state to recognise a DAO as a species of LLC — an entity "managed by rules encoded in smart contracts." Tennessee followed in April 2022, deliberately dropping "autonomous" and legislating for "Decentralized Organizations." Utah joined in 2023; Wyoming added the DUNA in 2024. Balaji's sequencing is correct, and his framing of the opportunity is the telling part — a jurisdiction that says "we've got a better DAO law and we're better than Delaware because we allow you to port your company from place to place" (14:27–14:53, 26:09–26:18).

The Irony Worth Naming: Every one of these is a statute, enacted by a legislature, enforceable in courts. The rule-of-code thesis at its strongest is not law's replacement but its most successful recent product — legal personality granted to code-governed entities so that they can transact inside the existing system.

🔴 Network School — The Model's First Host-State Failure

The flagship instance of the small-state-hosts-network-state arrangement, evicted by its host in July 2026.

What It Includes: Balaji's permanent-setup counterpart to the popup — "a popup like a one-month thing," a conference like a one-day thing, and Network School as the permanent version 05:54–06:21. Launched in 2024 in Forest City, Malaysia, as a 90-day pilot for roughly 100 participants, expanded to a year-long programme in early 2025. Shut down by the Malaysian government in July 2026 over alleged administrative violations, and relocated to Kazakhstan.

Why It Matters For The Thesis: Balaji's reverse-merger framing assumes the sovereign counterparty behaves like a listing venue: it supplies legitimacy, takes capital, and the deal holds. The Malaysia outcome shows the sovereign retains an unpriced option to terminate on grounds unrelated to the arrangement's performance. Every substitution of rule of code for rule of law in this conversation runs on physical territory held at a host state's pleasure.
