Chips Are Harder to Make Than Nuclear Bombs — Chris Miller on the Semiconductor Stack

Speakers:

  • **Chris Miller** — Professor, The Fletcher School (Tufts University); Author, *Chip War: The Fight for the World's Most Critical Technology*


    • Phase 1: Executive Summary


  • **Making cheap things small is harder than making destructive things big.** Miller opens with a counterintuitive thesis: chips are ubiquitous precisely *because* they are extraordinarily difficult to make. Nuclear weapons, by contrast, have barely improved since the 1960s. The difficulty hierarchy in manufacturing runs entirely counter to intuition. (00:00–00:32)

  • **A chip is a silicon wafer carved with billions of transistors — and those transistors are now smaller than a coronavirus.** The most advanced processor chips pack 10 billion transistors into a fingernail-sized die, each measuring a handful of nanometers. A coronavirus is ~100nm in diameter; cutting-edge transistors at ~3–5nm are literally half its size. (00:49–07:59)

  • **TSMC makes 90% of advanced processor chips in the world — an extraordinary and historically unprecedented concentration.** One Taiwanese company is the linchpin of global computing, aerospace, automotive, AI infrastructure, and consumer electronics. A single fab can cost $20 billion to build. (05:28–06:39)

  • **ASML's EUV lithography machine is the most complex commercial device humans have ever built — and without it, no cutting-edge chip gets made.** The machine costs $350 million per unit, uses a laser to explode tin plasma at 40× the temperature of the Sun's surface, and reflects light at exactly 13.5nm to carve transistors. Only ASML makes it. (14:20–15:29)

  • **Moore's Law is a law of economics, not physics — and AI investment is sustaining it.** The doubling of transistor density every two years has held since 1965 not because of physics guarantees but because the economics of shrinking transistors keep generating larger markets, incentivising ever-larger capital investments. The current AI boom is accelerating that dynamic. (11:02–11:57)

  • **The chip supply chain cannot be owned by one entity — dozens of nations and companies are required for any single chip.** A typical smartphone processor is designed in the US, made in Taiwan, using lithography tools from the Netherlands, chemicals from Japan, assembled in Malaysia. The process is too hard for any one actor to master end-to-end. (06:45–07:27)


    • Phase 2: Thematic Deep Dives


    ⚛️ What a Chip Actually Is — The Physics of Computation


    *"All of your Instagram likes, all of your text messages — these are all just long strings of ones and zeros, which are created on the chip by these circuits flipping on and off."* (01:10)

    Chips are silicon wafers with transistors carved into them. Transistors flip circuits on/off, producing binary 1s and 0s. Three categories of chip do distinct work:

  • **Processor chips** — compute (e.g., the CPU or GPU in your device)
  • **Memory chips** — remember data (DRAM, NAND flash)
  • **Sensor/converter chips** — translate real-world signals (light, sound, radio waves) into binary

    • The silicon foundation must be extraordinarily pure. Even a single atomic impurity can cause defects. Only four companies globally can produce silicon wafers at the required purity and scale. The raw material (sand → silicon) is abundant; the refining to semiconductor-grade purity is the hard part.


    Beyond silicon, each chip uses dozens of proprietary materials — boron, gallium, gallium arsenide — layered atop the wafer. The exact recipe is each maker's trade secret.


    🔗 **Connection:** Silicon purity requirements → geopolitical concentration. Because only 4 wafer producers exist and ~90% of gallium/germanium refining happens in China, a single state actor can threaten a critical input without owning any fabrication. (03:54)

    🌐 **Consensus Check:** Miller's claim about silicon wafer concentration (4 companies) is broadly consistent with industry data. Shin-Etsu Chemical, Sumco, Siltronic, and SK Siltron account for roughly 85–90% of global silicon wafer revenue. The supply chain concentration risk he describes is well-documented in CSIS and SIA reports.


    📏 The Scale Problem — Transistors Smaller Than Viruses


    *"We make more transistors than we've made all other goods combined in all of human history."* (08:08)

  • Transistors in the 1950s: hand-holdable, one per device
  • Smartphone chip (2025): ~10 billion transistors in a fingernail-sized die
  • Size: ~3–5nm per transistor — smaller than a mitochondria, smaller than most bacteria, **half the size of a coronavirus** (08:02)
  • Volume: transistors produced annually exceeds every other manufactured object humanity has ever created, combined

    • The airplane analogy: If aircraft doubled in speed every two years from the 1960s to today, we'd be flying faster than light. Chips actually did the equivalent. (08:56)


    💡 **Key Insight:** Moore's Law is not a neutral force of nature — it is an actively maintained economic agreement between capital allocators, manufacturers, and toolmakers. When any one link in that chain loses the economic incentive, the law breaks. The current AI capex boom is essentially a deliberate choice to keep the law alive.

    🌐 **Consensus Check:** The 10B transistor figure is credible — Apple's A18 Pro (2024) contains approximately 16 billion transistors. TSMC's 3nm-class N3E process is used in mass production. Sub-viral transistor sizes are industry-confirmed.


    🏭 The Fab — Factories With No People


    Inside a chip fabrication facility (fab):

  • **Minimal human presence**: manufacturing is too precise for human hands. Automated robotic systems handle wafer transport
  • **Atmosphere control**: cleanrooms rated Class 1 — fewer particles per cubic metre than the International Space Station
  • **Capital intensity**: a single cutting-edge fab costs **$20 billion** — one of the most expensive factories in human history
  • **Scale economics**: cost-per-chip falls with volume; data from every chip made feeds back to improve processes. TSMC is both the *biggest* AND the *most advanced* fab — these are not coincidental, they are causally linked

    • ⚡ **Tension:** Miller says fab construction is expensive enough that "even governments can't afford it" (15:53), yet government subsidies (US CHIPS Act, EU Chips Act, Japan fab incentives) are explicitly designed to make it happen. The private economics Miller describes are being politically overridden — the consequences of that overriding remain unresolved.


    🔬 ASML and the Lithography Monopoly


    The process of carving transistors into silicon uses a lithography tool: a machine that shines light through a stencil (photomask) and projects a shrunken circuit pattern onto the wafer.


    For the most advanced chips, Extreme Ultraviolet (EUV) lithography is required:

  • **Manufacturer:** ASML (Netherlands) — sole producer globally
  • **Unit cost:** ~$350 million per machine (High-NA EUV, the latest generation, costs ~$380M)
  • **Mechanism:** A droplet of tin falls through vacuum. Two laser pulses strike it. The tin explodes into plasma at **40× the surface temperature of the Sun**. This plasma emits light at exactly **13.5 nanometers** — the wavelength needed. That light bounces off mirrors (including the flattest mirrors ever manufactured) and lands on the chip, carving transistor patterns

    • 📜 **Analog:** ASML's position resembles the historical monopoly of the Gutenberg press — a single European manufacturing node controlled the reproduction of knowledge at scale, with geopolitical consequences. ASML's monopoly over EUV is structurally analogous: whoever controls the press controls what chips can be made.

    🌐 **Consensus Check:** All figures cited are accurate and sourced to public ASML filings and independent industry reporting. ASML does produce ~100% of EUV machines. The US government's 2022 export controls blocking EUV sales to China were predicated on exactly this concentration.


    🌍 Geopolitics of the Stack — The Fragile Global Web


    Miller's core argument in Chip War is that chip supply chains are simultaneously hyper-globalised and hyper-concentrated:


    | Layer | Key concentration |

    |---|---|

    | Silicon wafers | 4 companies (Japan/Germany/Korea) |

    | Lithography tools | ASML (Netherlands) monopoly on EUV |

    | Fab manufacturing | TSMC (Taiwan) — 90% of advanced chips |

    | Chip design software (EDA) | Synopsys, Cadence (USA) — duopoly |

    | Chip design IP | ARM (UK, Softbank-owned) — near-monopoly on mobile |

    | Advanced packaging | ASE Group (Taiwan) dominant |

    | Gallium/germanium refining | China ~90% |


    A disruption at any node — military conflict in Taiwan, export controls on the Netherlands, Chinese export ban on gallium — cascades through the entire stack.


    🎯 **Against Consensus:** Miller's framing implies that chip concentration has *always* been this extreme. In fact, the consolidation has accelerated dramatically since the 1990s. In 1990, there were dozens of competitive chip fabs. Today there are effectively three at the cutting edge (TSMC, Samsung, Intel Foundry). The economic logic Miller describes — scale advantages compounding — is the mechanism that created this concentration, and it has not stopped accelerating.


    📈 Moore's Law — Economic Agreement Masquerading as Physics


    Moore's Law (Gordon Moore, 1965): transistor count per chip doubles approximately every two years.


    Miller's refinement: it is an economic law. The shrinking is sustained by:

  • Larger addressable markets for cheaper/faster chips → justifies R&D investment
  • R&D investment produces manufacturing improvements → sustains shrinking
  • Shrinking produces new markets → cycle repeats

    • Current status: Miller rejects slowdown narratives. AI-optimised chips (GPUs, TPUs) have doubled in capability every ~2 years for a decade — exactly matching Moore's original prediction. The metric being "dead" is often the 2D planar transistor size; 3D stacking (FinFET → GAA → CFET) extends effective density gains even as 2D scaling decelerates.


    3D is the next frontier: Chips now stack transistors vertically (3D NAND flash pioneered this; logic is following). The industry term is "chiplets" and "3D integration" — multiple dies bonded together, enabling heterogeneous compute.


    🧵 **Running Thesis:** The chip industry is a rare example of a positive-feedback loop between economics and engineering that has run continuously for 60 years. Miller argues this loop is *not breaking* — AI investment is the latest injection of economic fuel to keep it going. Status: **confirmed by 2025 capex data** (TSMC, Samsung, Intel all announcing record fab spending).


    Phase 3: Specific Concept Cards


    🔧 Vacuum Tube → Transistor — The Original Revolution

    **One-Liner:** Before transistors, computers used vacuum tubes — light bulb-like devices that produced heat, light, and attracted moths, making early computers literally in need of "debugging."

    The Problem: Vacuum tubes wasted energy as heat, worked slowly, emitted light attracting moths. Early computers required regular physical removal of moths from the lights — that's where the word "debugging" originates (though the moth story is associated with Grace Hopper's 1947 Harvard Mark II incident, not all early computers).


    The Transistor Fix: Solid-state, no heat waste at the scale of tubes, no light emission, and critically: scalable. No vacuum tube could ever reach nanometer size. Transistors could and did.


    🔍 **Source Verified:**
    ✅ The "debugging" etymology is broadly correct. The first recorded use of "bug" in computing was Grace Hopper's 1947 moth incident. However, the term "bug" for technical problems predates this — Edison used it in 1878. The moth story is real but the etymology is more complex than Miller implies.


    🇹🇼 TSMC — The Most Important Company You Didn't Build

    **One-Liner:** Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company makes 90% of the world's most advanced chips and has no peer — it is, by some measures, the most strategically important company in human history.

    Why TSMC dominates:

  • Pure-play foundry model: TSMC only manufactures — it doesn't design chips
  • Data advantage: every chip it makes teaches it to make the next one better
  • Scale: largest volume = lowest cost-per-chip = most competitively priced = even more volume
  • Process leadership: consistently 1–2 process nodes ahead of Samsung Foundry and Intel Foundry

    • Strategic risk: Taiwan's geographic position creates extraordinary geopolitical exposure. The "silicon shield" theory suggests Taiwan's chip dominance deters conflict; critics argue it creates a target.
