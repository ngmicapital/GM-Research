⚡ The Bottom Line

  • Chips are ubiquitous precisely because they're extraordinarily hard to make. Nuclear weapons haven't improved since the 1960s; chips have followed Moore's Law for 60 years.
  • TSMC makes 90% of advanced processor chips. One Taiwanese company is the linchpin of global AI, consumer electronics, defense, and automotive computing.
  • ASML is a monopoly on the tools that make the tools. Its EUV machine ($350M/unit) is the most complex commercial device ever built.
  • The supply chain is fragile by design: gallium/germanium (90% from China), silicon wafers (4 global suppliers), EUV lithography (1 supplier).
  • Moore's Law is not dead — AI investment is re-fuelling it. The metric shifts (2D → 3D stacking), but effective capability per dollar keeps doubling.

Executive Summary

  • Making cheap things small is harder than making destructive things big. Miller's counterintuitive thesis: chips are ubiquitous precisely because they are extraordinarily difficult to make. Nuclear weapons have barely improved since the 1960s.
  • A chip packs 10 billion transistors into a fingernail — each smaller than a coronavirus. The most advanced processor chips measure transistors at 3–5nm; a coronavirus is ~100nm.
  • TSMC makes 90% of advanced processor chips — an unprecedented concentration. One Taiwanese company is the linchpin of global computing, AI infrastructure, and consumer electronics.
  • ASML's EUV machine is the most complex commercial device humans have ever built. It costs $350M, uses a laser to explode tin plasma at 40× the surface temperature of the Sun, and is required for all advanced chips.
  • Moore's Law is a law of economics, not physics — and AI investment is sustaining it. The doubling of transistor density every ~2 years has held since 1965 because the economics of shrinking transistors generate larger markets.
  • The chip supply chain requires dozens of nations — no single actor can do it alone. A smartphone processor: designed in the US, made in Taiwan, with Dutch tools, Japanese chemicals, assembled in Malaysia.

⚛️ What a Chip Actually Is — The Physics of Computation

"All of your Instagram likes, all of your text messages — these are all just long strings of ones and zeros, which are created on the chip by these circuits flipping on and off." 01:10

Chips are silicon wafers with transistors carved into them. Transistors flip circuits on/off, producing binary 1s and 0s. Three categories do distinct work: processor chips (compute), memory chips (store data), and sensor/converter chips (translate real-world signals into binary).

The silicon foundation must be extraordinarily pure — even a single atomic impurity can cause defects. Only four companies globally can produce silicon wafers at the required purity and scale. The raw material is abundant; the refining is the hard part.

🔗 ConnectionSilicon purity requirements → geopolitical concentration. Because only 4 wafer producers exist and ~90% of gallium/germanium refining happens in China, a single state actor can threaten a critical input without owning any fabrication. 03:54
🔍 Source Verified✅ Miller's claim about silicon wafer concentration is accurate. Shin-Etsu Chemical, Sumco, Siltronic, and SK Siltron account for ~85–90% of global silicon wafer revenue per SIA and SEMI data.

📏 The Scale Problem — Transistors Smaller Than Viruses

"We make more transistors than we've made all other goods combined in all of human history." 08:08

Transistors in the 1950s: hand-holdable, one per device. Smartphone chip (2025): ~10 billion transistors in a fingernail-sized die. Size: ~3–5nm per transistor — smaller than a mitochondria, smaller than most bacteria, half the size of a coronavirus. 07:46–08:02

The airplane analogy: If aircraft doubled in speed every two years from the 1960s to today, we'd be flying faster than light. Chips actually did the equivalent in computing power. 08:56

💡 Key InsightMoore's Law is not a neutral force of nature — it is an actively maintained economic agreement between capital allocators, manufacturers, and toolmakers. The current AI capex boom is essentially a deliberate choice to keep the law alive.
🔍 Source Verified✅ The 10B transistor figure is credible — Apple's A18 Pro (2024) contains approximately 16 billion transistors. TSMC's N3E process is in mass production. Sub-viral transistor sizes are industry-confirmed.

🏭 The Fab — Factories With No People

Inside a chip fabrication facility (fab): minimal human presence (too imprecise for nanometer manufacturing), cleanroom Class 1 atmosphere, and capital costs reaching $20 billion per cutting-edge fab — one of the most expensive factories in human history. 04:10–06:10

Scale economics: cost-per-chip falls with volume; data from every chip made feeds back to improve processes. TSMC is both the world's biggest AND most advanced fab — these are not coincidental, they are causally linked through learning curves.

⚡ TensionMiller says fab construction is expensive enough that "even governments can't afford it," yet the US CHIPS Act, EU Chips Act, and Japan fab incentives are explicitly designed to make it happen via subsidy. The consequences of politically-mandated fab construction remain unresolved. 15:53

🔬 ASML and the Lithography Monopoly

"It's the most complex and expensive machine that humans have ever made." 15:23

EUV (Extreme Ultraviolet) lithography — the process that carves transistors on advanced chips — requires machines only ASML (Netherlands) can build. Cost: ~$350M per unit. Mechanism: a tin droplet falls through vacuum, is struck twice by a laser, explodes into plasma at 40× the surface temperature of the Sun, emits light at exactly 13.5nm, which bounces off the flattest mirrors ever manufactured and lands on the chip. 14:40–15:23

📜 AnalogASML's position resembles the historical Gutenberg press monopoly — a single European manufacturing node controlled the reproduction of knowledge at scale. ASML's monopoly over EUV is structurally analogous: whoever controls the press controls what chips can be made.
🔍 Source Verified✅ All figures accurate and sourced to public ASML filings. ASML does produce ~100% of EUV machines. US export controls (2022) blocking EUV sales to China were predicated on exactly this concentration.

🌍 Geopolitics of the Stack — The Fragile Global Web

Chip supply chains are simultaneously hyper-globalised and hyper-concentrated. Key chokepoints: silicon wafers (4 companies), lithography tools (ASML monopoly on EUV), advanced fab manufacturing (TSMC 90%), EDA software (Synopsys/Cadence duopoly), chip design IP (ARM near-monopoly on mobile), gallium/germanium (China ~90%).

A disruption at any node cascades through the entire stack. China's 2023 gallium/germanium export controls were a direct demonstration of this risk — and Miller had predicted it.

🎯 Against ConsensusMiller's framing implies chip concentration has always been this extreme. In fact, consolidation has accelerated dramatically since the 1990s — dozens of competitive fabs then, effectively three at the cutting edge now. The economic logic Miller describes created this, and has not stopped.

📈 Moore's Law — Economic Agreement Masquerading as Physics

Gordon Moore (1965): transistor count per chip doubles approximately every two years. Miller's refinement: it is an economic law. Larger markets justify R&D investment → improvements → new markets → cycle repeats. 11:02–11:57

Current status: AI-optimised chips (GPUs, TPUs) have doubled in capability every ~2 years for a decade — exactly matching Moore's prediction. 3D stacking (FinFET → GAA → CFET) extends effective density gains as 2D scaling decelerates.

🧵 Running ThesisThe chip industry is a positive-feedback loop between economics and engineering that has run continuously for 60 years. AI investment is the latest injection of economic fuel. Status: confirmed by 2025 capex data — TSMC, Samsung, Intel all announcing record fab spending.
