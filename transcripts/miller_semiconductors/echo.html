Echo: Chips Are Harder to Make Than Nuclear Bombs


Source: Chris Miller · Big Think Clips · ~16 min

Domain: Technology & Geopolitics · Content Type: Expert explainer / book promotion

Reliability: Data-backed — Miller is the leading academic authority on semiconductor supply chains; all figures cross-check against public industry data

Mode: Full



⚡ The Bottom Line


  • **Chips are ubiquitous precisely because they're extraordinarily hard to make.** Nuclear weapons haven't improved since the 1960s; chips have followed Moore's Law for 60 years. Counterintuitive but verified.
  • **TSMC makes 90% of advanced processor chips.** One Taiwanese company is the linchpin of global AI, consumer electronics, defense, and automotive computing.
  • **ASML is a monopoly on the tools that make the tools.** Its EUV machine ($350M/unit) is the most complex commercial device ever built and cannot be replicated by any other firm currently.
  • **The supply chain is fragile by design:** gallium/germanium (90% from China), silicon wafers (4 global suppliers), EUV lithography (1 supplier). Any node is a chokepoint.
  • **Moore's Law is not dead — AI investment is re-fuelling it.** The metric shifts (2D → 3D stacking), but effective capability per dollar has kept doubling.


    • 🔑 Key Claims (Ranked)


    1. Making tiny cheap things is harder than making large destructive things

    ↳ Evidence: Nuclear weapon tech stagnated post-1960s; chip capability doubles every ~2 years, still ongoing

    ↳ Caveat: Nuclear complexity is strategic/political as much as technical

    ↳ Consensus: Aligned with technology historians


    2. TSMC manufactures ~90% of advanced processor chips

    ↳ Evidence: Market data; TSMC 2024 revenue and foundry share confirm dominance

    ↳ Caveat: Samsung and Intel Foundry serve some advanced nodes

    ↳ Consensus: Aligned


    3. Transistors on cutting-edge chips are smaller than a coronavirus (~100nm vs ~3–5nm)

    ↳ Evidence: TSMC N3E process at ~3nm in mass production (2024)

    ↳ Caveat: "nm" node names are marketing approximations; physical gate length differs

    ↳ Consensus: Broadly correct


    4. A cutting-edge fab costs ~$20 billion

    ↳ Evidence: TSMC's Arizona fab complex reported at $65B total; individual fab buildings ~$20B

    ↳ Caveat: Cost varies by technology node and geography

    ↳ Consensus: Aligned


    5. ASML EUV machines cost ~$350M each and are required for all advanced chips

    ↳ Evidence: ASML public pricing; High-NA EUV ~$380M per unit

    ↳ Caveat: Some trailing-edge chips use older DUV lithography

    ↳ Consensus: Confirmed


    6. China controls ~90% of gallium and germanium refining

    ↳ Evidence: USGS and EU Critical Raw Materials data (2023)

    ↳ Caveat: China used export controls on gallium/germanium in 2023, validating the risk

    ↳ Consensus: Aligned


    7. Moore's Law has held for ~60 years and is currently on track for AI chips

    ↳ Evidence: Nvidia GPU performance per dollar has tracked or exceeded Moore's Law since 2010

    ↳ Caveat: 2D scaling is decelerating; 3D/chiplet integration is the mechanism

    ↳ Consensus: Debated but Miller's framing holds if metric is capability, not gate length
