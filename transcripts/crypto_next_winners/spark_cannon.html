Crypto’s Next Winners Won’t Be Blockchains

Source Bankless · 2026-09-07 · 67 min
Domain Crypto Markets — Token Valuation & Value Accrual
Duration 67 min
Date 2026-09-07
Speakers
  • Austin Barack — Founder and Managing Partner, Relayer Capital (liquid and venture, founded roughly two and a half years before this recording); previously a Partner at CoinFund; began his career in corporate development and FP&A at a payments company.
  • David Hoffman — Host, Bankless.
Full Mode

Executive Summary

  • The title claim is a revenue claim, and Barack states it as a chart rather than a philosophy. For most of crypto's history, execution-layer infrastructure generated 95%+ of total protocol revenue. Today applications generate roughly two thirds and execution layers roughly a third — and Barack expects applications to reach 90%+. His conclusion: "the most enduring tokens [will] be applications and money." 63:21–63:57
  • That is the fat-protocol thesis running in reverse, and the reversal is measurable. Joel Monegro's 2016 "Fat Protocols" argued that crypto inverted the internet's thin-protocol/fat-application pattern so that value would concentrate at the shared protocol layer. Ethereum's own fee data since the Dencun upgrade is the cleanest evidence against it: L1 revenue fell roughly 75% year on year, from about $157m in August 2023 to about $39m in August 2025, while the applications built on top kept growing.
  • Barack's method is ordinary equity analysis applied to tokens, and the discipline is the interesting part. He builds a revenue line, subtracts cost of goods and opex, and only then asks what is genuinely burnable — which produces the sharpest single observation in the episode: a protocol burning 8% of revenue at a 10% EBITDA margin is burning most of its free cash flow, not a token gesture. 11:31–11:47
  • Venice is his largest conviction and also his most assumption-heavy model. $107m annualised revenue and $8.3m annualised burns as of August, projected to $336m and $70m by 2027, a 50x multiple on burns, a $3.5bn implied token value and a $43.89 price target against roughly $16 spot. Forty per cent of those 2027 burns come from Mines, a product that was not live at recording. He grades his own model a six out of ten on the optimism scale. (12:36–13:45, 15:53–16:09, 32:34–32:47)
  • The buyback is functioning as a trust instrument, not as a capital-allocation decision — and both men know it. Hoffman's challenge is that a two-year-old AI startup doing stock buybacks is backwards; Barack's answer is that until the CLARITY Act exists you have to "walk the walk," and Venice raised $65m specifically so it could burn and reinvest at the same time. (26:04–26:24, 27:14–27:19, 28:08–28:42)
  • Pump's valuation gap is a governance discount, not a revenue discount. It trades at roughly 5x buybacks against 30–40x for Hyperliquid and Lighter — despite leading Hyperliquid on trailing-30-day revenue — because its 50% buyback commitment is a twelve-month promise subject to renewal, not a permanent claim. Barack puts the resulting range at 6–10x versus 10–14x depending on how you handicap that risk. (44:12–44:30, 61:44–62:35)
  • The endgame Barack describes is narrow: applications, money, and usage. Chains survive as rails rather than as the value-capture layer. He names Bitcoin and Zcash as money, Solana as the most-utilised chain but revenue-light post-MEV, and — for the first time in a long while — says he is curious about ETH as money rather than as a revenue asset. 63:52–65:46

Editorial note: the sponsor reads — two blocks, at roughly 14:14–15:34 and 56:33–58:24 — are excluded from this document. Everything below is editorial content.

Market context: this conversation was recorded in the week Bitcoin moved from roughly $62,000 to nearly $80,000 after a long drawdown. Both men treat that move as the possible end of a bear market rather than a settled fact, and several valuation claims are explicitly priced off that moment.

📊 The Thesis Is A Revenue Chart: Value Has Already Moved Up The Stack

Hoffman sets up the closing question by stating the frame as settled fact — "we're firmly in a new era of crypto like the hyperinfra age is firmly behind us," with the excitement around new layer ones and layer twos "mostly a thing of the past" 63:01–63:12. Barack's answer is the episode's title, and he reaches for a number rather than a narrative.

"applications are generating about 2/3 of revenue and about a third is generated by execution layers" 63:36 — Barack, citing a Blockworks chart; for most of crypto's history the split was 95%+ to execution-layer infrastructure.
"I think we're going to find the most enduring tokens be applications and money." 63:52 — the whole thesis in one line.

This is worth slowing down on, because the claim being made is narrower and stronger than "blockchains don't matter." It is a claim about where the fee is charged. A blockchain earns when users pay for blockspace. An application earns when users pay for the thing they actually wanted. As blockspace becomes abundant and cheap — which is the explicit goal of every scaling roadmap in the industry — the revenue that used to be collected as congestion pricing migrates to whoever owns the user relationship.

🔍 Source Verified✅ The thesis being inverted here has a canonical source. Joel Monegro published "Fat Protocols" at Union Square Ventures on 8 August 2016. Its argument: the internet stack was thin protocols (TCP/IP, HTTP, SMTP) and fat applications, because the protocols produced immense value that was captured and re-aggregated at the application layer as data. Crypto, Monegro argued, reverses this — value concentrates at the shared protocol layer, with only a fraction distributed to applications, because applications must use the native protocol token to function. Notably, Monegro himself published a follow-up in 2020 titled "Thin Applications," revising the picture. Source: usv.com/writing/2016/08/fat-protocols/.
🌐 Consensus CheckEthereum's own fee data since March 2024 is the hardest evidence for Barack's inversion, and neither speaker cites it. The Dencun upgrade activated EIP-4844 on 13 March 2024, moving rollup data from calldata to "blobs." Before Dencun, L2 data posting was the single largest fee category on Ethereum L1, running roughly $34m a month; after it, blob fees totalled only single-digit millions in the first year. Ethereum's monthly protocol revenue fell around 75% year on year — about $157.4m in August 2023 against about $39.2m in August 2025 — and daily ETH burn collapsed to roughly 100 ETH, flipping the network from deflationary to mildly inflationary at around 0.23% annualised. The Fusaka upgrade in December 2025 introduced EIP-7918, replacing the 1-wei blob floor with a dynamic minimum pegged to 1/16 of the L1 execution base fee. The chain did not stop working; it stopped collecting. 63:36
💡 Key InsightThe fat-protocol thesis was not wrong about mechanism — it was wrong about durability. Monegro's causal claim was that applications must buy the native token to transact, so protocol demand rises with application success. That held while blockspace was scarce. The moment scaling made blockspace abundant, the token demand it predicted became elastic and collapsed, while the application-layer businesses on top kept charging their users the same amount. A useful reframing: fat protocols was a theory about a shortage, and the industry spent eight years successfully engineering the shortage away. 63:21
🌐 Consensus CheckThe current numbers support the direction Barack describes, at the top of the table at least. Hyperliquid generated $419.3m in gross fees in H1 2026, up 31% from $320m in H1 2025. Pump.fun and Hyperliquid together accounted for close to 90% of the roughly $638m of crypto token buybacks recorded across 2026 — and on DefiLlama's trailing-30-day revenue tracker around this period, Pump.fun sat at about $51.3m against Hyperliquid's about $47.4m. Both are applications. Neither is a chain. Sources: 21Shares, "Hyperliquid's H1 2026 earnings"; DefiLlama; buyback tallies reported August 2026. 63:36
🎯 Against ConsensusThe honest counter-case is that this measures fee capture, not value creation, and those are different things. A chain that runs at near-zero margin while securing hundreds of billions in application value is not economically worthless — it is a utility that has priced itself like one, which is exactly what its designers intended. The investable conclusion ("buy apps, not chains") can be right at the same time as the architectural conclusion ("chains don't matter") is wrong. Barack himself half-concedes this when he reaches for Solana as "one of the most compelling bets for crypto adoption" precisely because it is so heavily used while generating little revenue. 65:14

🧮 The Lens: Growth And Value In The Same Asset, Which Ordinarily Does Not Exist

Barack opens with a framing that shapes everything after it. Nobody comes to crypto to buy a business growing 10% a year at a 4x multiple — they could buy a power utility. What he looks for is the intersection.

"being at the intersection of growth and value" 01:02 — Barack, arguing that crypto's capital cycle is what makes the combination possible at all.

The mechanism he describes is straightforward: because crypto capital flows are violently cyclical, assets are periodically both growing fast and priced as if they were not, and those windows persist long enough to act on 01:41–01:58. In ordinary equity markets that overlap is rare and brief. In crypto it recurs.

He is explicit that this applies to the liquid book, not to venture, where he holds the opposite view — "it's very rare that the best deals are also priced cheaply," so the only lever is getting in early enough that the absolute valuation is low even if the relative one is not 03:23–03:47. His venture focus is preseed and seed, and the bet is on team and market rather than price.

💡 Key InsightThe allocation shift is the tell. Through the first three quarters of 2024, Relayer ran roughly 50/50 liquid and venture; at the time of recording it is 95% liquid 04:31–05:10. Barack's reason is not cycle timing — he says explicitly that it has been the focus for about a year. It is that the venture deals reaching market are growth-stage payments and fintech companies, which he finds more interesting as public equities than as private crypto bets. That is a quiet but significant statement: the private crypto market has begun producing the same thing the public market already offers, at worse terms. 05:08

His account of the bear market's function is the best short explanation in the episode of why a fundamentals-driven approach became possible at all: a long drawdown does the filtering for you. Instead of a hundred tokens you are looking at five or ten that are finding product-market fit, growing quickly, and — at least until recently — priced attractively 05:58–06:30.

🔗 ConnectionBear-market filtering → the "third inning" framing. Barack sketches a sequencing model: the bottom is the first inning, the first recovery of assets is the second, and yield-native protocols like Ethena and Pendle are third-inning assets that benefit once onchain yields rise 06:30–06:58. It is a simple relay ordering — beta first, then fundamentals, then yield-sensitive structures — and it is how he explains why an asset can rip 40% in thirty hours without the thesis having changed.

🎨 Venice: What It Looks Like To Model A Token As An Income Statement

Venice is the episode's centrepiece, and the process Barack describes is more interesting than the price target. He says he built the model the traditional way, from scratch, starting where an FP&A analyst starts: what is the business?

The business is private, uncensored AI — access to frontier and open-source models without logging — monetised through subscriptions (tiers he cites at $18, $68 and $200 a month) and, separately, through credit purchases when subscribers exhaust their allowance 08:20–09:05. The token, VVV, is fed by two programmatic burns: a burn on each new signup scaled to tier, and a burn of roughly 5% of each credit purchase (10:42–10:55, 29:51–30:12). Separately, VVV can be locked to mint DIEM, a tokenised-compute instrument giving roughly a dollar a day of inference 26:22–26:31.

The analytically valuable moment is where he refuses to take a burn rate at face value.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Barack's point at 11:31–11:47 is the one every token-buyback investor should internalise. "People say they're burning 8% of revenue" — but if gross margins are 50% and EBITDA margins are 10%, an 8% of revenue burn is consuming the large majority of free cash flow, not a rounding error. Burn-as-percentage-of-revenue and burn-as-percentage-of-earnings are different ratios, and only the second tells you whether the policy is sustainable. Applied to Venice — an AI business with real inference costs, unlike a matching engine — it is the difference between a generous policy and an unsustainable one.

The build-up, in his numbers: as of August, roughly $107m of annualised revenue and $8.3m of annualised burns; projected to $336m of revenue and $70m of burns in 2027; a 50x multiple applied to burns (his stated equivalent of a P/E, using buybacks-to-market-cap as the analogue) giving $3.5bn for the token; divided by projected end-2027 supply, a $43.89 price against roughly $16 spot, and about $12 when he last updated 12:36–13:52.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Venice's corporate facts check out and are slightly better than the podcast implies. Venice.ai — led by CEO Erik Voorhees, the ShapeShift founder, with Jesse Proudman as president and CTO — announced a $65m Series A at a $1bn valuation on 1 July 2026, led by Dragonfly with participation from Coinbase Ventures and North Island Ventures. The company was already profitable, with annualised run-rate revenue reported above $70m at announcement. Barack's August figure of roughly $107m is therefore a plausible two-month extrapolation rather than an aggressive one. Sources: TechCrunch, 1 July 2026; GeekWire; SiliconANGLE.
💡 Key InsightThe equity/token structure Barack calls "one of the most elegant balances" is doing specific work, and it is worth stating plainly for anyone who has watched crypto's token-versus-equity fights. Venice is fundamentally an off-chain business — consumers signing up with credit cards, compute contracts, model relationships — so it needs an equity entity. The token is not a claim on that entity; it is a claim on a policy the entity has chosen to run, with burns funded from free cash flow, and the investors' alignment comes from token warrants rather than from the token's legal position. That is a governance structure held together by reputation and disclosure, not by law. (09:16, 28:36)

Hoffman's pushback on Mines is the best piece of interviewing in the episode, and it lands. Barack has modelled $29m of the projected $70m in 2027 burns — about 40% — from Mines, which was not live at recording 15:53–16:09. His defence is precedent: the credit-purchase line did not exist at the start of 2026 and was already running at roughly $60m ARR by August, so a team with that execution record deserves the benefit of the doubt on a new line 16:18–17:22.

Hoffman's counter is a distinction, not a disagreement: credit purchases were never a new product. Venice has always sold tokens; credits are simply another way to sell the same tokens to the same users, amplifying a product that already exists. Mines is a genuinely new line item, and neither Barack nor — he suspects — Venice knows how it will do 17:29–18:16.

⚡ TensionBarack concedes the point and then partly recovers it, and the recovery is the more interesting claim. He accepts a "two out of ten" newness score for credits and offers "five out of ten" for Mines 18:27–18:41 — but then argues he may be making the wrong mistake: overestimating direct Mines revenue while underestimating how much Mines accelerates subscription and credit growth by making the existing product more useable 20:11–20:22. That is a real hedge rather than a rhetorical one, because the two errors point in opposite directions on the same line of the model.
💡 Key InsightThe bull case for Mines rests on a structural asset Hoffman names precisely: Venice owns the end-user relationship, and a model aggregator does not. Venice's power users have learned which models excel at which tasks; Mines would let them package that expertise as a structured product and monetise it — an App Store dynamic, with distribution inside a product the user is already in 20:22–21:56. Hoffman's contrast is with OpenRouter, which routes at the developer-tooling layer and has no consumer surface to distribute through. Barack adds the numbers: roughly 4 million historical users, by his estimate over a million active, who would be incentivised to market to each other on Twitter, Reddit, Telegram and Discord. 22:30
🌐 Consensus CheckThe OpenRouter comparison both men reach for became concrete three weeks before this episode published, and the real figures are cleaner than the ones quoted on air. Stripe agreed to acquire OpenRouter for more than $7bn, reported by Bloomberg on 16 August 2026 and confirmed by Stripe's own newsroom. OpenRouter had raised a $113m Series B at a reported $1.3bn valuation in May 2026 — so the acquisition was roughly six times its valuation three months earlier, not the "7x at 10 billion" mentioned in passing. It connects developers to more than 400 models through one account. Barack's inference from it is the durable part: the market has decided multi-model routing is a real business, and that is validation for a consumer product built on the same premise. Sources: Bloomberg, 16 August 2026; TechCrunch; stripe.com/newsroom. 35:45

🔁 The Buyback As A Trust Instrument, Not A Capital-Allocation Decision

Hoffman asks the question that any equity analyst would ask first: why is a two-year-old AI startup, in the fastest-growing sector in the world, returning capital instead of reinvesting all of it? "This just goes against like common sense about startups" 25:27–26:04.

Barack's answer does not pretend the objection is wrong. It relocates it.

"you need to, you know, walk the walk if you're going to say that pre-clarity act." 27:14 — Barack, on why a young company burns cash flow it could reinvest.

The logic is that in the absence of a legal framework that guarantees a token accrues the value of what is built, the only available guarantee is a demonstrated, escalating, programmatic commitment. The burn history he traces is exactly that ratchet: discretionary burns of a couple of hundred thousand a month, then a burn on new subscribers only, then 5% of credit-purchase revenue, with resubscription burns modelled to begin late this year or early Q1 and the credit burn modelled to rise from 5% to 10% by 2027 (26:48–27:56, 30:54–32:11).

💡 Key InsightThis is a costly signal in the technical sense, and the raise is what makes it legible. Barack notes that Venice has burned something in the low single-digit millions to date while raising $65m — ten to twenty times the burn — and that Erik Voorhees framed the raise as specifically enabling the company to prioritise the token and reinvest at the same time 28:08–28:42. Strip the crypto vocabulary and the structure is familiar: a company raising primary capital in order to sustain a distribution policy it could not otherwise afford. What makes it defensible rather than absurd is that the distribution policy is the product's credibility, not a return of surplus. 28:08
🌐 Consensus CheckThe CLARITY Act that both speakers treat as the coming fix did not arrive, and the timing matters for anyone reading this episode now. The Digital Asset Market Clarity Act passed the House in July 2025 and a revised version cleared the Senate Banking Committee in May 2026, with merged Banking and Agriculture text released in July 2026. On 15 September 2026 — eight days after this episode published — a Senate cloture vote failed 49–50, short of the 60 needed, with all Democrats and four Republicans voting no; a motion to reconsider was filed, leaving the bill technically alive but with 2026 passage in serious doubt. Every "pre-CLARITY" workaround discussed in this conversation — programmatic burns as a substitute for legal entitlement, token warrants as a substitute for equity claims — is therefore not a temporary bridge but the standing state of affairs. Sources: NPR, 15 September 2026; Paul Hastings Crypto Policy Tracker; Latham & Watkins US Crypto Policy Tracker. 26:36
🔗 ConnectionBuyback credibility → the multiple. Barack's comparison across the three assets makes the point mechanically. Hyperliquid burns roughly 99% of eligible fees — which he calls an anomaly of anomalies and doubts is sustainable long term unless customer acquisition is funded elsewhere 29:09–29:37. Venice burns a deliberately survivable fraction and escalates it. Pump committed 50% for a guaranteed twelve months. The market pays a materially different multiple for each, and the spread tracks credibility of commitment more closely than it tracks quality of revenue. 61:52

🎰 Pump: Pricing A Durable Casino, And What The Discount Is Actually For

Pump trades, in Barack's figures, at roughly 5x buybacks, against 30–40x for Hyperliquid and Lighter 44:12–44:30. He thinks 10x is more reasonable — a 2x from spot assuming no further growth — and expects growth too.

"I think it's not unreasonable to call it a durable casino business." 44:40 — Barack, immediately adding that there is nothing wrong with gambling.

His comparison set is deliberately unembarrassed: Wynn, Las Vegas Sands, MGM, DraftKings, FanDuel, prediction markets, zero-day-to-expiry options on Robinhood 44:55–45:12. The point is that speculation-as-product is a large, old, well-understood business category with public comparables trading at real multiples, and memecoin trading is a form of it.

He identifies three separate reasons the market has discounted it, and they are worth separating because only one of them is a real risk.

The first is revenue durability, which he thinks the evidence has now answered. The bear comparison was always OpenSea — billions of revenue, then 95% less twelve months later — and over two-plus years Pump's revenue has proved durable and growing 58:54–59:36. He reaches for Hyperliquid's own branding to make the point: if Hyperliquid is the house of all finance, "this is the house of all memecoins" 59:40.

The second is a sampling problem, and Barack is unusually candid about it.

"once you understand that you're not the target user, it's easier to understand why this business is so durable" 60:34 — Barack, noting there is surprisingly little overlap between crypto Twitter, liquid funds and actual memecoin traders — himself included.
💡 Key InsightThe negative-EV argument he makes at 61:00–61:31 is the analytically correct one and is regularly got wrong in crypto. The objection "these are negative expected-value products so usage must decay" does not follow, because it confuses aggregate EV with individual utility. Lotteries are enormous, durable businesses precisely because people knowingly accept a negative edge in exchange for variance. Any model that predicts memecoin trading decays because the average trader loses money is a model that also predicts lotteries and blackjack should not exist. 61:21

The third reason is the real one, and it is governance rather than economics. There is an equity business and a token business, and it is not clear what a token holder controls. Pump began with 100% buybacks that were not guaranteed for any period; earlier in the year it moved to 50% of revenue with a guaranteed twelve-month term, keeping the other half for reinvestment — and that commitment is subject to renewal 61:44–62:20.

🎯 Against ConsensusBarack's conclusion here is the most falsifiable thing he says all episode: depending on how you handicap renewal risk, the fair multiple is "6 to 10 or 10 to 14" 62:27–62:32. That is not an opinion about memecoins. It is an explicit statement that roughly half of Pump's valuation gap to Hyperliquid is the price of a promise that expires. If the commitment is renewed and extended, the discount should compress mechanically; if it is quietly narrowed, the discount was correct. Very few crypto valuation arguments come with a test this clean attached.

⚡ Hyperliquid And Ether.fi: Two Ways For An Application To Eat Its Own Infrastructure

Barack's enthusiasm for Hyperliquid is thesis-level rather than valuation-level.

"one of the best examples of the crypto thesis playing out which is instant settlement 247 trading bringing all assets on chain" 46:02 — Barack, ranking it behind only stablecoins and monetary assets as a demonstration of what crypto is for.

The detail he draws out is a structural split that most coverage misses. Most of Hyperliquid's re-rating came from volume in its HIP-3 markets — externally deployed markets tracking commodities, equities, indices and pre-IPO assets — but those markets are in growth mode and generate comparatively little revenue. The cash cow remains crypto perpetuals, which is also where the take rate is highest 42:03–42:45. The consequence he draws is directional: a genuine return of crypto flows would show up hardest in exactly the segment where Hyperliquid earns most.

🌐 Consensus CheckThe published H1 2026 numbers confirm the split almost exactly. Hyperliquid generated $419.3m in gross fees in the first half of 2026, up 31% year on year. Crypto perpetuals accounted for 82.7% of gross fees; HIP-3 perps grew from negligible to 11.2% of gross fees — while by some counts making up roughly half of total volume, because HIP-3's rules let outside builders keep a growing share of the trading fee. Hyperliquid has bought back and cancelled roughly $1.3bn of HYPE since launching in December 2024, with 99% of eligible trading fees routed into the Assistance Fund. Half the volume, an eighth of the fees: that is what Barack is describing, and it is a deliberate design choice rather than a failure. Source: 21Shares, H1 2026 Hyperliquid earnings analysis. 42:03
💡 Key InsightThe most quietly consequential claim in this stretch is about price discovery, not fees. Barack notes that new listings — SpaceX, Cerebras, Unitree — have seen pre-IPO price discovery happen on Hyperliquid, and that bankers setting IPO prices are "going to look at their Hyperliquid screen" 46:18–46:38. If that becomes normal, a crypto venue has acquired a function that has nothing to do with crypto: it becomes the reference price for private-market assets. That is the application layer absorbing a role the traditional financial infrastructure used to hold, which is the title thesis operating one level up from token revenue. 46:18

Ether.fi is the episode's clearest case study in application-layer mobility. Barack's fund made its first venture investment there — a Series A around February 2024, when it was purely a liquid restaking business 47:39–48:09. The business today is unrecognisable: 65%+ of revenue now comes from the neobank product, meaning credit-card usage and borrow revenue from users borrowing against balances, with only about 35% from yield and staking, and that share shrinking 50:43–51:11.

"a year ago, they were doing $300,000 of credit card volume a day. Now, they're doing 3 to 4 million a day." 52:01 — Barack, on a 10x that he thinks is about to hockey-stick rather than plateau.

Two structural details make the case. First, interest income — which mature neobanks earn most of their money from — is still only about 4% of Ether.fi's revenue, against 60–70% at Nubank 55:16–55:40. That is the gap he expects to close. Second, the way they closed the infrastructure requirement: rather than building a lending stack, they run their own instance of Aave v4 on an 80/20 revenue share, getting a best-in-class product with a lean team 55:45–56:22.

🔗 ConnectionMoney legos → the modern lean startup. Hoffman's framing is that Ether.fi is "packaging up Ethereum and selling it" 54:23 — that the cost of evolving from neobank to neo-brokerage was near zero because Ethereum itself evolved from a neobank into a neo-brokerage, and Ether.fi simply repackaged the new assets for consumers. Barack agrees and both land on the same phrase: the modern reincarnation of OG money Legos 58:26–58:34. This is the application-layer thesis in its most literal form — the chain does the capital-intensive work and earns rails economics; the application owns the customer and earns the margin.
🔍 Source Verified✅ The Ether.fi transformation is documented and slightly further along than the conversation suggests. On 13 August 2026 Ether.fi shipped a neobank upgrade adding tokenised stocks and metals via xStocks, portfolio-backed borrowing through an Aave market on Optimism at around 4% APY, expanded fiat rails and 3% card cashback — alongside programmatic ETHFI buybacks funded by product revenue. Contemporaneous reporting put the app at roughly 500,000 users, about 150,000 issued cards and a $2bn annual transaction run-rate, with the Aave borrowing market holding about $160m of supply against $23m of active loans. Sources: The Block, 13 August 2026; The Defiant; crypto.news.
🌐 Consensus CheckThe mispricing Barack describes has a specific recent cause. He notes that Ether.fi fell more than 10% alongside Lido on the day of "the Ethereum inflation reduction proposal" 51:16–51:27. That is EIP-8363, published in August 2026 by a group of Ethereum researchers, which would burn an increasing share of validator rewards as more ETH is staked, reaching a full burn at roughly 60.2m ETH staked — cutting net consensus yield from about 2.6% to about 1.2%, a 54% reduction phased over eighteen months. Lido said on 14 August that it supports issuance reform in principle but that this draft had not cleared its risk review; the EIP was left out of the Hegota upgrade package and no client team has backed it. Barack's point stands on the data: the market repriced Ether.fi as a staking business on a staking-policy headline, when staking is now the minority of its revenue and falling. 51:04

🪙 Coupling, Decoupling, And What Is Actually Left For Chains

Hoffman puts a genuinely difficult question: if VVV and HYPE grew idiosyncratically through a bear market on their own revenues, does that mean they also fail to benefit when crypto beta returns? Venice in particular has no exposure to Bitcoin going to $400,000 38:04–38:48.

Barack's answer is "partially coupled, partially decoupled, but the decoupling part is in a positive way" 38:50–38:56, and the mechanism he gives is about flows rather than fundamentals. Tokens as an asset class have had negative drift for eighteen months — capital leaving crypto for equities and AI — while equities enjoy structurally positive flows from 401(k)s and pensions. He argues that negative drift is cyclical rather than permanent and has probably just flipped, which means every token benefits from the asset-class rotation regardless of its own business 40:02–41:08.

On top of that base, he layers asset-specific reflexivity: Pump's fundamentals improve directly with onchain activity and memecoin trading; Hyperliquid's improve with crypto flows landing in its highest-take-rate segment; Venice has no crypto reflexivity but has what he considers an equally large tide in AI adoption 41:32–43:50.

🧵 Running ThesisBarack's closing taxonomy is tighter than "apps beat chains." He names three surviving categories — applications, money, and usage — and assigns each an example. Applications: Venice, Hyperliquid, Pump, Ether.fi. Money: Bitcoin, Zcash, and possibly ETH. Usage: Solana, Base, Robinhood's chain, which he calls heavily utilised while acknowledging Solana is "going through this transition where it's incredibly utilized, but they're not generating a ton of revenue because there's not like the MEV that it had back in the day" 64:00–65:46. The unstated implication is that "usage" is a category without a clear value-accrual story attached — which is precisely the problem the fat-protocol thesis was invented to solve, now reopened. Status: developing.
🎯 Against ConsensusHis remark on ETH is the most contrarian thing he says, and it is a reversal. Having looked at Ethereum "from a revenue perspective for a while, which made me think that Ethereum was quite overvalued," he now says he is more curious about ETH as money than he has been in a long time — citing quantum risk to Bitcoin and the concentration of Bitcoin ownership in vehicles like Strategy as the reason the monetary slot may not be permanently filled 64:19–65:00. That is an unusual move for a cash-flow investor: explicitly abandoning the discounted-cash-flow frame for one asset because he thinks the asset's value is monetary rather than commercial. It is also the honest consequence of his own thesis — if chains cannot capture fees, then the only bull case left for a chain's token is that it becomes money.
📜 AnalogBarack's enthusiasm for Zcash is framed historically rather than financially — it serves a different type of user and "in many respects is the original ethos of what crypto was 10 plus years ago," which is why he thinks it is resonating with OG Bitcoin holders and seeing structural inflows 64:04–64:19. It is the one asset in the conversation he does not model, and he does not pretend otherwise; the case is that a monetary asset's value is a function of what people believe about it, and cash-flow analysis has nothing useful to say about that.

Phase 3: Specific Item Breakdown

🟣 Venice / VVV — An Off-Chain AI Business With An On-Chain Payout Policy

A private, uncensored, multi-model AI product that routes a rising share of its free cash flow into programmatic token burns, because no law yet requires it to.

What It Covers: Subscriptions at $18, $68 and $200 a month plus credit purchases; two programmatic burns (new signups by tier, roughly 5% of credit purchases) with resubscription burns modelled next; DIEM as tokenised compute, minted by locking VVV, worth roughly a dollar a day of inference. Barack's model: ~$107m annualised revenue and ~$8.3m annualised burns as of August; $336m and $70m projected for 2027; 50x on burns; $43.89 target against ~$16 spot. Mines — an App Store for AI products — carries $29m, or 40%, of the 2027 burn projection and was not live.

Why It Matters: It is the clearest worked example in the episode of treating a token as a claim on a policy rather than on an entity, and of what that costs analytically: the entire model is downstream of a commitment the company can revise.

🔵 Pump / PUMP — The Governance Discount, Isolated

A durable speculation business trading at roughly 5x buybacks against 30–40x comparables, with most of the gap attributable to a buyback promise that expires.

What It Covers: 100% buybacks initially, unguaranteed; then 50% of revenue guaranteed for twelve months, subject to renewal, with the other half for reinvestment. Barack's fair multiple: 6–10x or 10–14x depending on renewal handicap. Revenue durability tested against the OpenSea precedent and, in his view, passed over two-plus years. On DefiLlama's trailing-30-day tracker around this period, Pump.fun led Hyperliquid on revenue.

Why It Matters: It isolates a variable crypto rarely prices explicitly. The discount is not about memecoins being distasteful or the revenue being fake; it is the market pricing the expiry date on a distribution commitment.

🟢 Hyperliquid / HYPE — Half The Volume, An Eighth Of The Fees

An exchange whose growth engine and its earnings engine are now two different products, by design.

What It Covers: HIP-3 markets let external builders deploy perps on commodities, equities, indices and pre-IPO assets, keeping a growing share of fees — roughly half of volume but 11.2% of H1 2026 gross fees. Crypto perps remain 82.7% of gross fees. H1 2026 gross fees $419.3m, up 31% year on year. Roughly $1.3bn of HYPE bought and cancelled since December 2024, funded by 99% of eligible fees via the Assistance Fund. Emerging function: pre-IPO price discovery for SpaceX, Cerebras and Unitree.

Why It Matters: Barack's directional bet is that a return of crypto flows lands in the highest-take-rate segment, so the fee line should move much faster than the volume line. It is also the cleanest case of an application acquiring a market function — reference pricing — that used to belong to intermediaries.

🟠 Ether.fi / ETHFI — The Pivot Out Of The Infra Hangover

A liquid restaking protocol that quietly became an onchain neo-brokerage while the market kept pricing it as Lido.

What It Covers: 65%+ of revenue now from credit-card usage and borrow revenue; ~35% from yield and staking and falling. Card volume from $300k a day to $3–4m a day year on year. Interest income just 4% of revenue against 60–70% at Nubank. Own instance of Aave v4 on an 80/20 revenue share. Blockworks modelled $21m of buy-and-burns over twelve months on halved growth rates; Barack argues $30m and a 30x multiple, implying a token price over a dollar. Verified: a 13 August 2026 neobank upgrade adding tokenised stocks via xStocks, Aave-powered borrowing on Optimism at ~4%, 3% card cashback and programmatic buybacks, with roughly 500k users and 150k cards.

Why It Matters: It is the mispricing the whole episode is built to detect: a business whose revenue mix has already changed, still trading on the identity it had two years ago — and repricing on staking-policy headlines that barely touch it.

🟡 The Fat Protocol Thesis — The Idea Being Inverted

The 2016 claim that crypto reverses the internet's value distribution, concentrating value at the shared protocol layer rather than the application layer.

What It Covers: Joel Monegro, Union Square Ventures, 8 August 2016. The internet had thin protocols and fat applications; crypto, the argument ran, would have the opposite, because applications must acquire and use the native protocol token. Monegro published "Thin Applications" in 2020, revising the picture. The strongest empirical test since has been Ethereum's post-Dencun fee collapse: L1 revenue down roughly 75% year on year, daily burn to roughly 100 ETH, the network mildly inflationary, and a dynamic blob-fee floor added via EIP-7918 in the December 2025 Fusaka upgrade.

Why It Matters: It is the thesis Barack's chart inverts, and understanding it is what separates "apps are winning" as an investment conclusion from "chains don't matter" as an architectural one. Only the first is supported.

🔴 The CLARITY Act — The Missing Legal Layer

The US market-structure bill both speakers assume will arrive and make token value accrual legally enforceable, and which failed a Senate cloture vote eight days after this episode published.

What It Covers: The Digital Asset Market Clarity Act would split digital-asset oversight between the SEC and the CFTC and set rules for classification, trading platforms, intermediaries, customer protection and DeFi. House passage July 2025; revised Senate Banking Committee version May 2026; merged Banking/Agriculture text July 2026; cloture failed 49–50 on 15 September 2026, with a motion to reconsider filed.

Why It Matters: Every structure in this episode — programmatic burns as a substitute for legal entitlement, token warrants as a substitute for equity claims, "walking the walk" as a substitute for enforceability — exists because this bill does not. Its failure makes those workarounds the permanent architecture rather than a bridge.
