Executive Summary

Man Group's talk is the most important enterprise AI governance case study from the conference. It covers the full arc: genuine AI deployment in production → the hidden failure mode of power-user-led adoption → the governance fix that unlocked agentic scale. The central argument: your organisational context is your AI moat, and skills governance is the mechanism that makes it usable.

💰 Stakes Framing

"We manage real people's money — teachers in Canada, metal workers in Japan. If we get this wrong, we could lose real money."

Regulated capital, real pensions, real consequences. This framing explains why governance is non-negotiable at Man Group — and it scales: the more consequential the AI output, the higher the governance bar must be.

💡 Key InsightThe stakes framing is itself a governance argument. When the cost of a bad AI output is someone's pension drawdown, the quality bar for skills is fundamentally different from when the cost is a bad Slack summary. Know your stakes; design your governance accordingly.

📊 AI Trading Pipeline

A trading signal ranks securities by some factor and goes long the top ones, short the bottom ones. Quality depends on: the factor (what you rank by), rigorous backtesting (15+ years of history), and comparability (if teams use different backtest versions, results are meaningless).

Man Group's full AI-driven pipeline: AI generated the idea → AI retrieved data → AI ran backtest → AI wrote strategy proposal → AI productionised the signal → humans reviewed for sanity.

"There are trading signals running right now in production at Man Group... that were researched, backtested and proposed by AI."

Value of $1,000 invested in 2021 using the credit card spend signal (demo case study, not live trading performance)
🌐 Consensus CheckAI-assisted quant research is mainstream. What is notable here is the degree of AI autonomy — the full pipeline end-to-end — and Claude Code as the orchestration layer. A meaningful frontier advance, not a marginal improvement.

🧊 The Iceberg Problem

"Think of it like an iceberg. The signal is the tip. Underneath it are all of the workflows that make it possible."

Beneath the visible signal: data cleaning workflows, price stitching routines, outlier detection, backtest infrastructure, distributed execution, result aggregation. If Team A uses workflow v1.2 and Team B uses v1.4, their results are incomparable — and you can't tell whether Signal A genuinely outperforms Signal B.

🔗 ConnectionThe workflows beneath the signal are exactly what skills encode. A well-governed backtest skill means every team runs the same version, with the same parameters, producing comparable outputs. The skill is not just a productivity tool — it's a measurement standardisation mechanism.

❌ The Power User Trap

"It was really the power users that were building the skills. It wasn't the process owners."

The expense report story: A frequent traveller built a skill to process expense receipts. Shared at show-and-tell. Teammates started using it. Days later: wrong cost centres across departments. Root cause: the developer's cost centre was hardcoded. Nobody reviewed it. It worked for him.

Why this is structural: Power users optimise for themselves. They don't know downstream consequences. Unreviewed skills spread virally. In backtesting: incomparable results. For agents: no commonality to compose.

"People were just codifying their ways of doing things. They weren't the organisational ways of doing things."
⚡ TensionFernando celebrates the adoption wave — "out of this world" — then immediately describes how it created dysfunction. The lesson: adoption energy and governance structure must exist simultaneously. Most orgs discover governance need only after adoption has already gone wrong.

✅ The Fix — Governance as the Secret Sauce

"Skills governance started to be the secret source that unlocked these enterprise use cases."

A common skills marketplace modelled on a library: every skill has visibility (searchable by all), tagging (by department/function), evals (quality testing), ownership (workflow owner, not power user), usage tracking, review process, and retirement path.

Managed vs. Community: Managed = formally governed, owned, tested. Community = experimental, visible, lighter governance. Plugins: Skills grouped into domain bundles — install a whole capability in one action.

📜 AnalogThe library model maps to software package management (npm, PyPI, Homebrew). Man Group is building the npm for AI skills — and npm's lesson is that governance matters more than quantity.

🎓 5 Lessons from Man Group

1. Focus on organisational context — it's your IP and your moat. The frontier labs cannot give it to you. The work is exposing what you already know.

"The frontier labs are not going to solve context for you. It's not on the internet. They don't know your workflows. And you already have that context."

2. Treat skills like production code. They will run in automated pipelines. They need versioning, testing, and deprecation. Plan for this from day one.

3. Plan governance before you plan rollout. Who owns each skill? Who reviews it? How does it retire? Decide before the first skill ships, not after the hundredth.

4. Adoption is a people problem, not a licensing problem. Access is not enough. Engagement requires outreach, training, and cultivation.

5. Rethink workflows, don't just augment them. The real value comes from restructuring how work gets done, not adding AI on top of existing manual processes.

📋 Protocol Cheat Sheet

Governance Checklist (apply before skill #2, not skill #100)

  • Identify the workflow owner (not the power user)
  • Assign formal ownership to that person
  • Write evals for every managed skill before publishing
  • Track usage — retire unused; update misused
  • Separate managed from community tiers
  • Group related skills into plugins
  • Build a searchable, tagged catalogue visible to everyone
  • Establish lifecycle: create → review → publish → track → update → retire

Golden Path to Agentic Capability

  1. Capture organisational context in skills
  2. Govern those skills (ownership, testing, lifecycle)
  3. Connect skills to data and compute capabilities
  4. Enable agents to compose skills for complex tasks
  5. Iterate toward agent swarms for autonomous exploration
