⚡ The Bottom Line

  • The Orchard counterfeiting vulnerability was found because Zcash went looking for it, and the fix was not a patch but a mathematical proof. Multiple teams formally verified that the new Ironwood circuit's implementation matches its spec, which converts "could there be a second undetected bug?" from a judgement call about auditor competence into a claim that would require the proof engine itself to be false.
  • The turnstile is the reason a soundness bug in a $1.7 billion shielded pool was survivable. Because value crossing between pools is metered, the blast radius was bounded to Orchard rather than Zcash's whole supply — and because the turnstile did not trigger during migration, the team can now say with high confidence the bug was never exploited.
  • Ojha's ranking of the quantum problem is the most useful framework in the episode, and the top item is not cryptography. It is commitment to a plan. Second is whether you can hold funds safely without acting; Zcash solved that in Ironwood, Bitcoin has not. Privacy against a quantum adversary is third, and the final upgrade is fourth because frozen pools make it a non-event.
  • Mumtaz's strategic framing is that privacy is crypto's last PvE — the only market where nobody is competing. "Everybody's trying to be a trading app or a launchpad... but who's trying to be private money?" He runs Helius on Solana and holds ZEC personally, and treats the two as non-rival.
  • There is no Solana privacy pitch in this episode at all — which is itself the finding. Ojha says the choice was explicitly between building a privacy layer on Solana and making Zcash win, and they chose Zcash because "uncompromising privacy... has to be at the base layer." Neither man argues for L1-privacy-as-a-feature.

🔬 The Bug Was Found Because Zcash Went Looking — And The Answer Was A Proof

Mumtaz's framing of the Orchard vulnerability inverts the usual crisis narrative: the bug was a product of a deliberate programme, not a failure of one.

"A year ago, maybe even longer than that, it was pretty clear that AI was particularly good at exposing security flaws — it can just look at more places more deeply at all times. And so Zcash got ahead of this by red teaming and doing audits internally and beefing up all sorts of efforts like that across multiple core teams, not just one or two."

The finder was "Taylor, who is a very specific domain expert on Zcash and related tech," working with the latest AI tools, and the flaw "would have allowed for potentially counterfeiting shielded notes in the Orchard pool specifically" 06:30–06:50.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed with names and dates. Independent researcher Taylor Hornby found the Orchard soundness vulnerability on 29 May 2026, during an AI-assisted security audit contracted by Shielded Labs, using Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.8. Separately, Anthropic's restricted Mythos model audited the full Zcash protocol at Shielded Labs' request and found no new critical bugs, reported 12 June 2026. Ironwood, formally NU6.3, activated on 28 July 2026 at block 3,428,143, creating a new shielded pool and restricting Orchard to withdrawals. Source: crypto.news; CoinDesk, 28 July 2026; Bitcoin.com News.

Mumtaz then makes the argument he thinks investors actually care about, which is not about the bug but about the next one: "As an investor, that's what you look at. You look at, well, could this happen again, because there'll be more bugs" 09:20–09:27. His answer is that the core teams "have basically mathematically proven the soundness of undetectable counterfeit bugs," so the order of magnitude of risk is now "an order of magnitude less."

Ojha's explanation of what that means is the clearest passage in the episode, and worth following carefully because it is easy to overstate.

His starting point is the trust chain you had before. How do you know there are only 21 million Bitcoin? You run a full node and count. In Zcash you cannot count, because balances are private. So the next best thing is metering the boundaries — counting value into and out of each shielded pool. "This is what turnstiling, like, words mean" 11:17–11:26. But within a pool, the guarantee used to be social: "there's this spec that a bunch of smart people wrote, reviewed and really audited, and we just like know, because all these audits and people are smart, that there's no bugs" 11:49–12:00.

"Now, what formal verification has done is we have a mathematical proof that our implementation perfectly matches the spec."

And the spec obligation is narrow enough to state in a sentence: "If there's a payment and the proof passes, that means someone owned some notes and the total input of the transaction is equal to all the outputs. So if you spend five ZEC, there cannot be more than five ZEC out. And that's kind of all we have to prove here" 12:44–13:04.

His de-mystification of "mathematical proof" is the best explain-it-simply moment in the cluster: "Let's say you had a rule that all brown dogs are nice. Sally is a brown dog. Okay, that implies Sally is nice. Formal verification is just doing that at a large scale. It's not some big scary thing. It's just a lot of tedious work once you have millions of conditions" 13:25–13:50.

💡 Key InsightThe precise thing formal verification bought here is worth stating carefully, because the marketing version overstates it. It proves the implementation matches the spec. Ojha is explicit that Zcash's failure mode was an implementation bug, not a design error — "Zcash has never had a problem where we've designed a wrong spec." A wrong spec would still be wrong, formally verified. What it genuinely eliminates is the largest historical source of ZK circuit failures: the gap between what cryptographers intended and what the code does. 12:05–14:06
⚡ TensionMumtaz says he is "95% certain just statistically speaking, but logically speaking I think I am 99.999% sure" that the bug was never exploited 09:05–09:13. Those two numbers are not two views of one thing — 95% and 99.999% differ by three orders of magnitude in residual risk. He never reconciles them, and neither figure is sourced to any published analysis.
🎯 Against ConsensusThe standard industry response to a critical vulnerability is disclosure, patch and audit. Zcash's response — freeze the affected pool, build a new one, formally verify its circuit, and force a migration bounded by an independently checkable supply constraint — treats the bug as a reason to change the class of assurance rather than to fix the instance. Whether or not it was proportionate, it is a materially different playbook from anything a comparable protocol has run. 08:38–09:05

🪆 The Turnstile Is What Makes A Zero-Knowledge Bug Survivable

This is the structural point most commentary on the incident missed, and both guests spend real time on it.

The trade-off Mumtaz states is fundamental rather than a Zcash design flaw: "The big trade-off in ZK, or privacy in general, is the auditability of the supply. If you have perfect privacy, by definition there are some things that have to be private — such as who holds what — and so that makes it particularly difficult to completely verify that supply" 07:45–08:09.

Zcash's mitigation is the turnstile: value moving between shielded pools is metered, so what exits a pool can never exceed what verifiably entered it. Ojha's analogy is a lending market: "Let's say you have two different lending markets, one with 10 million USDC, one with 1 million USDC. There's a bug in the 1 million one. Only 1 million's at risk" 11:28–11:40.

💡 Key InsightThe turnstile is a compartmentalisation primitive disguised as an accounting one. It exists to make supply auditable, but its operational value here was that it capped the blast radius of an unknown future bug to a single pool — and then, during the forced migration, doubled as the detector: if the turnstile does not trigger, the vulnerability provably was not exploited at scale. Any system that keeps a supply invariant across privacy boundaries gets both properties for the price of one. 06:52–09:05

Mumtaz is direct about the information environment this created, and it is a useful case study in itself. "There was a lot of misinformation about this, because people were not aware that there is a turnstile mechanism which basically infers that only that pool would be affected and not the entire supply" 06:52–07:04. His account of why the rumours were incoherent on their own terms: the claim that Zcash was "printing infinite money" would require overcoming infinite selling pressure and would not produce a rising aggregate shielded pool 07:27–07:43.

🌐 Consensus CheckMumtaz's argument that "uncertainty" is a weapon specific to privacy assets is correct and under-discussed. On a transparent chain, a counterfeiting claim is falsifiable in minutes by anyone with a node. On a shielded chain, the same claim can only be bounded, not disproven — which makes FUD structurally cheaper to manufacture and more expensive to rebut. That asymmetry is a permanent cost of privacy, not a one-off incident cost. 08:13–08:27
📜 AnalogCompartmentalised lending markets → shielded pools — Ojha's isolated-lending-market analogy is exact, and it points at the general design lesson: in any system where global state cannot be verified, the substitute is bounded compartments with metered boundaries. Zcash reached this by necessity; most DeFi protocols reached the same conclusion after losses. 11:28–11:40

⏱️ The Quantum Ladder: Commitment First, Cryptography Third

Ojha's ranked list is the most transferable thing in the episode, because it applies to any long-duration asset facing a known-but-undated technology threat.

Rung one: commitment to a plan. "One of the biggest things about quantum is a commitment to a plan. In Zcash this has been clearly there from a long time ago... In Bitcoin, it's still kind of unclear — are we adding a new signature? What are we going to do?" 24:17–24:37.

Rung two: can you hold funds and do nothing? "Can you hold funds and go into a coma for 20 years? That is solved in Zcash" — via the quantum recoverability shipped in Ironwood. Move to Ironwood and your funds remain recoverable even if elliptic curves break, with no action required 24:37–25:27.

Rung three: privacy against a quantum adversary. Orchard already limited quantum privacy leakage — an adversary who knows your address learns only about payments you receive, nothing about payments you make. Tachyon removes the conditionals entirely: "everything you do cannot be learned by a quantum computer at all" (20:00–20:31, 25:30–25:51).

Rung four: the final upgrade. Ojha argues it barely matters. "You shouldn't care if that came the day before quantum computers came out, because we just freeze the old pools and everyone can safely move over to the quantum one" 26:02–26:18.

His Bitcoin comparison at rung two is the sharpest technical criticism in the episode: Bitcoin holders would need a signature-scheme change, and even the cleverest mitigation has a hole. "You have a slight asterisk of, if you use an HD path and Bitcoin one day decides to freeze all non-post-quantum funds, you could do a ZKP — but provably Satoshi's coins don't follow that. So Satoshi's coins would be stolen" 25:02–25:19.

💡 Key InsightRung one is the one worth stealing. Ojha is arguing that for a store of value, the existence of a credible plan is itself a monetary property — it removes a class of tail risk from the holder's calculation regardless of when the threat arrives. That reframes quantum readiness from an engineering roadmap item into a component of the asset's moneyness, which is exactly the argument Mumtaz then formalises. 24:17–24:37

Mumtaz's version is information-theoretic and is the most original thing he says.

"The entire point of a store of value is that it is a low entropy asset in a high entropy channel."

His illustration: on this very call, low-entropy carriers — antennas, fibre — transmit a high-entropy message, and "you can only get signal from the difference of those things." A highly volatile or surprise-prone asset "fundamentally cannot be a store of value, because store of value requires no surprises. It requires that if I put my money here, it'll still be around in five years. Sure, I don't control the price, but there won't be tail risk" 27:28–28:45.

From which the quantum conclusion follows as an expected-value argument: "Best case scenario is that quantum is delayed, but you're at least ready for it. Worst case is that everything is hacked. So one path leads to ruin, one path leads to meh. If you're opting into the ruin, that is a bad trade" 29:08–29:27.

⚡ TensionHaving built that case, Mumtaz then substantially defuses it: "there's pretty wide consensus that it's going to take a long time to actually arrive... there's always the physical layer which you just cannot necessarily speed up," and "I do think Bitcoin will be ready, for what it's worth, by the time quantum comes." If Bitcoin will be ready and quantum is far off, the quantum differential is a narrative advantage rather than a material one — which is not the impression the first half of the section leaves. (26:55–27:16, 31:46–31:57)
🔍 Source Verified⚠️ The claim is contested by serious people, including Mumtaz himself in public. Nic Carter has argued that for privacy coins, migrating to post-quantum schemes does not protect historical transactions, which could be retroactively decrypted. Mumtaz publicly agreed this applies to many privacy coins but said it is "not necessarily true for Zcash's privacy, given advanced opsec." Sean Bowe's position is stronger: Zcash's fully shielded pool does not put critical sender/receiver information on the ledger at all, so there is "no quantum computer or powerful AI that will be able to look back at the Zcash blockchain 1000 years from now and figure out who made every fully shielded transaction." None of this debate surfaces in the episode. Source: Bitcoinist, "Is Zcash Quantum-Resistant Yet? Experts Weigh In."

The host raises Cameron Winklevoss's claim that Zcash is "the one and only quantum resistant network" and the pushback it drew. Ojha's answer is a model of how to handle a founder-adjacent overstatement: he neither endorses nor attacks it, and instead replaces the binary label with the four-rung ladder — "we're already through two out of the four, the two most important. The third most important one comes out in a couple months" 26:22–26:33.

⚡ Tachyon: Recursion Deletes Node Compute, And Wallets Stop Scanning

Ojha lists four things Tachyon does, and two of them are structural rather than incremental.

Full post-quantum privacy. No conditionals, no address-leakage asterisk.

Scale via recursion. "We introduce recursive zero knowledge proofs into Zcash." Transaction size drops "by over 5x" — but the bigger claim is about what stops mattering: "node processing time goes away as a dimension, as a thing you have to worry about, as does state size. So the only bottleneck of scaling Zcash is then going to be consensus — the bandwidth of consensus. Compute fully eliminated, state size fully eliminated" 20:44–21:43.

Metadata leakage. Edge cases — a multisig, or starting to sign a transaction and losing internet for an hour — currently leak the fact that an edge case occurred. Tachyon removes even that 21:52–22:30.

Wallets. "The other big one's wallets. I don't know if you ever use a Zcash wallet — you have to go through a stupid syncing thing... Today, if we had a million transactions, you'd have to scan a million things. In the Tachyon world, it's O(1). No scanning at all" 22:52–23:28. He mentions he had made sync 6x faster immediately before the call, while insisting the interim fix is not the answer.

🔍 Source Verified✅ Confirmed, including the attribution. Sean Bowe, with Daira Grigg (Hopwood) and Hopwood, introduced the accumulation technique in Halo (2019) — deferring linear-time polynomial commitment opening checks and accumulating them into one final operation — which was later generalised and rebranded as "folding" in Nova. Ojha's telling is accurate. His own Fractal paper (Chiesa, Ojha, Spooner, 2019) is the post-quantum transparent recursion work he references from the same era. Project Tachyon's published design centres on proof-carrying data and oblivious synchronisation, which is the mechanism behind the O(1) wallet claim: wallets query untrusted servers for relevant proofs without revealing what they are looking for. Source: eprint.iacr.org/2019/1076; Halo (IACR 2019/1021); tachyon.z.cash.
🔗 ConnectionFractal (2019) → Halo (2019) → Tachyon (2026) — the two people on this call independently produced the two recursion lineages that Zcash is now shipping together. Ojha's group did post-quantum transparent recursion; Bowe's did accumulation. Tachyon needs both: accumulation for the scale win, post-quantum transparency for the privacy win. That is an unusually direct line from academic work to production monetary infrastructure. (01:44–02:01 → 20:52 → 21:13)
💡 Key InsightThe O(1) wallet is the most consequential item on the list and gets the least airtime. Every practical failure across this whole cluster — bad UX, users not shielding, the "stupid syncing thing" — traces to wallets having to scan the chain to find their own notes. Removing that is what makes shielded mobile money viable at all; the TPS numbers are downstream. 22:52–23:28

Ojha's headline ambition, stated at the top of the episode, is worth recording because it is the most aggressive scaling target anyone in this cluster gives: "in two or three years' time you should be able to do 50,000-plus transactions, the same level that Mastercard and Visa hit, and get this all sub-second, and in parallel make this all post-quantum and secure" — under pure proof of work. And his diagnosis of what stands in the way: "the blocker for scale is actually the wallet side, not even the nodes" 01:09–01:38.

🕹️ Privacy Is The Last PvE — Crypto's Only Uncontested Market

Mumtaz's strategic case is market structure, not technology, and it is the part of the episode most likely to survive whatever happens to the price.

He starts by deflating the migration narrative the host offers him. Zcash "is still relatively speaking a tiny asset, even in crypto... there doesn't even need to be a migration from Bitcoin to Zcash. It can just be 5% of people putting some amount of money from Bitcoin, and that'll already be noticeable" 15:30–16:05.

Then the framing: "Privacy is the last PvE, in that it doesn't necessarily interfere with really anybody else's goals except for potentially Bitcoin. I still run my company on Solana, but on a personal capacity I hold Zcash... what Solana needs to do is completely different than Zcash. Same with really all the major chains except for Bitcoin" 16:09–16:43.

"Everybody's trying to be like a trading app or a launchpad and all those different things, but who's trying to be private money?"

The host adds the network-effect point and Mumtaz endorses it: "it's very hard to compete with the anonymity set as it keeps growing — how are you going to siphon liquidity? It's going to be very difficult" 18:01–18:10.

🏗️ Decentralisation Is Coordination Without A Middleman, Not Disorganisation

The host asks the right question: why can Zcash's contributors reach consensus and ship protocol changes when Bitcoin's cannot?

Mumtaz's answer is definitional. "One of the main fallacies in crypto is conflating decentralization with being uncoordinated. People assume, given past experiences, that it must mean disorganisation. But with any project there needs to be a unifying cohesive vision. Decentralization actually just means coordination without the middleman" 30:09–30:35.

He gives two reasons Zcash manages it: it is smaller, so the message stays unified; and the underlying principle is simple enough to be uncontroversial — "it just has to follow that a store of value has to be ready for surprises such as these" 30:44–31:04.

His Bitcoin diagnosis is an innovator's-dilemma argument: "Bitcoin people have this risk aversion now, which actually in itself is a risk for them — which is to say, well, we got to a trillion dollars or $2 trillion, so now we just have to keep doing what we were doing before. And then it's a misinterpretation of history that they think will lead to something different" 31:12–31:35.

Ojha's contribution is the more durable one, because it is historical rather than rhetorical. "It goes back to the very first Zcash announcement post that we need a culture of updating in order to get there. Zcash launched pioneering zero-knowledge proofs, and it was obvious to everybody that this first thing we're doing is not the end state" 32:55–33:12. Crucially, the culture has explicit boundaries: "people are mindful of — don't update things that should never be updated, like the halving schedule" 33:30–33:35.

💡 Key InsightThe distinction Ojha draws is the one Bitcoin's ossification debate usually collapses. A protocol can have a strong culture of updating and a strong culture of immutability, provided the two apply to different layers: cryptography updates, monetary policy does not. Zcash appears to have encoded that split at genesis, which is why the same community that shipped an emergency pool migration also treats the 21 million cap as untouchable. 32:55–33:35

⛏️ Why 18% Of Hashrate In One Listed Company Reads As Decentralising

The host raises Cypherpunk Technologies' mining announcement and asks the obvious question: does one organisation holding a large share of hashrate centralise the network?

Ojha's answer is counterintuitive and rests on a baseline correction. "A misconception people have across all of crypto is, what is decentralization at the consensus layer? Ethereum markets that we have hundreds of thousands of validators — no. If you take the top five, you're already at 60-plus percent." Proof of work is "a little bit more honest," with public pool trackers, "but you still don't see the breakdown of which company owns them — Foundry, now Cypherpunk, Chinese miners, etc. And whenever you do dive in, it's smaller than you'd expect. All the security arguments rest on it being like tens" 35:16–36:04.

Given that baseline, and given that economies of scale make warehouse mining structurally dominant, his conclusion is arithmetic: "Cypherpunk being in here adds one extra miner." Plus a governance bonus — "they hold a ton of coins, so it's very easy to see their opinion on stuff, because they post every single vote." The exchange ends on a dry note from Mumtaz: "we know for sure, no matter what, it's now one higher than what it was before" 36:56–37:02.

🔍 Source Verified⚠️ The share is understated on air. Cypherpunk Technologies launched Cypherpunk Mining on 18 August 2026 with approximately 4.2 GSol/s of Equihash hashrate across US facilities — about 18% of the Zcash network, not the "16% I believe" stated in the episode. 🎯 Against ConsensusThe standard reading of a listed treasury company taking a large hashrate share is capture risk. Ojha inverts it on two grounds — that the honest baseline everywhere is already concentrated among a handful of operators, and that a public company's governance positions are legible in a way an anonymous pool's are not. It is a genuine argument, but it is also a convenient one, and neither speaker mentions the concentration stacking underneath it.
⚡ TensionCypherpunk Technologies now occupies three positions in the Zcash ecosystem at once: roughly 18% of hashrate, a large ZEC treasury position accumulating toward a stated 5% of supply, and an investor slot on ZODL's cap table. Ojha's "one extra miner" framing evaluates only the first. The stacked position — mining, holding and funding the dominant wallet team — is the actual centralisation question, and the episode does not ask it.

🪙 Fair-Launch-Adjacent, Down-Only For Nine Years, And A Lockbox That Expires In 2028

The closing segment is a tokenomics crash course, and it is the least reliable part of the episode.

Mumtaz's distribution argument: Zcash mimics Bitcoin, launched in 2016 "in the proof-of-work type of days," so it never had "these extremely high FDV, low float types of games." And the nine-year drawdown was a feature: "the coin has been basically down only since launch, which is actually really good, because it means the only people remaining are the people who actually believe in the project and picked it up on the open market. He also corrects a common claim in Zcash's favour and against it: yes, ZEC avoided the high-profile privacy-coin delistings, but "shielded Zcash is available almost nowhere, and Zcash is also delisted in many places. Phase 3: Specific Item Breakdown

🟣 Ironwood (NU6.3) — A Formally Verified Replacement Pool

The emergency shielded pool built to replace Orchard after a counterfeiting vulnerability, and the first whose circuit ships with a machine-checked proof that the implementation matches its specification.

What It Includes: Activation on 28 July 2026 at block height 3,428,143. A new shielded value pool; Orchard restricted primarily to withdrawals, with funds crossing through the existing turnstile. Quantum recoverability, so held funds remain recoverable without any user action even if elliptic curves break. Formal verification — Mumtaz says "two or three in parallel" — of circuit soundness.

The Evidence: The proof obligation reduces to: if a payment's proof passes, the spender owned the notes and total inputs equal total outputs. Roughly $1.7–1.8 billion of shielded value migrated; 87% of Orchard's balance moved within five weeks, against 8% for Sprout→Sapling in 2018 and 1.2% for Sapling→Orchard in 2022.

What It Does Not Prove: Implementation-matches-spec is not spec-is-correct. Ojha is explicit that Zcash's failure mode was implementation, not design, and rests the stronger claim on that history. A cryptographic-assumption break would also invalidate the guarantee — his own caveat: "as long as your basic cryptography assumptions hold."

🟣 The Turnstile — Supply Auditability Without Balance Disclosure

The metering mechanism that counts value into and out of each shielded pool, and the only reason a soundness bug inside a billion-dollar pool was a contained incident rather than a supply crisis.

What It Includes: A boundary check on inter-pool transfers: the amount exiting a pool cannot exceed the amount verifiably deposited into it. It exists because Zcash cannot do what Bitcoin does — you cannot run a node and count balances when balances are private.

The Evidence: Two distinct jobs done by one primitive. Prospectively it compartmentalises: a bug in one pool risks only that pool's contents, which Ojha likens to isolated lending markets. Retrospectively it detects: because the turnstile did not trigger during the Orchard-to-Ironwood migration, the team can assert the vulnerability was not exploited at scale.

The Underlying Trade-Off: Mumtaz states it plainly — perfect privacy means some things must stay private, which makes complete supply verification impossible. The turnstile buys back a bounded, checkable version of supply auditability. The residual cost is epistemic: on a shielded chain, a counterfeiting rumour can be bounded but never falsified in the way a transparent chain can falsify one in minutes.

🟣 Project Tachyon — Four Upgrades In One, And The One That Matters Is Wallets

Sean Bowe's recursive-proof overhaul, which Ojha says delivers full post-quantum privacy, over 5x smaller transactions, the elimination of node compute and state size as scaling dimensions, and O(1) wallet sync.

Full Post-Quantum Privacy: Orchard limited quantum leakage conditionally — an adversary knowing your address learns about payments you receive but nothing about payments you make. Tachyon removes the conditionals: "everything you do cannot be learned by a quantum computer at all."

Scale Via Recursion: Recursive proofs using the accumulation technique Sean Bowe introduced in Halo (2019), later generalised as folding in Nova. Transaction size falls by more than 5x; more importantly, Ojha claims node processing time and state size cease to be constraints, leaving only consensus bandwidth and wallets.

Metadata Leakage: Today, edge cases — a multisig, or an interrupted signing session — leak the fact that an edge case occurred. Tachyon removes even that signal.

Wallets, The Real Prize: Current Zcash wallets scan the chain to find their own notes, so sync time grows with chain activity. Tachyon replaces this with proof-carrying wallet state and oblivious synchronisation — wallets query untrusted servers for relevant proofs without revealing what they seek. Ojha shipped a 6x interim speedup the day of recording while insisting the interim fix is not the answer. Timeline per Ojha: "a couple months."

🟣 The Quantum Ladder — Four Rungs, Ranked By Urgency

Ojha's ranking of the quantum problem for a monetary asset, whose top rung is not cryptography at all but the existence of a credible, committed plan.

Rung One — Commitment To A Plan: Holders need to know upgrades are coming and roughly what they are. Zcash has had this "from a long time ago"; in Bitcoin, "it's still kind of unclear — are we adding a new signature?"

Rung Two — Hold And Do Nothing: Can you leave funds untouched for twenty years and still recover them? Solved in Zcash by Ironwood's quantum recoverability. Ojha argues Bitcoin's equivalents all carry an asterisk, and that a freeze-plus-ZKP scheme would provably fail for Satoshi's coins.

Rung Three — Quantum Privacy: Closing the residual address-linked leakage. Tachyon's job, due within months of recording.

Rung Four — The Final Migration: Ojha argues this rung barely matters, because old pools can simply be frozen and users moved across — so its timing is not a risk.

The Contested Part: Whether shielded history is retroactively safe is disputed. Nic Carter has argued post-quantum migration does not protect pre-migration transactions for privacy coins generally; Mumtaz has publicly agreed for many privacy coins but not for Zcash "given advanced opsec"; Sean Bowe argues fully shielded transactions never put the critical information on the ledger at all. None of this appears in the episode.

🟣 The Funding Model — A Lockbox, A Multisig, And A 2028 Expiry

Zcash's post-2024 development funding: a fixed slice of block rewards split between an upfront grants committee and an accumulating lockbox that only disburses as private coinholder votes direct, with the whole arrangement expiring at the 2028 halving.

What It Includes: Under ZIP 1015, active since NU6 in November 2024, 20% of block subsidy splits into 8% for Zcash Community Grants — the upfront, DAO-managed channel Ojha calls "community grants" — and 12% into an in-protocol lockbox. Under NU6.1, lockbox contents disburse to a 2-of-3 P2SH multisig held by the Zcash Foundation, ECC and Shielded Labs, directed by a coinholder retroactive grants programme.

The Governance Feature Worth Noting: Zcash runs coin-weighted voting privately: votes run for a couple of weeks, individual balances and amounts are never revealed, and only aggregate totals publish. That neutralises the strongest objection to token voting — that it exposes and chills large holders — and it is a capability almost unique to a shielded chain.

Where The Episode Errs: Ojha implies the full 20% flows into the lockbox; it is 12%, with 8% going to grants. He does correctly identify both channels and correctly dates the expiry to the third halving in 2028, after which the community re-votes on whether to extend, change or return everything to miners — the same sunset mechanism that has now forced three previous renegotiations.
