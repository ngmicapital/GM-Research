This week four unrelated domains tell the same story: the binding constraint moved off the obvious lever. The IEA finds electricity and grid connections — not chips or algorithms — now gate AI's buildout, with data-centre demand more than doubling to ~945 TWh by 2030; a 2025 study in Societies (n=666) links heavy AI use to weaker critical thinking via cognitive offloading; and prediction markets crossed $20B+ in monthly volume as Google began embedding Kalshi and Polymarket odds in Search and Finance. The practical throughline: stop optimising the visible lever and find the real bottleneck.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Primary Source · IEA + WEF · Energy & AI · Apr 2025 / May 2026

AI's binding constraint is electricity, not intelligence

The most important AI story of 2026 is not a model release — it is a power bill. The IEA's Energy and AI analysis projects global data-centre electricity demand to more than double by 2030 to roughly 945 TWh (about Japan's total consumption today), with AI-specific demand tripling as the primary driver. The scarce resource is no longer compute. It is the megawatt — and, more precisely, the grid connection that delivers it.

The constraint is structural, not financial. Capital for chips is abundant; interconnection queues are not. Developers face multi-year waits to plug into the grid, so a wave of operators is routing around the bottleneck with on-site generation — the IEA counts 15–27 GW of on-site gas plausibly powering US data centres by 2030, while the pipeline of small-modular-reactor offtake agreements has jumped from 25 GW at end-2024 to 45 GW. The World Economic Forum now names grid connectivity outright as “the strategic bottleneck” of the AI transition.

When everyone optimises the visible lever (bigger models, more GPUs), the real limit migrates to the lever no one is watching. In 2026 that lever is a transformer and a queue position — physical infrastructure that money cannot conjure on a training-run timescale.

Research · Gerlich · Societies (MDPI) · 2025

The cost of outsourcing thought: cognitive offloading and the critical-thinking decline

A peer-reviewed study by Michael Gerlich — AI Tools in Society, published in Societies (2025), surveying 666 participants — reports a strong negative association between frequent AI-tool use and critical-thinking scores (r = −0.68), mediated by cognitive offloading: the habit of delegating mental work to a tool correlated +0.72 with AI use and −0.75 with critical-thinking performance. Younger users showed the highest dependence and the lowest scores. (It is correlational — not proof that AI degrades cognition — but the mechanism is plausible and the signal is large.)

The useful distinction, which Anthropic's own education research echoes, is between beneficial offloading (letting AI handle grammar or boilerplate so attention goes to the argument) and detrimental offloading (bypassing the thinking itself, eliminating the “desirable difficulty” that builds durable knowledge). Same tool, opposite outcomes — the difference is what you hand over.

The constraint on human capability in an AI world is not access to answers — that is now free and infinite. It is sustained cognitive engagement, which is finite and atrophies when unused. The bottleneck moved from knowing to still bothering to think.

Reporting · Bloomberg / TRM Labs · Information Finance · 2025–26

Prediction markets go mainstream: skin in the game beats the pundit

Kalshi and Polymarket have crossed from novelty to infrastructure. Combined volumes set records through late 2025 (a single week topped $2B for the first time; October cleared $7B+), and industry trackers put monthly volume above $20B heading into 2026. The mainstreaming signal: in November 2025 Google began embedding live Kalshi and Polymarket odds in Search and the revamped Google Finance. Recent raises valued Polymarket and Kalshi at roughly $9B and $5B.

Why it matters beyond gambling: markets aggregate dispersed information because participants are paid to be right and punished for being wrong. Accuracy is measured by Brier score (squared error of probabilistic forecasts) — and calibrated markets have repeatedly tracked outcomes more closely than poll aggregates. The constraint on good forecasting was never a shortage of opinions; it was the absence of a cost for being confidently wrong.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · Theory of Constraints, applied

Find the binding constraint before you optimise anything

Eliyahu Goldratt's old factory insight is the unifying lesson of this week's research: a system's throughput is set by its single tightest bottleneck, and effort spent anywhere else is wasted motion. The skill is diagnostic — locate the real constraint, then work that. Four ways to put it into practice:

  • Name the bottleneck out loud. Before optimising the obvious lever (more compute, more hours, more dashboards), ask: if this doubled tomorrow, would output actually rise? If not, you have found a non-constraint. The constraint is whatever breaks first when you push — for AI in 2026, it is power and the grid, not the chip.
  • Offload mechanics, never judgment. Hand AI the grammar, the boilerplate, the first-draft scaffolding — keep the synthesis, the framing, and the final call. The research line between beneficial and detrimental offloading is exactly here: protect the “desirable difficulty” that builds the skill you are trying to keep.
  • Write your number before you peek. Borrow the prediction-market discipline: commit a probability to a forecast before checking the market or the consensus, then compare. Tracking your own Brier score over time is the cheapest available upgrade to calibrated judgment.
  • Build the eval before you scale the agent. The reliability research is blunt: agents fail in production from missing context and missing evaluation, not weak models. Treat the test harness as infrastructure you build first — not an afterthought once it is already breaking.

Optimising a non-constraint feels productive and changes nothing. The entire return is in correctly identifying which lever is actually binding — a 5-minute diagnostic that beats hours of misdirected effort.

04 On the Horizon
Energy

The grid becomes a geopolitical chokepoint

If interconnection queues, not chip supply, set the pace of AI, then power policy becomes AI policy. Watch for accelerated SMR approvals, “bring-your-own-generation” data-centre campuses, and friction between hyperscaler demand and residential rate-payers — the political economy of who gets the next available megawatt.

AI Work

Evals graduate into a profession

As agents move from answering to executing multi-step work, “reliability science” for long-horizon agents is maturing fast. Expect evaluation, context management, and audit trails to be treated like container orchestration — integrated runtime operations, with dedicated roles to match. The moat shifts from the model to the harness around it.

Mind

The offloading divide widens by age

The sharpest open question in the cognitive research: what AI does to a 45-year-old who already built the skill differs categorically from what it does to a 14-year-old who never will. Selective, deliberate offloading — protecting desirable difficulty on purpose — looks set to become an explicit literacy, not an instinct.

Information Finance

Prediction markets as a real-time truth layer

With Google surfacing odds and volumes north of $20B/month, “information finance” is becoming a default reference price for uncertainty. The next frontier is reflexivity and manipulation resistance — and whether mainstream institutions start citing market-implied probabilities the way they cite poll averages today.
