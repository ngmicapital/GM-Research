When you make an engine more efficient, you burn more fuel — not less

The Jevons Paradox — named 1865 — explains why LEDs haven’t saved electricity, highways haven’t eased congestion, and cheaper AI will devour more power than ever

In 1865, economist William Stanley Jevons proved that making steam engines more efficient caused Britain to burn vastly more coal — not less. His paradox explains why every efficiency revolution since has consumed more resources, not fewer: cheaper access expands demand faster than efficiency saves supply.

TL;DR

In 1865, William Stanley Jevons published a book that horrified Victorian England: making coal engines more efficient wasn’t saving coal — it was causing Britain to burn more of it than ever. The mechanism he identified has never stopped operating. Every major efficiency revolution since — fuel-efficient cars, LED lights, cheaper AI — has produced the same result. Total resource consumption rises.

Section 01

The Story

In 1854, nineteen-year-old William Stanley Jevons left Liverpool for Australia with a modest salary and a large question forming in his mind. His father’s iron-trading business had collapsed in the financial panics of the late 1840s, and Jevons had accepted a position as an assayer at the newly opened Sydney Mint — testing the purity of Australian gold rush ore. It was unglamorous work, but it bought him time. During five years of colonial mornings weighing gold bars and colonial evenings reading political economy, he developed the instincts of a systems thinker: the tendency to ask not just what a change does directly, but what it does to everything connected to it.

He returned to England in 1859 and began publishing papers on economics and formal logic, but coal — the fuel of nearly all British industrial energy — seized his attention. By 1865, a reassuring argument had taken hold in Parliament. Yes, England’s coal reserves were finite. Yes, the country was extracting them at a pace that would someday become a problem. But improved steam engine technology would naturally extend those reserves: as engines became more efficient, each ton of coal would do more work, demand would ease, and the crisis would recede.

“It is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth.”
William Stanley Jevons, The Coal Question, 1865

Jevons disagreed. He pointed to James Watt’s revolutionary steam engine — a design that consumed roughly three-quarters less coal per unit of mechanical work than the Newcomen pumping engines it had displaced. The engineering triumph was real and undeniable. But England had not saved coal. In the decades after Watt’s improvements, the country was burning far more of it than before. Efficiency had not reduced demand. It had unlocked demand that had never previously existed.

The Engine That Proved the Paradox

In 1769, James Watt patented an atmospheric engine with a separate condenser that consumed approximately 75% less fuel than a comparable Newcomen engine to produce the same mechanical work — making it roughly four times as efficient. By 1800, Boulton and Watt had sold nearly 500 of them. Britain’s coal production during this period did not fall. It grew explosively. The engines made steam power affordable for textile mills, ironworks, and eventually railways, opening up industrial applications that Newcomen’s expensive, mine-bound machines could never have served.

Jevons published his findings in The Coal Question in 1865. The book reached Prime Minister William Gladstone, who cited it in Parliament. Jevons, thirty years old, was suddenly famous. He continued writing prolifically — on economics, on logic, on sunspot cycles he believed drove the business cycle (a theory that did not survive) — until a summer afternoon in 1882, when he went bathing near Hastings and did not come back. He was forty-seven. The paradox he had named outlived him by about a century and a half, and is still accumulating evidence.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The paradox operates through a mechanism economists call the rebound effect. When a technology becomes more efficient, it lowers the effective cost of whatever that technology produces. A more fuel-efficient engine makes mechanical work cheaper. Cheaper mechanical work makes it worthwhile to do more mechanical work. The efficiency gain is partially — and sometimes entirely, and occasionally more than entirely — consumed by the behavioral response to the lower price.

The direct rebound is the most intuitive layer: the same user of a more efficient device simply uses it more, because each unit of use now costs less. A household that installs LED lighting keeps the lights on longer and adds fixtures to rooms that were previously unlit. A driver with a more fuel-efficient car takes longer commutes, makes more discretionary trips, and may trade a smaller vehicle for a larger one. Studies consistently find that for personal vehicle transport, somewhere between 10% and 30% of potential fuel savings from efficiency improvements are offset by increased driving — and that is only the direct effect.

Three Layers of Rebound

Economists distinguish at least three levels: (1) Direct — users of an efficient device use it more because it is cheaper to operate. (2) Indirect — savings from efficiency are spent on other goods and services with their own energy footprints. (3) Economy-wide (or “backfire”) — when efficiency gains are large enough to drive economic growth at a pace that consumes more total energy than was saved. The full Jevons Paradox is the third case, and computing over the past fifty years is its cleanest modern example.

There is also the indirect rebound: money saved on one energy cost gets spent elsewhere, and spending has its own energy footprint. A household that saves on heating bills may book a flight. A manufacturing firm that reduces energy costs may reinvest in production capacity. The economy is not a closed box in which efficiency savings stay as savings — they re-enter circulation.

Efficiency is not the enemy of conservation. But it cannot be the mechanism of conservation, because it reliably makes the conserved resource cheaper, and making a resource cheaper reliably causes more of it to be used.

The third level — economy-wide, or “backfire” — is where Jevons actually lives. A 30% improvement in computing efficiency doesn’t just let existing users do the same computations at 30% lower cost. It enables computations that were previously economically impossible. New industries form around cheap computation. New applications emerge. Demand expands to fill the capacity and then some. Total resource consumption is higher than before the efficiency gain, not lower. The key variable is price elasticity: how strongly does demand respond to a price drop? In markets where a resource enables entirely new activities when its price crosses a threshold, the answer can be dramatically large.

Section 03

The Connections

The LED paradox. In 2009, the European Union began phasing out incandescent light bulbs in favour of LEDs that consume roughly 90% less electricity per lumen. Total electricity consumed for lighting across Europe has not fallen by 90%. It has barely moved. LEDs made light so inexpensive that buildings began illuminating previously-dark facades, retailers doubled their window display brightness, and cities expanded street lighting into areas that had been dark. Researchers tracking global light output from satellite data found that the brightness of Earth’s nighttime lights increased by approximately 2.2% per year between 2012 and 2016 — after LEDs were already widespread and cheap.

The 23-lane freeway. The Katy Freeway in Houston was widened to twenty-three lanes at a cost of approximately three billion dollars, completed in 2011 — making it one of the widest highways in the world. The project was designed to ease chronic congestion. Within three years of its completion, commute times were longer than before the expansion. The mechanism is identical to Jevons: cheaper driving (measured in time rather than money) materialised latent demand from commuters who switched from transit, lengthened their commutes to access better jobs, or simply made more trips. Transport economists call this induced demand, and a landmark 2011 study by Gilles Duranton and Matthew Turner found that a 1% increase in lane capacity generates approximately a 1% increase in vehicle kilometres travelled — across decades and metro areas, with remarkable consistency.

The DeepSeek moment. In January 2025, a Chinese AI lab released a reasoning model that matched the capability of leading American AI systems while being dramatically cheaper to run. Nvidia’s share price fell 17% in a single trading day, erasing roughly six hundred billion dollars in market capitalisation. The market’s initial logic: fewer chips required per query means less demand for chips. Then it remembered Jevons. Cheaper, more efficient AI doesn’t reduce compute demand — it lowers the barrier to entry. Tasks that couldn’t justify expensive AI become viable. Applications multiply. Start-ups that had been priced out of LLM integration could now build. Within weeks, analysts had converged on the Jevons-compatible view: total compute demand would rise, not fall.

January 27, 2025

The day Nvidia fell 17% on DeepSeek news — approximately $600B in market value erased in one session. The move was reversed as investors recognised that cheaper AI inference does not reduce total chip demand; it expands the addressable market for AI applications, increasing long-run demand for the compute infrastructure that runs them.

The bandwidth paradox. Broadband internet arrived with connection speeds orders of magnitude higher than dial-up, and the naive prediction was that people would do the same things faster. What actually happened: streaming video, cloud backup, always-on applications, and video calling consumed every bit of that capacity and demanded more. The average American household consumed roughly 20 gigabytes of data per month in 2010. By the mid-2020s, it exceeded 600 gigabytes. Efficiency — in the sense of cheaper, faster transmission — did not reduce bandwidth consumption. It catalysed applications that could not have existed without cheap bandwidth, and those applications defined their own insatiable demand.

The tragic coda. Jevons wrote The Coal Question as a warning that Britain was exhausting its coal reserves. On his own terms, he was right about the mechanism: efficiency increases consumption. But he was wrong about the endpoint. Britain did not run out of coal. New seams were discovered, coal imports began, oil displaced coal for many applications, and nuclear and renewables displaced it for others. The crisis he feared arrived not through scarcity but through a different technology entirely. The man who correctly identified how efficiency paradoxically drives consumption used that correct insight to predict a catastrophe that never happened in the form he imagined. The paradox outlived the problem. It still does.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

🔄
The Rebound Effect Taxonomy

Economists distinguish direct rebounds (using the efficient device more), indirect rebounds (spending savings on other energy-consuming goods), and economy-wide backfire (efficiency drives growth that exceeds the savings). The policy implication: efficiency gains alone cannot reduce total consumption without pricing mechanisms that keep the resource expensive.

🛣️
The Fundamental Law of Road Congestion

Duranton and Turner’s 2011 paper found a near-perfect 1:1 ratio between lane capacity added and vehicle kilometres generated across US metro areas over multiple decades. The result: road-building as congestion relief is self-defeating. The highway expands, new drivers fill it, and commute times return to where they started — or worsen.

💡
The Khazzoom-Brookes Postulate

In the 1980s, economists Leonard Khazzoom and Leonard Brookes independently updated Jevons for the modern energy economy: micro-level efficiency gains are swamped by economy-wide growth effects, meaning policies relying solely on efficiency to reduce national energy use will fail. Controversial then, increasingly well-supported by four decades of data since.

🌍
Borlaug’s Paradox

Norman Borlaug’s high-yield wheat varieties — the Green Revolution — made farming vastly more productive per acre and averted predicted famines. But because farming became more profitable, it also expanded: global agricultural land did not shrink as efficiency rose. More efficient farming made it worthwhile to cultivate land that had previously been marginal.

⚙️
The Policy Escape Hatch

If efficiency alone cannot reduce consumption, what does? Most economists point to pricing: a carbon tax or cap-and-trade system that keeps the resource expensive even as efficiency improves. Efficiency + higher prices can achieve reductions that neither can alone. Jevons himself had no clean answer — he ended The Coal Question recommending only that Britain save money during the boom years.
