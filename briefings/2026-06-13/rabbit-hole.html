The Smell Nobody Could Name Until 1964

How humanity's most universally loved scent went unexplained for millennia — and what decoding it revealed about antibiotics, evolution, and champagne physics

The smell of rain has a name — petrichor — but nobody coined it until 1964, when two Australian scientists discovered it was ancient plant oil escaping from rocks. The bacteria responsible also invented most of our antibiotics.

TL;DR

The smell of rain is called petrichor, a word that didn't exist before 1964. Two CSIRO scientists in Australia identified it as a combination of plant oils stored in dry rock and a compound produced by soil bacteria. Those same bacteria gave us streptomycin, tetracycline, and most of the antibiotics that saved the twentieth century. In 2015, MIT explained how the smell actually becomes airborne: the same physics as a champagne bubble.

Section 01

The Story

Every human being who has ever stood outside as the first drops of rain hit warm dry ground has smelled it. Cultures separated by oceans named it in their languages, built rituals around it, and attempted to capture it in perfume. In India, artisans in the city of Kannauj were baking clay and steam-distilling it through sandalwood oil to bottle the scent — a product called mitti attar, or "earth perfume" — centuries before Western scientists took notice. And yet, for all of recorded history, nobody in the scientific tradition had bothered to ask what the smell actually was.

That changed in 1964, in a laboratory at Australia's Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation (CSIRO). Two researchers, Isabel Joy Bear and R. G. Thomas, published a short paper in the journal Nature under the wonderfully understated title "Nature of Argillaceous Odour" — argillaceous meaning, simply, "clay-like." What they had found was a yellowish oil, extracted by steam-distilling rocks that had been sitting dry in warm conditions. The oil, they showed, came from plant matter — fatty acids and organic compounds secreted by vegetation during dry spells and then absorbed into porous rock and clay. When moisture arrived, the rock released it.

They reached back to Ancient Greek for a name: petra — stone — and ichor, the ethereal fluid said to flow in the veins of the gods. Petrichor. The blood of the stone.

Bear and Thomas were not romantic types, but they understood they had named something universal. The etymology they chose was exact: this was not a manufactured smell but something the earth itself had been storing and releasing since long before anyone was around to notice. The paper barely caused a ripple in 1964. It took fifty years and the internet for petrichor to become a cultural touchstone — the word spread virally in the 2010s, appearing in crossword puzzles, design blogs, and candle descriptions worldwide.

Isabel Joy Bear — CSIRO

Bear joined CSIRO in the 1940s and spent over seventy years there. When journalists rediscovered the petrichor paper in 2015, she was 88 years old and still active at the organisation. Her co-author Thomas had retired before the paper's publication and died in 1974, never knowing the word they invented would one day trend on Twitter.

But the 1964 paper only answered half the question. Bear and Thomas had identified the plant-oil component. There was a second smell hiding inside petrichor — earthier, deeper, more insistent — and it came from somewhere else entirely.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The second molecule in petrichor is called geosmin. It is a sesquiterpenoid — a class of organic compound built from isoprene units — and it is produced not by plants but by bacteria: specifically by Streptomyces, a genus of filamentous microbes that live in virtually every handful of soil on the planet. Streptomyces produce geosmin when conditions shift from dry to wet, as a signal during the process of forming spores. The smell is, in a real sense, the bacteria announcing that they are about to travel.

What makes geosmin remarkable is the concentration at which humans can detect it. In water, the threshold is roughly 5 to 15 parts per trillion — the equivalent of a single teaspoon dissolved in roughly 200 Olympic swimming pools. This is not a minor sensitivity. It places humans among the most acute detectors of any single molecule in the natural world. For comparison, sharks detect blood in seawater at around one part per million. The numbers are not directly comparable (different molecules, different media, different sensory machinery), but the scale suggests something worth paying attention to: we are far better at smelling geosmin than sharks are at smelling blood.

OR11A1 — The Receptor, Identified 2024

In 2024, researchers at the Leibniz Institute published a study in the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry identifying the specific human olfactory receptor responsible for detecting geosmin: OR11A1. Of 616 human olfactory receptor variants tested, it was the only one that responded to physiologically relevant geosmin concentrations. It is also described as one of the most highly expressed receptors in the human nasal lining — not a vestige, but a prominently maintained sensor.

But knowing the source of the smell still left an open question: how does it actually get into the air? Rain hits soil — but geosmin and petrichor oil are bound up in the ground. Something has to launch them. In 2015, a team of mechanical engineers at MIT answered this using high-speed cameras filming raindrops at 5,000 frames per second. What they found was the same physics that makes champagne effervescent.

When a raindrop strikes a porous surface, it momentarily flattens on impact and traps tiny air pockets beneath it — exactly like a raindrop landing on a glass of sparkling wine. Those air pockets form bubbles inside the droplet. The bubbles rise, reach the surface of the droplet, and burst, ejecting hundreds of microscopic aerosol particles into the air per raindrop, in a matter of microseconds. Those aerosols carry geosmin, petrichor oil, and whatever else happens to be living in the top layer of soil — bacteria, viruses, spores, organic fragments — and deliver them into the air we breathe.

The MIT team's counterintuitive finding: light rain produces more petrichor than heavy rain. At high impact velocities, raindrops flatten too fast for bubbles to form. The gentle shower that opens a storm is doing the most chemical work.

This also explains why the smell is strongest at the very beginning of rain — and why, within a few minutes of heavy downpour, it fades. The top layer of soil is saturated, bubbles can no longer form efficiently, and the aerosol launch mechanism shuts down. Petrichor is not rain's smell. It is rain's opening act.

Section 03

The Connections

The antibiotic factory beneath your feet. The same genus of bacteria that produces geosmin — Streptomyces — is responsible for the majority of antibiotics in clinical use. Streptomycin, discovered in 1943 and the first drug to effectively treat tuberculosis. Erythromycin, tetracycline, chloramphenicol. Over two-thirds of all naturally derived antibiotics used in medicine trace back to Streptomyces species. Geosmin is a secondary metabolite — a compound the bacteria produce not for energy or growth but for ecological signalling. So are antibiotics. The same complex biochemical machinery that weaponised soil chemistry against bacterial competitors also, incidentally, produces the smell of rain. Every time you inhale petrichor, you are smelling the exhaust of the organism that built modern medicine.

Why we are so absurdly good at smelling it. The leading evolutionary hypothesis is that our extreme sensitivity to geosmin — that 5 parts per trillion threshold — evolved because detecting it at long range helped our ancestors locate water. Streptomyces activates in wet soil; detecting that activation from a distance, before you could see or hear rain, would have been life-saving information in a dry landscape. The hypothesis is supported by geography: the kangaroo rat, a desert-adapted rodent, is roughly 100 times more sensitive to geosmin than humans. Organisms that evolved in arid environments where finding water was a survival constraint seem to have been under stronger selection pressure for this exact ability.

The ecology of the smell. Streptomyces don't produce geosmin just for humans to enjoy. A 2020 study in Nature Microbiology found that springtails — tiny soil arthropods whose ancestors diverged from insects around 500 million years ago — are strongly attracted to geosmin and are specifically recruited by it to carry Streptomyces spores to new locations. The bacteria are, in effect, using the smell as a courier signal. The convergent evolution of geosmin sensitivity across mammals, insects, and arthropods — all independently tuned to the same molecule — suggests it functions as one of the most broadly used chemical signals in terrestrial ecology.

The problem of too much. Geosmin's extraordinary detectability is a double-edged sword. Water treatment plants fight a constant battle against it: when Streptomyces or cyanobacteria bloom in reservoirs, even tiny concentrations make tap water taste and smell musty. It appears in wine — the "earthy rot" character of certain bottles comes from geosmin-producing mould on grapes damaged by rain. And it creates a challenge for perfumers: because the human nose detects it at parts-per-trillion concentrations, any formula containing it must be extraordinarily precise. A fraction too much and the fragrance tips from "petrichor" into "damp basement."

Mitti Attar — Kannauj, India

For centuries before scientists arrived, perfumers in Kannauj — a city in Uttar Pradesh historically known as the perfume capital of India — produced mitti attar ("earth perfume") by baking dry clay at high temperature and then hydro-distilling it, condensing the steam through sandalwood oil as a fixative. The process takes weeks. The result is petrichor in a bottle, captured through entirely artisanal means with no knowledge of geosmin, no spectrometers, no aerosol mechanics — just accumulated craft knowledge that the smell could be extracted from baked earth. Bear and Thomas gave the chemistry its name. The Kannauj perfumers had the product on the shelf five hundred years earlier.

The aerosol dimension. The MIT finding that petrichor travels as a microscopic aerosol burst has implications beyond aesthetics. The same mechanism that launches geosmin into the air also launches bacteria, viruses, and spores from the soil surface. Researchers have noted this as a plausible transmission pathway for soil-borne pathogens during light rainfall — the kind of gentle rain that maximises aerosol production. The smell of rain is, in a literal sense, the smell of the soil's contents being suspended in the air around you. Most of those contents are harmless or beneficial. The mechanism itself is indiscriminate.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

🫧
The Champagne Physics of Rain

The MIT aerosol paper draws a direct parallel to sparkling wine: bubbles trapped under an impacting drop behave exactly like CO2 bubbles in champagne. The same fluid dynamics paper that explained petrichor is also a study in why fizzy drinks smell when you pour them.

🦔
Springtails and the Spore Courier Network

Springtails — 0.5mm soil invertebrates — navigate by geosmin toward Streptomyces colonies, eat the bacteria, and carry spores away on their bodies. This is a 500-million-year-old dispersal deal. The smell of rain is partly the bacteria calling for their delivery service.

💊
Streptomyces: The Antibiotic Genus

Over 120 clinically used antibiotics trace back to Streptomyces species. The genus evolved an extraordinary capacity for secondary metabolite chemistry — the same toolkit that makes geosmin also makes drugs. Soil is the original pharmaceutical library.

🏺
Mitti Attar and Kannauj

India's mitti attar tradition predates the science by centuries. Kannauj artisans bake earth, steam-distil it through sandalwood, and bottle the result — a practice documented in Mughal-era texts. Today mitti attar is exported globally as a niche fragrance ingredient and cultural artifact.

🌵
Desert Amplification

Geosmin sensitivity correlates with aridity: the kangaroo rat detects it at ~100x lower concentrations than humans. Peoples living in historically arid regions report petrichor as one of the most emotionally powerful smells they know — the promise of water in a landscape that offers none.
