Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

This week's newsletters converge on one move: the highest-leverage gains are internal, not external. Mark Manson reframes attention as a "diet of the mind" you give away rather than have stolen — set against Gloria Mark's data showing average focus on a screen has collapsed from 2.5 minutes in 2004 to 47 seconds today; Morgan Housel argues reducing your needs beats chasing returns because it is more certain and in your control; and James Clear's "three selves" framework gains empirical teeth from Hal Hershfield's research on future-self continuity.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Newsletter · Mark Manson · "You Gave Away Your Attention" · Jun 11, 2026

Your Attention Isn't Being Stolen — You're Giving It Away Without a Fight

Mark Manson opens his weekly note with a confession that lands because it is so ordinary: in 2007 he could "roll out of bed and bang out a 1,000-word article before breakfast"; by 2018 he was "trapped in a digital hellscape" where creating anything meaningful felt "attentionally impossible." His diagnosis is the part worth keeping. The popular framing — your attention is being stolen — casts you as a victim of mega-corporations. Manson rejects it: "your attention isn't being stolen — you're just giving it away without a fight." The governing metaphor is diet. Push notifications and memes are to the mind what Doritos and Mountain Dew are to the body: engineered, moreish, and corrosive in proportion to how much you consume. "Your life is nothing more than the diet of your mind."

The reframe matters because it relocates the lever. A stolen thing requires someone else to give it back; a thing you give away, you can simply stop giving. But the scale of the problem is not anecdotal, and this is where the research sharpens Manson's point. Gloria Mark, an informatics professor at UC Irvine who has measured screen attention for two decades, documents a collapse: average attention on a single screen ran about 2.5 minutes in 2004, fell to 75 seconds by 2012, and now sits at roughly 47 seconds — a figure corroborated by five independent studies between 2014 and 2020. Worse, the cost of a single interruption is not the interruption itself: Mark finds it takes an average of 23 minutes and 15 seconds to fully return to the original task, and that fragmentation drives measurable spikes in stress and cognitive load.

"Stolen" makes you a plaintiff; "given away" makes you an agent. The 47-second number is not a reason for despair — it is the size of the prize for anyone willing to design their environment instead of negotiating with it in the moment.

Newsletter · James Clear, quoting Morgan Housel · 3-2-1 · Jun 11, 2026

The Untapped Alpha Is Lifestyle Bloat, Not a Better Return

James Clear's 3-2-1 this week carried a long passage from Morgan Housel's essay "The Biggest Returns" that reframes a question most people get backwards. We pour effort into one side of the financial equation — returns — and almost none into the other. Housel's framing: "There are investors who grind 80 hours a week to add 10 basis points to their returns when there are two or three full percentage points of lifestyle bloat in their finances that can be exploited with less effort." The asymmetry is the whole point. Increasing income and reducing needs can have the same effect on your balance sheet, but reducing needs is more certain and more within your control — which makes it the higher-expected-value move for almost everyone.

Crucially, Housel insists this "is not about living like a monk, hampered by frugality." It holds at every level of wealth: "The value of all money is relative to expectations." Someone who needs half as much to be happy is, in a real sense, wealthier than someone earning higher returns whose lifestyle compounds as fast as their assets. The lever is not the market; it is the expectations you bring to it.

Outperformance is scarce, effortful, and partly luck. Sufficiency is abundant, controllable, and quietly compounding. Most people optimise the variable they can't control and ignore the one they can.

"What makes people happy is having options — doing what you want, with who you want, when you want, where you want." — Morgan Housel, "The Biggest Returns," Collab Fund
Newsletter · James Clear · 3-2-1 · Jun 11, 2026 + Research

Forgiving Past, Strict Present, Flexible Future: A Self-Management Algorithm With Empirical Teeth

The sharpest idea in Clear's issue is a three-line rule for how to treat your own selves across time: "Be forgiving with your past self. What's done is done… Be strict with your present self. Win the moment in front of you right now… Be flexible with your future self. There are many paths to success." Guilt over the past and rigidity about the future are both wasted motion; the only self you can act on is the present one.

What makes this more than a fridge magnet is that the "future self" half is one of the most replicated findings in behavioural science. Hal Hershfield (UCLA Anderson) has shown that future self-continuity — how connected you feel to the person you'll become — predicts real outcomes: people with higher continuity discount future rewards less and accumulate more wealth over time. The interventions are concrete. Showing people age-progressed renderings of their own face increases how much they allocate to retirement; having them write a letter to their self 20 years out measurably increased exercise in the following days. Clear says be flexible with your future self; Hershfield shows the prior step is to make that self real enough to bargain with at all.

The present self always wins the negotiation because the future self isn't in the room. Continuity work — renderings, letters, vivid specifics — is how you give that future person a seat at the table.

Newsletter · Mark Manson · "The Hidden Fee of Boredom" · Jun 8, 2026

Everything That Matters Most Will Sometimes Bore You — That's the Fee, Not a Mistake

Manson's earlier note this week is the quiet companion to the attention piece. "Everything that matters most in life will sometimes bore you. Your marriage will bore you. Your career will bore you. Your kids will bore you." The instinct is to read boredom as evidence of a wrong choice. Manson's correction: "boredom isn't a sign that you chose wrong. It's a signal that you have chosen to make something more important than your own momentary pleasure. It's the hidden fee that comes with purpose and meaning."

This closes the loop with the attention argument. The same reflex that makes the brain "shop for exits" when a task turns tedious — new tab, phone, anything — is the reflex that makes us flee meaningful-but-boring commitments for the next hit of novelty. Manson's diagnostic questions are worth sitting with: "Where in your life are you chasing excitement instead of depth? What have you stayed with long enough for it to become meaningful?" Depth, by definition, is on the far side of boredom.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · Manson + Gloria Mark

Build an Attention Diet: Treat Inputs Like Food, Default to Friction

If attention is a diet, then willpower-in-the-moment is the wrong tool — you don't out-discipline a pantry full of junk, you change what's in the pantry. The leverage is environmental and decided in advance.

  • Audit the menu. For one day, log what you consume — feeds, notifications, tabs — and label each as nourishing or junk. You can't change a diet you haven't seen.
  • Default to friction. Manson's own protocol is blunt: phone left at home, everything blocked on the laptop, and the willingness "to sit in painful, empty space before I can write." Add friction to junk, remove it from the work.
  • Protect recovery, not just focus. One interruption costs ~23 minutes to fully recover (Mark). Batch interruptions into windows rather than sprinkling them through deep work — the recovery tax, not the interruption, is what bankrupts the day.
  • Sit with the empty space. The discomfort before focus is the entry fee, not a malfunction. Reaching for the exit is the habit; staying is the skill.
Practice · Morgan Housel

Run a Sufficiency Audit Before You Chase a Single Basis Point

Before optimising the return side of the ledger, audit the needs side — it's where the larger, more certain gains hide. This is a decision discipline, not a vow of frugality.

  • Find your actual number. How much do you genuinely need to be content? Lowering it has the same effect as a raise — with none of the uncertainty.
  • Hunt the bloat first. Look for the "two or three percentage points" of lifestyle creep hiding in your expenses before grinding for ten basis points of outperformance you may never capture.
  • Manage expectations, not just income. "The value of all money is relative to expectations." Expectations are the one input fully under your control — treat them as a position you actively manage.
  • Buy options, not status. Housel's measure of wealth is optionality — doing what you want, with whom, when, where. Spend toward freedom of action, not toward a higher fixed cost base.
Practice · Hershfield + Clear

Write the Letter: Make Your Future Self a Real Person

The future self loses every negotiation because it isn't in the room. The fix isn't more discipline; it's making that person vivid enough to count.

  • Write to your 20-years-out self. Hershfield's letter intervention measurably increased follow-through on health behaviour. Describe the life you're trying to hand them — specifically, in their voice.
  • Strict present, flexible future. Hold this week's commitments firmly; hold the exact shape of the destination loosely. There are many paths; today's action is the only one you control.
  • Apply Clear's sorting question. Before spending energy on a worry, ask: "Is this a problem you need to solve, or a tension you need to learn to live with?" Solve the first; stop re-litigating the second.

The three practices share a structure: each replaces an in-the-moment fight you tend to lose with a decision you make once, in advance, when you're clear-headed.

The empirical backbone of the attention thread. Mark's two decades of screen-tracking data — the slide to 47 seconds, the 23-minute interruption-recovery cost — plus a practical model for restoring focus by managing rhythm and "attentional resources" rather than brute willpower. Subtitle: A Groundbreaking Way to Restore Balance, Happiness and Productivity.

The science behind Clear's "flexible future self." Hershfield (UCLA Anderson) lays out future self-continuity — why we treat our future selves like strangers, and the concrete interventions (age-progression, letters, vivid specifics) that close the gap. Subtitle: How to Make Tomorrow Better Today.

04 On the Horizon
AI

Attention Becomes the Scarce Asset as Content Goes Infinite

Generative AI is driving the marginal cost of plausible content toward zero, which floods every feed faster than any human filter was built for. As supply explodes, the bottleneck skill flips from finding information to refusing it. The premium moves from the consumer to the editor — the person who can decide, fast and on purpose, what not to read. Manson's "diet of the mind" stops being self-help and starts being a core competency.

Finance

Sufficiency as the Un-Sexy Alpha in a Returns-Obsessed Culture

Housel's lever — reduce your needs, manage expectations — runs directly against a financial culture that sells outperformance. Expect "enough" to keep gaining quiet ground as an explicit strategy (the FIRE movement's more durable half), especially as returns normalise and the gap between effort spent chasing yield and value left on the table in lifestyle bloat becomes harder to ignore.

Work

Future-Self Tech Migrates From the Lab to Your Retirement App

Hershfield's age-progression and letter-writing interventions began as VR lab studies; the nudge is now industrialising into fintech, retirement platforms, and health apps that render an older "you" to push present-day saving and behaviour. Watch the ethics catch up: a tool that makes your future self vivid to help you save is the same tool that makes you easier to persuade.

Mind

Boredom Tolerance Becomes a Trainable, Premium Skill

When the exit is always one tap away, the capacity to stay with the boring-but-meaningful — the marriage, the craft, the four-hour problem — becomes both rarer and more valuable. Expect "boredom tolerance" and deliberate under-stimulation (walks without earbuds, single-tasking, scheduled dullness) to keep moving from fringe practice toward mainstream performance advice.
