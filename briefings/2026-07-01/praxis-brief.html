Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

The discomfort you're trying to minimise on the way to a goal is not overhead — it is the actual mechanism producing the goal, and this week two Farnam Street Brain Food issues plus decades of expertise research converge on exactly that claim from different angles. Brain Food's June 28 issue uses skateboarder Danny Way to argue that repeated, painful failure is not a tax paid en route to flow states, it is the substrate flow states are built from. The June 21 issue catches something adjacent: people say they want less effort, but audited honestly, most don't want less work if it means less achievement — the complaint is about visible cost, not actual preference. Anders Ericsson's deliberate-practice research, popularised by Angela Duckworth's Grit, gives the mechanical explanation for why: deliberate practice only produces skill gain when it operates at the edge of current ability, meaning by definition it must feel effortful and slightly aversive, or it has stopped working. Put together, these aren't three motivational takes on hard work — they're three independent descriptions of the same design feature: the discomfort is not a signal that something has gone wrong with your approach, it is confirmation that your approach is calibrated correctly.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Newsletter · Farnam Street · “Brain Food No. 687: The Game of Failure” · Jun 28, 2026

Failure isn't the price of the flow state — it's the substrate it's built from

Farnam Street's June 28 issue leans on professional skateboarder Danny Way to make a claim sharper than the usual “embrace failure” platitude: skateboarding at the highest level is, structurally, a game of failure — weeks of repeated, physically punishing attempts that only occasionally resolve into the breakthrough moments where, as the newsletter quotes him, “everything goes silent” and “time slows down.” The newsletter's framing is that this isn't survivable collateral damage on the way to mastery. The failures are the training signal. Remove them and the skill plateaus.

The issue pairs this with Rory McIlroy's process-over-outcome framing — golf's version of the same idea, that “the outcome will ultimately happen if you just focus on the process” — and a harder-edged “Tiny Thought”: “Self-pity feels like medicine and works like poison.” Read together, the issue is making a specific claim, not a general one: comfort-seeking around failure (self-pity, avoidance, premature pivoting) doesn't just fail to help — it actively degrades the very repetition that produces the breakthrough.

Note on sourcing: Farnam Street's newsletter presents the Way and McIlroy lines as curated quotes without citing the original interview or documentary; they're accurately reproduced here as the newsletter's own curation, not independently re-verified against a primary transcript.

The flow state isn't the reward that comes after the failures stop. It's what repeated failure, tolerated long enough, eventually produces. Trying to get to flow without the failures is trying to get to the destination by skipping the road.

Newsletter · Farnam Street · “Brain Food No. 686: Pressure is Energy” · Jun 21, 2026

You don't actually want less effort — you want the effort to stop being visible

The June 21 issue opens with a sharper diagnosis than it first appears: “People desire outcomes, not the effort required to achieve them. Once success arrives, effort becomes visible — and that's when complaints emerge.” Farnam Street illustrates it with a successful acquaintance who has spent years complaining about being overextended. Audited honestly, he doesn't want less work if it means less achievement — the complaint is about the cost being visible now that the outcome has arrived, not a genuine preference for a lighter load.

The issue's anchor quote, from freeskier Eileen Gu, reframes the felt experience directly: “Pressure is just energy. It's something that can be harnessed and used to push you to new heights.” Paired with Howard Thurman's “ask what makes you come alive” and — notably — a Jim Simons line on craftsmanship (“Be guided by beauty… doing it right… it's a beautiful thing to do something right”), the issue assembles three different domains — sport, vocation, and quantitative finance — all describing pressure and effort as fuel rather than friction.

The convergence with the Way/McIlroy issue one week earlier is not a coincidence of editorial calendar. It's the same underlying claim seen from the complaint side rather than the performance side: what people call “too much pressure” is frequently just the achievement itself, still attached to its true cost, before the mind has finished re-labelling that cost as normal.

The complaint about effort almost never resolves by removing the effort — because the effort and the outcome are the same object viewed from two moments in time. What people actually want is amnesia about the cost, not a lower cost.

Research · Anders Ericsson & Angela Duckworth · deliberate practice · 1993 / 2016

Deliberate practice only works if it feels bad — that's not a side effect, it's the definition

Where the two Brain Food issues describe the pattern, K. Anders Ericsson's deliberate-practice research explains the mechanism. Ericsson, Krampe, and Tesch-Römer's 1993 Psychological Review paper — the foundational study behind the modern “deliberate practice” concept — defines it as training that takes place outside the learner's current ability, requiring “near-maximal effort” on tasks that are just beyond what the person can already do. Angela Duckworth's Grit (Scribner, 2016), which popularised this research for a general audience in its “Practice” chapter, states the implication plainly: practice that doesn't feel effortful and slightly aversive has, by definition, stopped operating at the edge of ability — and has therefore stopped producing skill gain.

This is the piece the two newsletter issues gesture at without naming: the discomfort in Way's repeated falls and in Gu's “pressure as energy” is not an unfortunate emotional tax that a better mindset would eliminate. It is the operational signature of training that is actually working. A version of “deliberate practice” that felt comfortable the whole way through would, on Ericsson's own definition, no longer be deliberate practice — it would just be repetition of what the learner can already do.

The reframe this produces is precise, not motivational: discomfort is diagnostic, not incidental. Its presence indicates you are still at the edge of your ability. Its absence — comfortable, effortless repetition — indicates the training has stopped working, whatever the volume of hours logged.

This is the week's convergence stated plainly: Way's falls, Gu's pressure, and Ericsson's “near-maximal effort” are the same finding from sport, from a general-audience newsletter, and from forty years of expertise research. The discomfort is not the price of the gain. It is how you can tell the gain is actually happening.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · drawn from Ericsson/Duckworth's deliberate practice research

Audit your discomfort instead of trying to eliminate it

If discomfort is diagnostic rather than incidental, the useful question when something feels hard isn't “how do I make this feel easier” — it's “is this the right kind of hard.” Here's how to run that audit this week.

  • Name the task you've been avoiding because it feels aversive. Before assuming the discomfort means something is wrong, check whether it's the productive kind: are you attempting something just beyond your current ability, or something you already know how to do but dislike doing?
  • Apply Ericsson's edge-of-ability test. If you can complete the task at your current skill level without strain, the discomfort is not deliberate-practice discomfort — it's just aversion, and it's fair to look for a way around it. If you genuinely cannot yet do it well, the discomfort is the signal the training is calibrated correctly. Don't smooth it away.
  • Track discomfort, not just hours. Log a single note after each practice session: did this feel like effortless repetition, or near-maximal effort at the edge of what I can do? Ericsson's research says only the second kind produces skill gain. If a week of sessions all felt easy, the difficulty needs raising, not the schedule.
  • Re-run Farnam Street's effort audit on one current complaint. Pick something you've been complaining about being “too much”. Ask honestly: would you actually accept less of this if it meant a smaller version of the outcome attached to it? If not, the complaint is about visible cost, not real preference — and the fix is reframing, not reducing.

Most self-improvement advice treats discomfort as a problem to solve. This audit treats it as a reading to take — the number that tells you whether the training is actually working.

Practice · drawn from Danny Way (Brain Food No. 687) and Eileen Gu (Brain Food No. 686)

Build a failure quota, not just a goal

Way's skateboarding and Gu's freeskiing both operate on volume of attempts, not certainty of outcome. Translate that into a weekly practice that treats failed attempts as the actual unit of progress rather than a regrettable side effect.

  • Set a failure quota, not a success target. For whatever skill you're building, pick a number of attempts you expect to fail at this week — not a number you expect to succeed at. If you're not hitting the failure quota, you're not training at the edge of your ability.
  • Separate the process check from the outcome check. Borrowing McIlroy's framing, review each session on whether you executed the process correctly, independent of whether the attempt succeeded. Outcome-only review teaches you to avoid the attempts most likely to fail — which are usually the ones training the skill.
  • Relabel pressure before the session, not after. Gu's “pressure is just energy” works as a pre-performance reframe, not a post-hoc rationalisation. Before a high-stakes attempt, name the physical sensation of pressure explicitly as fuel, out loud or in writing, rather than waiting to see how the attempt goes.

Framework

The Edge-of-Ability Test

A one-question filter for any practice session: can I already do this reasonably well? If yes, repeating it is comfortable but not deliberate practice — it maintains skill rather than building it. If no, and it takes near-maximal effort, the discomfort is doing its job. Use it before deciding whether a hard session needs easing off or needs to stay exactly as hard as it is.

04 On the Horizon
Mind

Comfort-optimised self-improvement culture is on a collision course with deliberate-practice research

A large share of popular wellness and productivity advice treats discomfort as a symptom to be minimised — smoother habits, gentler goals, more self-compassion. Ericsson's research says a meaningful subset of that discomfort is the training signal itself, and removing it removes the skill gain along with it. Expect a sharper, more visible split between advice aimed at feeling better and advice aimed at getting better, with more writers this year forced to explicitly pick a side rather than blend the two into vague “balance” language.

Work

“Friction audits” will need a second column: productive friction vs. waste

Organisations are increasingly running friction audits to strip out anything that slows work down. The Ericsson/Duckworth distinction — comfortable repetition vs. near-maximal effort at the edge of ability — implies some of that friction is exactly where skill is being built, and stripping it out for the sake of throughput will quietly flatten the learning curve of people doing the work. The more useful audit separates friction that wastes time from friction that is, structurally, the point.

Finance

The Simons “guided by beauty” standard is a harder due-diligence bar than it sounds

Simons's aesthetic standard for “doing it right” is easy to read as a soft, almost decorative philosophy. Read against this week's thesis, it's the opposite: a process-quality bar that has to survive the discomfort of being wrong repeatedly before a model earns trust. As more capital chases systematic and AI-driven strategies in the second half of 2026, the operators who last will be the ones who kept the process-quality bar even through the drawdowns that made keeping it uncomfortable — not the ones who relaxed the bar the moment outcomes turned favourable.

Health

Training programs that eliminate all discomfort will plateau by design, not by bad luck

The same edge-of-ability logic applies directly to physical training. Programs (and increasingly AI-driven coaching apps) that auto-adjust difficulty downward the moment a session feels hard are optimising for adherence at the direct cost of the near-maximal-effort stimulus that produces adaptation. Expect a coming correction in fitness-tech marketing: from “never feel discouraged” language toward products that explicitly track and defend time spent at the uncomfortable edge, because that is the metric that actually predicts outcomes.
