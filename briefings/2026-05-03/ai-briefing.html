Research Radar

Andreoletti's March 31 paper provides the first formal proof that Transformer-based forecasts of financial time series under squared loss are bounded by an irreducible variance term proportional to model expressivity — a result that directly invalidates the marketing premise of every "AI predicts crypto prices" product.

Forecast collapse of transformer-based models under squared loss in financial time series — Andreoletti, arXiv 2604.00064

ResearchAndreolettiTransformer limitsTrading

Pierre Andreoletti, "Forecast collapse of transformer-based models under squared loss in financial time series," arXiv 2604.00064, March 31, 2026. The paper studies trajectory forecasting under squared loss for time series with weak conditional structure (i.e., low signal-to-noise ratio — exactly the regime that describes daily crypto price returns). The formal result: in this regime, the conditional expectation of future trajectories is effectively degenerate, meaning the Bayes-optimal predictor is flat for prices and zero for returns. Highly expressive prediction models (like Transformers with many parameters) do not improve predictive accuracy in this regime; instead, increased expressivity introduces spurious trajectory fluctuations around the optimal predictor, arising from the reuse of noise — these manifest as prediction variance without any reduction in prediction bias. The paper provides a process-level explanation for the consistently disappointing performance of Transformer-based forecasts on financial data observed in the empirical literature.

⚡ Implication in 6 months: Direct relevance to dc's crypto-trading work. Three concrete takeaways. (1) Any "AI predicts crypto prices" product marketed to retail traders is selling against a now-formally-proven negative result; the underlying claim is bounded by the SNR of the input data, not the parameter count of the model. (2) The Wyckoff approach dc uses survives this critique cleanly — Wyckoff doesn't try to predict prices under squared loss; it identifies regime-changes (accumulation, distribution, SoS) using volume-and-context signals that don't fall under the Andreoletti theorem's scope. (3) AI-DePIN tokens that pitch "decentralised compute for AI prediction models" implicitly assume those models will work — the Andreoletti result narrows the addressable market for that specific use case, though it doesn't touch other AI workloads (rendering, training, inference) that don't depend on financial prediction.

Technicals

Adaptive test-time compute allocation — models that decide whether a question is easy or hard before allocating reasoning budget — is the architectural shift moving from research to production this quarter, and it's why API pricing for "easy questions" can fall while "hard questions" stay expensive.

Adaptive test-time compute allocation — production deployments now decide reasoning budget per query

UpdateTest-time computeAdaptive routingArchitecture

The latest test-time compute literature has converged on adaptive allocation: models include a complexity assessment mechanism that classifies each query as easy or hard, and only triggers deep reasoning chains when the assessment says it's needed. The empirical results are consistent across multiple papers: optimal performance scales monotonically with compute budget for a given model, but no single test-time scaling strategy universally dominates across problem types. This means production deployments are now running adaptive routers in front of the reasoning model — easy questions get a single forward pass, hard questions get multi-step reasoning with verification. The economic effect: per-token cost diverges from per-query cost. Anthropic's "extended thinking" mode and OpenAI's o-series reasoning gating are both implementations of this pattern; the underlying research is now broad enough to call this a standard production architecture rather than a frontier-lab experiment.

⚡ Implication in 3–6 months: Per-token API pricing will become an increasingly weak signal of actual workload cost — the relevant metric is per-query cost weighted by query difficulty mix. For the briefings harness specifically: the simple ticker-quote calls are essentially free under adaptive routing (one forward pass), while the legal-brief synthesis is expensive (multi-step reasoning). The cost difference will widen, not narrow, as adaptive routing matures. Watch for Anthropic to introduce a per-query pricing tier (rather than per-token) for the Sonnet/Opus tiers in H2 2026.

Latent-reasoning recurrent depth — alternative to chain-of-thought for test-time scaling

ResearchRecurrent depthLatent reasoningArchitecture

A separate test-time-scaling thread: latent-reasoning recurrent depth (Geiping et al., recent arXiv) — an architecture that scales test-time compute by iterating a recurrent transformer block in latent space rather than producing more output tokens. A 3.5B-parameter proof-of-concept trained on 800B tokens shows the model can use varying levels of test-time compute and improves materially on math and coding tasks with additional iterations. The key distinction from chain-of-thought: the reasoning happens inside the model's hidden state, not in visible output tokens — there's no "thinking trace" to read, but the compute is spent the same way. This is the architectural alternative to OpenAI's o-series style reasoning, and several Chinese-lab models (Kimi K2.5 included) are believed to implement variants of it.

⚡ Implication in 6 months: Latent-reasoning architectures are cheaper per equivalent reasoning step than visible chain-of-thought because they don't pay the token-generation cost. If they reach competitive quality with the visible-CoT approach (current evidence: roughly comparable on math/code, less verifiable for safety), the inference economics tilt toward latent reasoning for any application that doesn't need to expose the reasoning trace. For dc's briefings work, the trace is genuinely useful (it's how the QA pass verifies citations), so the visible-CoT approach is preferable — but for ticker-data parsing or pattern-matching tasks, latent reasoning would be cheaper.

🔬 Plain-English Explainer — why the Andreoletti paper matters for anyone using AI to predict crypto prices

Imagine you're trying to predict tomorrow's BTC price. The honest answer to "what is tomorrow's price likely to be?" is very close to today's price — that's the Bayes-optimal prediction when the data has very little signal hidden in a lot of noise (which crypto returns do). A simple model that just outputs "today's price" gets a low error score. Now you train a Transformer with billions of parameters on the same data. You'd hope it does better. The Andreoletti paper proves that under the standard squared-loss training objective, it cannot — and worse, the bigger the model, the more it "hallucinates" tiny squiggles around the correct flat prediction. Those squiggles look impressive to a human eye reading a chart, but they're just noise the model has memorised and re-emitted. The error doesn't go down; it stays the same or rises slightly because the variance has gone up without the bias going down. For traders, this matters in three ways. First, every "buy our AI signal service for $99/month" product on social media is selling against a result that's now mathematically settled — the only way to escape this conclusion is to use a different loss function or operate on a higher-SNR data regime (which is what regime-detection methods like Wyckoff actually do). Second, this is why you can't just throw more parameters at the problem — the failure isn't computational, it's information-theoretic. Third, AI does have edge in trading, but it's in regime-classification, signal extraction from order-flow microstructure, and feature engineering — not in trying to forecast tomorrow's close as a regression target. The paper is a useful filter for how to evaluate any AI-trading product in 2026: ask what loss function it's optimising and what the SNR of its inputs is. If the answer is "we're predicting log-returns under MSE," walk away.
