TL;DR

On January 30, 1962, three girls at a missionary boarding school in Kashasha, Tanganyika — what is now Tanzania — began laughing uncontrollably. The laughter spread to 95 of the school's 159 pupils and could not be stopped. It lasted for days. The school closed. Students returned home to their villages and the laughter went with them, jumping from community to community, school to school, until roughly 1,000 people had been affected and 14 schools had shut their doors. It ran for 18 months. No infectious agent was ever found. No one died. The symptoms — which included not just laughter but crying, pain, fainting, rashes, and respiratory distress — were completely real. The best theory researchers have developed is that Tanganyika's newly independent population, particularly its schoolgirls caught between missionary discipline and collapsing traditional structures, was under a particular kind of collective stress that the nervous system converted into the one release valve available: an epidemic of laughter that nobody could explain and nobody could stop.

Section 01

The Story: Three Girls, One School, and a Laugh That Would Not Stop

Tanganyika had just become independent. The British had governed the territory since the end of World War I, first under a League of Nations mandate, then under United Nations trusteeship, and in December 1961 — barely six weeks before the outbreak — the territory had achieved full independence under Julius Nyerere. It was a moment of enormous national pride and equally enormous uncertainty. Colonial structures were gone, but the institutions built on them — schools, hospitals, administrative systems — were still there, still operating largely on their original terms, staffed by missionaries and civil servants who hadn't quite left yet.

The Kashasha mission school sat on the western shore of Lake Victoria in the Bukoba District, run by the White Fathers, a Catholic missionary order. It was a boarding school for girls aged twelve to eighteen, which meant girls away from their families, operating under strict institutional discipline, in a country whose social order had just shifted beneath them. Parents and teachers, swept up in the energy of independence, had raised their expectations of what students could and would achieve. The girls were caught between the conservatism of their home communities and the new demands of an institution that expected more of them than any generation before them had been expected to deliver.

On January 30, 1962, three girls began laughing.

What happened next was not the ordinary contagion of a joke or a mood. The laughter was uncontrollable. It lasted minutes, then hours. It spread. Within days, other girls were laughing too — and crying, and reporting pain, and fainting. Teachers were unaffected. Adults who came to help were unaffected. The affliction moved through the student body along social lines: those with stronger friendships were more likely to catch it; girls who spent time near affected students were more likely to develop symptoms than those who kept their distance.

What the Symptoms Actually Were

Laughter and crying: The primary and most visible symptom — uncontrolled, involuntary, lasting from minutes to hours. Observers noted it looked more like distress than amusement.

Pain: Students reported pain during episodes, particularly abdominal. This was not the comfortable laughter of a funny moment.

Fainting: Some students lost consciousness. The intensity of the episodes caused genuine physical exhaustion.

Respiratory distress: Breathing irregularities were reported during attacks.

Rashes: Cutaneous symptoms appeared in some cases, indicating genuine physiological response.

Duration: Individual episodes lasted from a few hours to 16 days, averaging around 7 days per student. Once it passed, the student generally recovered fully.

By March 18, 1962 — 47 days after the first reported case — 95 of the school's 159 pupils had been affected. The school closed. The White Fathers sent the girls home.

That is when the second phase began.

Girls who returned to the village of Nshamba, 55 miles west of Bukoba, brought the epidemic with them. Between April and May, 217 mostly young villagers experienced laughing attacks over 34 days. In June, the epidemic appeared at Ramashenye girls' middle school, affecting 48 students. By the time the wave crested and receded 18 months after it began, authorities had closed 14 schools across a 100-mile radius of Bukoba. All the affected communities had one thing in common: they had sent students to Kashasha.

Local doctors and colonial medical officers were bewildered. They tested for toxins in the food and water. They looked for viral or bacterial pathogens. They found nothing. The laughter was real — the pain, the fainting, the rashes were real — but there was no physical agent causing them. The British-trained physicians eventually concluded it was "mass hysteria" and moved on. It would take decades for researchers to build a more nuanced account of what had actually happened.

"The laughter of the 1962 epidemic was no joke — it was the body doing what the mind could not: releasing a pressure that had no other exit."

— Robert Bartholomew, sociologist, on the Kashasha outbreak
Section 02

The Mechanism: How Stress Becomes Contagious

The term "mass hysteria" is both accurate and misleading. It's accurate in that there is no physical contagion agent — no virus, no toxin, no bacteria passing from person to person. It is misleading because it implies the sufferers are faking, exaggerating, or behaving irrationally. They are doing none of these things. What happens in mass psychogenic illness — the clinical term researchers now prefer — is that genuine physiological symptoms are generated by the nervous system itself, triggered by a combination of acute stress, social suggestion, and shared cultural context. The body is not pretending. It is responding to a signal that is internal rather than external.

The mechanism begins with stress. When the human nervous system perceives a threat — social, psychological, or physical — it activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, flooding the body with cortisol and adrenaline. This system evolved to handle predators and physical dangers. It is poorly calibrated for the sustained, diffuse, socially structured stress of a school system caught between two collapsing worlds. When the stress is chronic and has no clear external target, the body sometimes converts it: the technical term from psychiatry is "conversion disorder," a condition where psychological distress manifests as genuine physical symptoms — paralysis, seizures, pain, loss of speech, involuntary movement.

What makes mass psychogenic illness distinctive is the social transmission layer. Once one person's nervous system has converted stress into symptoms, others can catch those symptoms through a process that resembles nothing so much as learning. The brain has neural systems — including what researchers associate with mirror neuron activity — that are wired to simulate and replicate the states of people around us. This is the machinery of empathy, of social coordination, of learning by imitation. Under the right conditions, it can cause one person's distress response to become another's.

The transmission pathway is not verbal or even conscious. Watching someone lose control of their laughter, watching the distress on their face, watching the apparent involuntariness of it — this activates something in an observer's nervous system, particularly in someone who shares the same stressors, the same social world, the same cultural interpretation of what is happening. The observer's threat-detection system reads the sufferer's state as a signal: something dangerous is happening here, something my body should respond to. And it does.

The stress context that generated it — post-independence anxiety, missionary school pressure, cultural dissonance — does not disappear, but something shifts. Later research cannot precisely identify what.

The researchers who have studied this most carefully — sociologist Robert Bartholomew and psychiatrist Simon Wessely — emphasise two specific features of the Kashasha situation that explain why the epidemic happened there, at that moment. The first is that the boarding school was a cohesive group under shared stress: 159 girls living together, eating together, sleeping together, all facing the same pressures, all with the same insufficient resources to manage them. Mass psychogenic illness almost never erupts in isolation — it requires a group that shares both the stressor and the social bonds through which symptoms can travel.

The second feature is cultural: the community needed to lack an alternative explanation that would interrupt the transmission. If the first girls had been immediately told "you are experiencing a conversion reaction caused by psychological stress," that cognitive reframing might have broken the loop — given observers a different interpretation for what they were seeing, one that didn't trigger their own threat-detection systems. Instead, the response was confusion, investigation, the closure of the school — all actions that signalled, implicitly, that something genuinely dangerous and unknown was happening. Fear amplifies MPI. A community that doesn't know what is wrong is a community whose threat-detection systems stay maximally activated.

Section 03

The Connections: A Pattern Across Five Centuries

The Tanganyika epidemic is strange, but it is not isolated. Mass psychogenic illness has been documented across centuries and cultures, always following the same structural pattern: a cohesive group under shared stress, a precipitating event that one or more members responds to physically, and a social network through which the response propagates. The symptom — laughter, dancing, fainting, paralysis, tics — varies with the culture and the era. The mechanism does not.

Historical Outbreaks

  • The Dancing Plague, 1518. In Strasbourg (then in the Holy Roman Empire), a woman named Frau Troffea began dancing in the street and could not stop. Within a month, 400 people had joined her. People danced until their feet bled, collapsed from exhaustion, and in some accounts died. Local authorities, convinced the cure was more dancing, hired musicians and erected a stage. Historians now classify it as MPI triggered by famine, disease, and religious terror — specifically the fear of being cursed by St Vitus.
  • Salem Witch Trials, 1692. The afflictions of the Salem accusers — convulsions, visions, sensations of being bitten and pinched — match MPI symptomology precisely. A cohesive group (young women in a Puritan community under intense social pressure) began exhibiting symptoms that spread through social networks. The cultural frame — witchcraft — provided the interpretation that both explained the symptoms and amplified the fear.
  • The Halifax Slasher, 1938. Residents of Halifax, England reported being attacked by a mysterious razor-wielding stranger. Wounds appeared on multiple victims. Police eventually discovered all the wounds were self-inflicted — but unconsciously so, by people caught up in a community-wide panic that had made victimhood the socially legible way of expressing fear.

Modern Equivalents

  • Le Roy, New York, 2011–12. Twelve teenage girls at Le Roy Junior-Senior High School began exhibiting Tourette's-like symptoms: uncontrollable tics, verbal outbursts, spasms. Investigation found no environmental toxin, no infection. Researchers concluded MPI. The pattern was identical to Kashasha: a cohesive group of adolescent girls, a shared stress environment, transmission along friendship networks.
  • TikTok Tourette's, 2019–21. A global surge in adolescents presenting with functional Tourette-like behaviours was traced to TikTok videos of a German YouTuber (Jan Zimmermann, channel "Gewitter im Kopf") documenting his life with Tourette's. Neurologists at the University of Münster described it as "mass social media-induced illness" — the first documented case of MPI spreading through a purely virtual social network rather than physical proximity.
  • Havana Syndrome, 2016–present. US diplomats in Cuba and elsewhere reporting hearing sounds and developing neurological symptoms. The debate continues, but several research groups have raised MPI as a plausible contributing mechanism, particularly for the rapid spread through diplomatic communities and the symptom clustering among those who had heard about others' experiences.

The TikTok case is the most important update to the Tanganyika framework. What the 1962 epidemic demonstrated is that MPI can jump institutional boundaries — from school to village — when affected individuals return home and carry the social vector with them. What the 2019–21 Tourette's outbreak demonstrated is that the social vector no longer requires physical presence at all. A teenager in Australia could watch videos of a German YouTuber, spend hours immersed in a community of people describing and displaying the same symptoms, and develop those symptoms herself — without ever meeting anyone affected, without ever entering the institution where the outbreak began.

The nervous system, it turns out, cannot reliably distinguish between social signals received in person and social signals received through a screen. The same threat-detection circuitry that caused a Kashasha girl to catch her dormitory mate's distress response is activated by watching dozens of TikTok videos of people experiencing the same distress. The epidemic's geographic radius — once limited by how far an affected student could walk or travel — now extends as far as the internet reaches.

"The laughter epidemic of 1962 was a tragedy. But it was also a proof of concept: the human nervous system is so social that it can make entire communities physically ill through suggestion alone."

— Synthesis from Bartholomew & Wessely, "Protean Nature of Mass Sociogenic Illness"

There is a final connection that tends to get overlooked. The Tanganyika epidemic began six weeks after independence. The post-independence moment in newly decolonised states produced a specific stress profile: the abrupt removal of one authority structure without a settled replacement, heightened expectations on young people to achieve what their parents could not, the collision between the values taught by colonial missionary schools and the values being reclaimed by nationalist movements. Similar outbreaks of MPI have been documented in other post-colonial transition periods, in other missionary school settings, in other communities undergoing rapid normative reorganisation. The laughter at Kashasha was not random. It was a symptom of a historical moment being processed — imperfectly, physically, collectively — by bodies that had no other language for what was happening to them.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

The Dancing Plague of 1518

John Waller's research for his book A Time to Dance, A Time to Die (2008) is the definitive treatment of the Strasbourg outbreak. His argument — that dancing mania was a culturally specific conversion disorder triggered by famine, disease, and the fear of St Vitus's curse — mirrors the Tanganyika analysis almost exactly, 444 years earlier. The book also covers the broader history of "dancing manias" across medieval Europe.

Robert Bartholomew's Catalogue

Bartholomew, a sociologist who has spent decades cataloguing mass psychogenic illness, maintains a database of hundreds of outbreaks across history. His 2001 book Little Green Men, Meowing Nuns and Head-Hunting Panics covers the breadth of the phenomenon. His academic papers on Tanganyika specifically are the primary secondary sources on the 1962 outbreak.

Functional Neurological Disorder

The modern clinical framework that has largely replaced "conversion disorder" is Functional Neurological Disorder (FND) — a condition in which the nervous system generates real symptoms without structural pathology. The FND Research Foundation and neurologist Jon Stone's work at the University of Edinburgh are the best entry points into how medicine currently understands what was happening to the Kashasha girls.

The TikTok Tourette's Papers

The 2021 paper by Kirsten Müller-Vahl and colleagues — "Stop that! It's not Tourette's but a new type of mass sociogenic illness" — published in Brain (Oxford Academic) is the primary document on social media-transmitted MPI. The paper's finding that the spread was entirely virtual, requiring no physical contact, is arguably the most significant update to MPI theory since the field was formalised in the 1970s.

Julius Nyerere and Tanganyikan Independence

The political context of the epidemic is inseparable from the epidemic itself. Nyerere's account of the independence transition — the pressures he placed on young Tanganyikans to prove that the new nation could achieve what colonialism had denied them — is the background against which the Kashasha girls' stress must be understood. His essays from 1961–62 are collected in Freedom and Unity (1966).
