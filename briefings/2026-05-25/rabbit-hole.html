The Dancing Plague of 1518: When Fear Made Strasbourg Dance Itself to Death

In July 1518, a woman named Frau Troffea stepped into a street in Strasbourg and began to dance. She danced for six days without stopping. Within a month, up to 400 people were dancing with her — for days and weeks, barely sleeping, some until their hearts gave out. The city’s solution was to hire musicians and clear a stage. It made things much, much worse.

In July 1518, a woman in Strasbourg began dancing uncontrollably in the street and could not stop; within weeks, up to 400 people had joined her in a mania that lasted two months and killed an unknown number from exhaustion. The most credible explanation is mass psychogenic illness — a collective nervous breakdown triggered by famine, plague, and a genuine belief that the saint St. Vitus could curse you with unstoppable dancing.

TL;DR

In the summer of 1518, Strasbourg — then part of the Holy Roman Empire — experienced one of history’s most documented cases of mass psychogenic illness. It began with one woman dancing in the street and escalated to hundreds of people dancing compulsively for days and weeks, some until they died. The city’s authorities, acting on doctors’ advice that the mania needed to “work itself out,” hired musicians and cleared the guild halls for the dancers. This made the contagion spread faster. The plague eventually ended when dancing was banned and the sufferers were taken to a shrine of St. Vitus. Historian John Waller’s research established that the most plausible cause was not ergot poisoning or religious ritual but a form of collective psychological collapse — a population so overwhelmed by famine, disease, and superstitious dread that the body simply broke in a very specific, culturally shaped way.

Section 01

The Story

Strasbourg in the summer of 1518 was a city under acute stress. The previous years had brought repeated crop failures, severe famines, and epidemic disease — smallpox, syphilis, and a sweating sickness all circulating simultaneously. Infant mortality was high. Hunger was widespread among the poor. The city’s population was deeply pious in a manner that was specific and consequential: they believed, sincerely, that the Catholic saint St. Vitus could afflict people with a dancing madness as divine punishment. This was not a fringe superstition. It was established folk belief with a name — the “St. Vitus curse” — and a history of apparent precedent stretching back more than a century.

In this context, sometime in mid-July 1518, a woman known to history as Frau Troffea stepped out of her house in the Rue des Veaux in Strasbourg and began to dance. She danced alone, in the street, without music, for somewhere between four and six days. She was not performing. She did not appear to be celebrating. She was, by all accounts, distressed — crying out, unable to stop, sweating through her clothes, her feet bleeding. Her husband, by various accounts, tried to restrain her and could not. Physicians were summoned. They had no diagnosis that fit.

Within a week, 34 more people had joined her. Within a month, the number had grown to perhaps 400 — dancing night and day, barely sleeping, some for weeks without stopping.

The city council of Strasbourg convened and consulted its physicians. The doctors, applying the humoral medicine of the day, decided that the dancers were suffering from “hot blood” — an excess of blood and heat that needed to be discharged through movement. Their prescription was therefore to let the dance continue and provide conditions for it to exhaust itself. The council accordingly hired professional musicians, cleared out the guild halls and the market square, built stages, and arranged for the dancers to be accompanied so they could dance more vigorously. They may have meant well. The effect was to transform a street outbreak into a sanctioned public event, remove social inhibitions against joining, and signal to the city’s population that this was something real and recognised — not individual madness but a known affliction.

The dancing spread. Contemporary chronicles record people collapsing with exhaustion, some dying of heart attacks and strokes after days of near-continuous movement. The figure of fifteen deaths per day cited in some later accounts is almost certainly an exaggeration — the primary sources do not support it — but fatalities did occur. Dancers were described as hallucinating, screaming in pain, begging to stop but continuing anyway. Some reported visions of demons or divine figures. The outbreak reached its peak in late July and August before the council reversed course: banned the musicians, removed the stages, banned all dancing in the city, and transported the worst-affected patients to the hilltop shrine of St. Vitus in Saverne, where religious rituals were performed. The mania gradually subsided. By September 1518 it was over. It did not return.

The event was documented by multiple contemporary sources — city council minutes, a pamphlet printed in Strasbourg that year, the chronicle of physician Symphorien Champier, and later accounts by the humanist Sebastian Brant. These are not legend or folk memory. The outbreak is as historically attested as any event of its kind from the sixteenth century.

Section 02

The Mechanism

Three explanations have been seriously advanced. The first is ergot poisoning. Ergot (Claviceps purpurea) is a fungus that infects rye and other grains, producing alkaloids including ergotamine — a precursor to LSD — which causes hallucinations, convulsions, and a burning sensation in the limbs sometimes called “St. Anthony’s fire.” Contaminated rye bread was a plausible vehicle in sixteenth-century Europe. The theory has a certain appeal because it provides a chemical cause for what otherwise looks like a mass delusion.

Why Ergot Doesn’t Hold Up

John Waller’s 2009 analysis identifies three fatal problems with the ergot hypothesis. First: ergot causes vasoconstriction — it restricts blood flow to the extremities. Someone poisoned by ergot could not physically dance for days at a time; their limbs would fail. Second: even granting that ergot produces spasms, it is implausible that hundreds of people poisoned by the same grain would react with the same specific behaviour — rhythmic, purposeful-looking dancing — rather than a range of convulsive responses. Third: the geographic pattern of historical dancing plagues clusters along the Rhine and Moselle river valleys, linking areas by trade route and social contact rather than by shared climate or crop conditions. Ergot contamination would predict a different distribution. None of the three objections is individually decisive, but together they sink the hypothesis.

The second explanation — that the dancers were participants in a secret religious cult, perhaps a survival of pre-Christian fertility ritual — has been largely abandoned. It was popular in the nineteenth century but lacks documentary support. The outbreak was treated by contemporaries as an affliction, not a celebration, and the sufferers consistently appear to have been involuntary participants.

The third explanation, and the one that commands scholarly consensus, is mass psychogenic illness — sometimes called mass hysteria, though that term carries misleading connotations. Mass psychogenic illness (MPI) is a well-documented phenomenon in which physical symptoms spread through a group without a detectable organic cause, typically in communities under severe psychological stress. The symptoms are real — they are not faked or imagined — but they are generated by the nervous system in response to psychological conditions rather than by external pathogens or toxins.

The mechanism Waller proposes is specific. The population of Strasbourg in 1518 held a shared cultural belief: St. Vitus could inflict a dancing madness on sinners. This belief was not merely an abstract theological position; it was a visceral folk terror. When Frau Troffea began dancing and could not stop, her community interpreted this through the lens of the St. Vitus curse. Once that interpretive frame was established and the council validated the outbreak as real by hiring musicians and providing stages, the conditions for contagion were set. People who were already crushed by famine, disease, and existential dread — people whose nervous systems were operating at the extreme edge of their capacity to cope — saw that dancing was what the curse looked like. And their bodies, in a state of extreme stress and susceptibility, obliged. The cultural expectation, the social validation, and the extreme psychological pressure combined to produce a motor symptom that spread through the population the way beliefs spread: by observation, imitation, and shared meaning.

This is not unique to 1518. Mass psychogenic illness has been documented continuously from the medieval period to the present. What changes is the specific symptom — dancing, fainting, tics, nausea — which is always culturally specific and always shaped by whatever the affected community believes an affliction looks like. In 1518 Strasbourg, affliction looked like dancing.

Section 03

The Connections

The Rhine valley dancing plagues were a pattern, not an anomaly. The 1518 Strasbourg outbreak was not the first or only choreomania in medieval Europe. In 1237, a group of children in Erfurt leaped and danced twenty kilometres to the town of Arnstadt before collapsing from exhaustion. In 1374, a far larger outbreak originating in Aachen spread along the Rhine to Cologne, Mézières, and into Belgium and the Netherlands; thousands were affected. Smaller outbreaks occurred throughout the fifteenth century. The 1518 event was the largest and best-documented, but it existed within a recognisable regional tradition of choreomania that clustered along specific trade and river routes — networks of social contact that explain contagion far better than contaminated grain does.

Nuns, meowing, and barking. Dancing was not the only medieval mass psychogenic illness with a specific, culturally patterned symptom. Between the fifteenth and nineteenth centuries, multiple outbreaks in French, German, and Dutch convents saw nuns climbing trees, meowing like cats, barking like dogs, and imitating sexually explicit behaviour — collective symptoms that terrified convent authorities and were interpreted as demonic possession. The specific behaviours tracked theological anxieties: possession was what affliction was supposed to look like in a convent. Modern analysts categorise these as the same class of phenomenon as the dancing plagues, differing in symptom only because the cultural context differed.

MPI Is Not a Medieval Relic

Mass psychogenic illness continued to be documented throughout the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. The 2011–2012 outbreak in Le Roy, New York, where eighteen high-school students developed involuntary tics and verbal outbursts with no identified neurological cause, followed the same pattern: a tightly knit community under stress, a symptom that spread by social observation, and an eventual resolution when media attention dissipated and the social context changed. A 2018–2019 outbreak among US diplomatic personnel in Havana — the “Havana syndrome” cases — has been partially, though not universally, interpreted through the same lens. The specific symptoms change; the social and psychological structure of the outbreak does not.

The city authorities made it worse, intentionally. One of the counterintuitive findings of Waller’s research is that Strasbourg’s response — hiring musicians, clearing halls, providing a stage — was not incompetence or superstition. It was the medical consensus of the day, derived from humoral theory. The doctors genuinely believed they were treating the patients appropriately. The tragic irony is that the treatment was precisely what amplified the contagion: social validation, public visibility, and the removal of the social inhibitions that might otherwise have prevented susceptible individuals from succumbing. The outbreak subsided when the council reversed course and banned dancing — withdrawing the social permission structure that had enabled spread. This maps cleanly onto the modern understanding of how MPI outbreaks resolve: disrupting the social reinforcement loop, typically by dispersing the affected group and removing media or community attention, is the effective intervention.

What the Dancing Plague reveals about collective suffering. The 1518 outbreak is sometimes framed as a curiosity — a bizarre footnote to medieval superstition. Waller’s argument is that it is something more important: a window into what happens to human communities under conditions of extreme, sustained, inescapable stress. The people who danced in Strasbourg were not credulous fools. They were people crushed by famine and epidemic and genuine existential dread, living inside a belief system that provided a specific, culturally legible shape for breakdown to take. The counterintuitive lesson is that the bizarre specificity of the symptom — dancing, of all things — is the clue that makes the cause comprehensible. It was not random. It was exactly what their culture taught them affliction looked like.

Further Down the Hole

📖
John Waller — A Time to Dance, A Time to Die (2009)
Waller’s book is the definitive modern account of the 1518 outbreak, drawing on primary sources in Strasbourg’s municipal archives, contemporary chronicles, and the medical literature on mass psychogenic illness. He is a medical historian at Michigan State University. His earlier paper “A Forgotten Plague” (2008) in Endeavour laid out the core argument. The book is readable and argues its case rigorously against the ergot hypothesis and the religious cult theory.
🎶
St. Vitus and the Geography of Sacred Terror
St. Vitus was a Sicilian Christian martyr who died around 303 CE and became the patron saint of dancers, actors, and epileptics. His feast day — 15 June — was associated in the Rhine valley with the belief that dancing before his statue would bring good health for the year, and, conversely, that his curse could inflict compulsive dancing on sinners. The shrine at Saverne was a regional pilgrimage site. The dancing plague outbreaks clustered most heavily in areas where St. Vitus veneration was most intense, which aligns with the mass psychogenic illness hypothesis: the cultural belief was the precondition.
🧠
The Le Roy, New York Outbreak (2011–2012)
In late 2011, a cluster of students at Le Roy Junior-Senior High School in upstate New York developed involuntary tics and verbal outbursts resembling Tourette syndrome. Eighteen students were affected; most were female. Extensive neurological testing found no organic cause. Media coverage amplified the outbreak. The New York State Health Department and the National Institutes of Health ultimately concluded this was a case of mass psychogenic illness. It resolved as media attention faded and students were separated. The case is the most thoroughly studied MPI outbreak in recent American history.
🍃
Ergot and the Salem Witch Trials
The ergot hypothesis that Waller rejected for 1518 was separately proposed for the 1692 Salem witch trials by historian Linnda Caporael in a 1976 paper in Science. The argument: the accusers’ fits, hallucinations, and sensations of being pinched or bitten could have been ergot poisoning from contaminated rye grown in the wet marshlands of Salem Village. The hypothesis remains controversial; historians point out that the symptom distribution does not match ergot’s known effects and that the sociological dynamics of Salem — family disputes, community tensions, theocratic authority — provide a sufficient explanation without the toxicology. But it surfaces reliably in pop-science summaries of Salem, making it worth knowing both the claim and the objection.
🌐
Choreomania in Fiction and Culture
The dancing plague has accumulated a long cultural afterlife. W.H. Auden wrote a poem about it. The 2019 horror film Midsommar is loosely inspired by it (and by related European folk customs). The 1374 Aachen outbreak appears in multiple historical novels. Writers are drawn to it because it encodes, in a single image, something genuinely difficult to think about: that human beings, under sufficient pressure, can lose voluntary control of their own bodies not through external force but through the invisible machinery of collective belief. It is a better metaphor for social contagion than most metaphors designed explicitly for that purpose.
