Alpha — Kyle's Lambda

Sunday · 25 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #31 11-min read
TL;DR

Kyle's Lambda is the slope between order flow and price — how many basis points price moves per million dollars of net aggression. Spread and depth tell you the cost of one click; lambda tells you the cost of scaling. A weekend tape like today's, with BTC parked at $76,638 and 1% depth thinned out, is a high-lambda regime — the same $5M market order that barely scratched a Tuesday midday book can drive 30+ bps of slippage right now, which is precisely why Sunday flushes overshoot and Monday opens often retrace half of them.

§01
What It Is
Price impact per unit of flow · the slope of liquidity
Kyle's Lambda (λ) is a single number — the regression slope of price change on signed order flow — that measures how much price moves per dollar of net aggression hitting the book.

Most traders measure liquidity qualitatively. Tight spread = "liquid". Stacked bids = "deep". Both are right but neither is portable. You can't compare BTC liquidity in Asia hours versus US hours, or BTC versus an alt, or a Tuesday afternoon versus a Sunday night, with words like "tight" and "deep". You need a number. Lambda is that number.

The concept comes from Albert "Pete" Kyle's 1985 paper "Continuous Auctions and Insider Trading" — one of the foundational works of modern market microstructure. Kyle's framework imagines a single informed trader hiding inside the noise of uninformed flow, and a market maker who has to price-discriminate against the possibility that any given order knows something. The math falls out to a stunningly clean equation: Δprice = λ × signed flow. Lambda is the price impact coefficient — the slope of the line connecting how aggressive buying moves price up and aggressive selling moves price down.

Practically, lambda is an inverse proxy for liquidity. High λ means thin book — every dollar of flow pushes price hard, because there's not much resting size to absorb it. Low λ means deep book — large orders barely register because there's a wall of passive liquidity ready to soak them up. When pros say "BTC liquidity has thinned out", what they're describing — whether they realise it or not — is lambda rising.

The reason this matters more than spread or top-of-book depth: spread is the cost of one market click; lambda is the cost of scaling. A book with a 0.1 bps spread can still have a vicious lambda if there's nothing two ticks deep. The cost of executing a $50M order isn't 50× the cost of executing $1M — it's closer to √50× under the square-root impact law (more on that in §02), and lambda is the constant of proportionality that sets the level.

§02
Mechanics
The Kyle regression · square-root law · permanent vs temporary impact
Lambda is estimated by regressing 5-minute returns on signed square-root dollar volume — a single slope coefficient that summarises an asset's price impact regime.

Kyle's original 1985 derivation is the linear form: price change is proportional to net flow, with λ as the slope. The empirical estimation procedure most pros use today comes from Hasbrouck (2009) and Goyenko, Holden & Trzcinka (2009), who fit lambda as the slope of a regression of percentage returns on the signed square-root of dollar volume, sampled at 5-minute intervals.

Kyle's original (1985) linear form: Δp = λ × Q where Q = net signed order flow ($ buy − $ sell) λ = Kyle's lambda (price impact coefficient) Hasbrouck (2009) regression estimator: r(i,n) = λ(i) · S(i,n) + ε where r(i,n) = return over 5-min bar n for asset i S(i,n) = Σ sign(v(k)) · √|v(k)| over all trades k in bar n v(k) = signed $ volume of trade k λ(i) = slope coefficient (Kyle's lambda) Practical readout (bps per $M of flow): λ̂ ≈ 10⁴ × (mean |Δp/p|) / (mean √|Q|)
The signed square-root specification is the trick. Raw signed volume produces a slope that scales with √(notional) — consistent with the empirical square-root impact law. Multiply the estimated coefficient by 1e6 to get bps-per-million-dollar units that traders actually use.

The square-root impact law is one of the most robust empirical findings in microstructure. A meta-order of total size Q moves price by approximately k · σ · √(Q/V), where σ is volatility, V is daily volume, and k is an asset-specific constant. The square root is universal — it shows up in equities, futures, options, and crypto with surprising stability. Lambda is the practical instantiation: the coefficient that ties this law to the specific book in front of you.

One nuance crypto traders should know: Talos (2025) calibrated this for crypto and found the pure square-root law underestimates actual slippage by about 4 bps in the 0–0.5% participation rate range — exactly where most institutional orders live. Their fix is a sigmoid-adjusted exponent. The takeaway isn't that the model is wrong; it's that crypto's microstructure is worse than equities at the size most pros actually trade, and lambda runs hotter than the textbook predicts.

Two flavours of impact matter and they're often confused. Temporary impact is the part that mean-reverts after your order — pure cost of demanding liquidity from a depleted book. Permanent impact is the part that sticks because the market interpreted your order as information and re-priced. Kyle's original lambda models the permanent piece; the temporary piece is captured by extensions like Almgren-Chriss. In practice both bleed into your fill, and lambda as commonly estimated mixes them.

§04
In Practice
Reading lambda live · sizing against the slope · worked example
The one rule: size your orders against current lambda, not against the average — weekend BTC is a different asset than midday BTC, and an order calibrated to the average will overshoot half the time and undersize the other half.
1

Estimate Lambda Before You Click — The 1% Depth Shortcut

You don't need a Hasbrouck regression to get a usable lambda. A practical shortcut: take the venue's 1% market depth and divide your intended order size by it. Order = 5% of 1% depth → expect roughly 5 bps of impact (assuming square-root scaling and a normal book). When that ratio crosses ~15%, you're in dangerous territory — break the order up or use a TWAP/VWAP execution.

2

Lambda Spikes Are Tradeable Signals

Lambda isn't just an execution input — it's market state. A sudden spike in measured lambda (price moving a lot for not much flow) is a leading indicator of liquidity withdrawal: market makers stepping back, often ahead of an event. The VPIN measure formalises this. Watching 1% depth on Kaiko or Amberdata is the lazy man's lambda meter; a 30%+ drop in 24h depth ahead of CPI or FOMC is a warning that your normal sizing is now oversized.

3

Different Λ for Spot vs Perps

Crypto pros track two lambdas in parallel — spot λ on Binance/Coinbase and perp λ on Bybit/Binance Futures. Perp λ is usually lower (deeper books, more market makers) but more reflexive: when funding flips violently or OI unwinds, perp λ can blow out faster than spot. If you're hedging a spot position with a perp short, modelling each leg's lambda separately is the difference between a clean hedge and one that costs you 40 bps on entry.

4

Time-of-Day Lambda Cycle

Crypto trades 24/7 but liquidity doesn't. US session (13:30–20:00 UTC): lowest λ — deepest books, most market makers active. Asia day (00:00–07:00 UTC): mid-to-high λ, sensitive to JP/KR news. Weekend Asia evening (Sat/Sun 09:00–14:00 UTC): highest λ — the regime where flash moves and overshoots cluster. Default a position-entry calendar around lambda, not around your time zone. The current weekend tape (BTC pinned at $76,638 with thin books) is exactly this regime.

Worked Example — Sizing a $10M BTC Entry Today
SetupWant to add $10M long BTC at $76,638 on Sunday Sydney evening. Binance 1% depth on BTC/USDT currently around $400M (well below the ~$600M high-water mark).
Naive market buyOrder = 2.5% of 1% depth. Square-root estimator: impact ≈ 14 × √(10) bps ≈ 44 bps in a thin-book regime. On $10M that's $44,000 of slippage — wiped out on entry.
Better — slice itBreak into 10 × $1M TWAP slices over 60 minutes. Per-slice impact: 14 × √(1) ≈ 14 bps. Total realised slippage ≈ 14 bps + permanent piece (~5 bps) = ~19 bps total instead of 44.
Even better — waitIf thesis tolerates delay, hold until Monday US open (13:30 UTC Monday). Expect 1% depth to recover toward $550–600M; lambda drops by roughly 40%. The same $10M order then costs ~9 bps as a single click.
The cost of impatience~$35,000 difference between the naive Sunday market order and the patient Monday-US execution. That's the price of ignoring lambda.
InvalidationIf BTC is breaking structure (e.g. losing $76,500 with size), the lambda cost is irrelevant — you're paying for immediacy, which is the right call.

Timeframe fit: Lambda is an execution concept — relevant on every horizon from scalp to swing, but most acutely on multi-million-dollar single tickets and on entries where the cost of delay is small (no breaking-news, no Wyckoff Phase D break). For tactical re-entries on news, accept the slippage — paying lambda is cheaper than missing the move.

§07
Common Traps
Four ways traders misread lambda — plus one honest "academic-vs-execution" callout
The single biggest mistake: confusing spread with lambda — a 0.1 bps spread can sit on top of a brutal lambda, and traders who size off spread alone get crushed on scaling.
1
Sizing off Spread, Not Depth

Tight spread doesn't mean cheap execution. BTC's top-of-book spread is often sub-bp on Binance, but the depth two ticks deep can be a fraction of what it looks like at the touch. Always check 1% depth (Kaiko, Amberdata, or the venue's own L2 snapshot) before sizing. Spread is the first tick; lambda is the next forty.

2
Assuming Linear Impact (Doubling Order = Doubling Slippage)

The square-root law is universal for a reason — impact grows with √Q, not Q. Doubling a $5M order to $10M doesn't double the slippage; it multiplies it by roughly √2 ≈ 1.41. This sounds reassuring but cuts both ways: you can scale meta-orders gracefully, but you also can't escape impact by shrinking — a $1M ticket still pays meaningful lambda in a thin regime.

3
Ignoring the Permanent / Temporary Split

Naive lambda estimates mix permanent impact (information signal) with temporary impact (liquidity cost that mean-reverts). If you're trading on alpha — i.e. your order is information — most of your impact is permanent and you should expect price not to bounce back. If you're rebalancing or hedging, expect the temporary piece to mean-revert and consider passive limit orders inside the spread to capture it back.

4
Trading the Lambda Spike Instead of Reading It

A sudden lambda spike (price moving a lot per dollar of flow) is a warning, not an entry signal. Retail traders see "price is breaking out hard" and chase; what's actually happening is liquidity withdrawal — market makers stepping back ahead of an event, or a thin air-pocket between levels. Step back from spiking-λ tape unless you have a specific structural reason to engage.

5
Honest Note — Lambda Is a Lens, Not a Trade

Be clear-eyed: Kyle's lambda is an execution framework, not a directional one. It tells you the cost of trading; it doesn't tell you whether to be long or short. Some quant funds run pure-lambda strategies (provide liquidity where λ is high, harvest mean-reversion), but for a directional trader the value is calibration — sizing right, picking the right session, knowing when to slice. Don't fall in love with the metric. Spread and 1% depth get you 80% of what lambda would tell you for a fraction of the work; the residual 20% is where institutional desks earn their fees.
