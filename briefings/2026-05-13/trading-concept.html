Alpha — Passive vs Aggressive Order Flow

Wednesday · 13 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #19 11-min read
TL;DR

Every price tick is a transaction between two opposing roles: an aggressor who pays the spread to be filled now (a market order) and a passive participant who sits in the book waiting to be filled (a limit order). Master traders don't track price — they track which side is doing which: when aggression keeps hammering passive size that refuses to move, the aggressor is being absorbed and the next leg almost always favours the passive side. This is the single conceptual frame that unifies CVD, footprint, absorption, sweeps and heatmaps under one mental model.

§01
What It Is
The atomic distinction · the auction's two roles
Every trade has two sides — one party crossed the spread to take liquidity (aggressive), the other was sitting in the book providing it (passive). Knowing which is which is the foundation of orderflow.

Two participants are required for any single tick to print. One submitted a limit order earlier, parked it at a specific price, and waited — that is the passive side, also called the maker. The other submitted a market order (or a marketable limit that crossed the spread) and demanded execution at whatever price was available — that is the aggressive side, also called the taker. The price moved because the aggressor was willing to pay the spread to get filled immediately; the passive party was willing to be filled at their chosen price but not chase it.

Every other orderflow tool you read about is a derivative of this distinction. CVD is the cumulative sum of aggressive buys minus aggressive sells. Footprint charts split each candle's volume into the portion executed at the bid (aggressive sells) versus the ask (aggressive buys). Absorption is the situation where passive size eats persistent aggressive flow without budging price. Sweep orders are extreme aggression that consumes multiple price levels of passive size in one trip. Liquidity heatmaps visualise where the largest passive resting orders are stacked. If you don't have the passive/aggressive split clear in your head, none of the rest of it has structural meaning.

The reason this frame is so generative: it forces you to think of price as the output of a negotiation, not as a signal in its own right. Aggressors initiate, passives respond. When aggression is consistently absorbed, the initiating side is wrong about price discovery. When aggression goes through cleanly (sweeps), the passive side has stepped back and price will travel. The whole Auction Market Theory tradition — and the Wyckoff method underneath it — rests on this single observation about who is doing what.

AGGRESSORThe taker — submits a market order, crosses the spread PASSIVEThe maker — submits a limit order, sits in the book SPREADGap between best bid and best ask — the cost of being aggressive HITTING THE BIDAggressive sell — market order lands at best bid LIFTING THE OFFERAggressive buy — market order lands at best ask FILLThe match between an aggressive and a passive order
If you only remember one thing Price moves because someone is willing to pay the spread to be filled now. Track that party — not the candle.
§02
Mechanics
Classification rule · the four flow regimes · effort vs result
Every tick can be classified by whether it printed at the bid (aggressive sell) or the ask (aggressive buy) — and the pace of price relative to that classification tells you which side is dominating.

At the tick level, the exchange feed publishes each trade with its execution price and the prevailing bid/ask at that instant. The classification is mechanical:

Tick-level classification: if execution_price >= best_ask → AGGRESSIVE BUY (taker lifted the offer) if execution_price <= best_bid → AGGRESSIVE SELL (taker hit the bid) if best_bid < price < best_ask → MID-FILL / NEUTRAL (auction match or hidden fill) Per-bar aggression balance (Delta): Δ = Σ aggressive_buy_vol − Σ aggressive_sell_vol Passive participation rate: PPR = (bar_volume − |Δ|) / bar_volume high PPR (→1) = balanced two-way market; low PPR (→0) = one-sided aggression Effort-vs-result test (Wyckoff): if |Δ| large AND price_change ≈ 0 → ABSORPTION (passive side wins) if |Δ| small AND price_change large → LIQUIDITY VOID (passive side stepped back)
Note: this classification is approximated by retail charting tools (Pine Script delta uses bar direction × volume — a hack). True classification requires direct trade-stream data from the exchange (Binance trade ws, Bybit trade ws, ATAS, Bookmap, Hyblock, Tradinglite) where each print carries an explicit side flag.

Once each tick is classified, the four canonical flow regimes drop out naturally. They are the only four price-volume relationships that exist, and every chart you'll ever look at is showing you some sequence of these states:

Flow regimeAggressionPrice responseInterpretation
Trend / breakoutOne-sided, sustainedMoves in aggressor's directionPassive side is offside or absent — momentum continues
AbsorptionOne-sided, sustainedStalls at a levelLarge passive size eating flow — reversal candidate
Liquidity voidLightMoves rapidlyPassive side stepped back — sweep or thin-book travel
Balance / chopTwo-way, alternatingRangeboundBoth sides agree on fair value — wait for resolution

This is exactly the "effort vs result" law that Wyckoff codified in the 1930s — long before electronic order books made it measurable. Wyckoff's framework: high effort (volume / aggression) with little result (price movement) signals that the opposing side is winning the auction. The order book just made the measurement explicit. As Rubén Villahermosa puts it in his Wyckoff 2.0 framework, modern orderflow analysis is "Wyckoff's logic, instrumented" — the same auction principle, with a microscope attached.

The key reframing for crypto traders: in spot markets, passive orders represent real capital sitting at a level. In perp markets, passive orders are often algorithmic market-making that pulls within milliseconds if pressure builds. This is why liquidity vanishes during flash moves — a fact made painfully visible during the 10 October 2025 BTC liquidation cascade, when executable depth collapsed by over 98% in 25 minutes while volume stayed elevated. The passive side chose not to participate.

§04
In Practice
Four practice cards · the absorption long · timeframe fit
The one rule that matters: if heavy aggression cannot move price, fade the aggressor — the passive side is winning and price will revert.
i

Classify the regime before you classify the trade.

Look at the last 15–30 minutes of tape. Aggression on with price moving = trend; aggression on with price flat = absorption; aggression off with price moving = void; aggression two-way with price flat = balance. The setup, stop and target are different for every one.

ii

Trade with the side that is winning the auction.

Aggressor wins (trend / void) → trade in the aggressor's direction with stop beyond the last micro-pullback. Passive wins (absorption / balance) → trade in the passive party's direction with stop beyond the level being defended. Never trade against the side that is winning and against the price.

iii

Confluence multiplies — but only with one orderflow signal at a time.

Absorption at a key Wyckoff level (Spring/UT) or at a high-volume node (POC) is high-quality. Absorption with no structural context is just a wall — fine, but lower R-multiple. Don't stack five orderflow signals; they're correlated. Stack one orderflow signal with one structural signal.

iv

Watch what the passive side does after the test.

Real passive size that survives a test will often reload — the wall that held at 80,770 reappears on the next dip, sometimes larger. Spoofed size disappears. If the wall comes back, conviction is real; if it stays gone, the absorption was opportunistic and you should tighten your stop.

Worked example — long the absorption
SetupBTC perp at 80,790; CVD has been negative for 18 minutes, price flat-lined within 30 ticks. Coinglass heatmap shows ~420 BTC bid stacked at 80,770.
TriggerFirst green delta candle on 1m after 14 consecutive red. Confirms aggressors have stopped (or thinned).

Quotes
Wyckoff's effort vs result · modern operators on absorption
Every voice worth quoting on this topic says the same thing in different words: price is the result of who is paying the spread, not a signal in itself.

"When volume and price action diverge — high effort with little result — the operating side is changing hands. The composite operator is absorbing what the public is throwing at him."

Richard D. Wyckoff · paraphrase of the Law of Effort vs Result · 1931 · Wyckoff Analytics

"Prices move because aggressive participants are willing to pay the spread to get filled immediately. Until that is true, you do not have a trend — you have noise."

§07
Common Traps
The five mistakes that turn this edge into noise
The single biggest trap: confusing visible passive size with executed passive fills. Walls that don't transact are spoofs, not absorption.
i.
Confusing displayed size with executed size.

A 500 BTC bid that sits in the book and never trades is information about intent, not absorption. Real absorption requires the wall to actually fill — you should see the size on the wall decrement print by print on the footprint or DOM. If the wall holds without prints decrementing it, the wall is decorative.

ii.
Mistaking thin-book travel for trend.

When the passive side steps back during a news event or off-hours, even small aggressive orders can move price multiple ticks. This looks identical to a real trend on the price chart but the aggression bars are tiny. Do not chase moves where price is travelling on minimal flow — the passive side will return and reprice violently.

iii.
Pine Script CVD ≠ true delta.

Most TradingView CVD scripts approximate sign by candle direction — buying volume on up bars, selling on down bars. That is not the same as true tick-classified delta. Intra-candle absorption is invisible to the approximation. If you're trading from this signal seriously, you need a tool that ingests the tick stream directly (ATAS, Bookmap, Tradinglite, Hyblock).

iv.
Fading the first sweep through a wall.

When a wall breaks, the passive side is gone and there's nothing on the other side to slow the move. Fading the break "because it broke a level" without re-confirmation of passive size on the other side is how you take 5R losses in one tick. Wait for new passive size to appear before fading.

v.
Overhyped: most "smart money" orderflow indicators on YouTube.

The retail-facing orderflow indicator industry has badly inflated this concept. Any indicator that claims to show "smart money buying" from spot data alone — without paying for the tick stream — is reading the same publicly available candle data you are, dressed in different colours. The conceptual frame on this page is rigorous; the products selling against it largely aren't. Buy the books, not the indicators.
