Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

Four sources converge on a single discipline this week — the cost of doing the easy substitute when the harder version is the one that actually works. Daily Stoic on Marcus Aurelius and the adaptive mission: what you thought your assignment was matters less than what life has now put in your path; the obstacle is the path. Cal Newport's empirical case against AI-assisted academic writing using Organization Science Task Force data: reading-ease scores down 1.28 standard deviations since ChatGPT's 2023 release, heavy-AI papers desk-rejected at 70% versus 44% for non-AI — easier is not better. Mark Manson's underrated cheat code, stated bluntly: show up on time, do what you said you would, own your mistakes. Shane Parrish on trusting habits over ambitions when the two diverge — and on the conversational technique that converts "you're wrong" into "tell me what you know that I don't".

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Daily Stoic · Ryan Holiday · 12 May 2026

"No, This Is The Mission" — Marcus Aurelius and the Mission That Redefines Itself

Holiday's Tuesday entry takes one of the most frequently quoted lines in Meditations — the passage that became the title of his 2014 book The Obstacle Is the Way — and uses it to make a point that the popular reading consistently misses. The opening is deliberately disorienting: "You thought you were supposed to be doing this. You thought your job was to do that. You were on a mission to do something very specific. But then what happened? Life happened. Stuff happened. Other people happened. And now? Now that is the mission."

The pronoun shift in the last sentence is doing the entire essay's work. The reader expects "the mission is still the mission; you just have to fight harder for it". Holiday delivers the opposite: the mission has changed, and the changed mission is the real one. The original plan is not the standard against which the new conditions are to be measured. The new conditions are the standard. Whatever you imagined yourself doing has been overwritten, in real time, by what life is asking of you now — and the discipline is to recognise the overwrite rather than mourning the original.

"There can be no impeding our intentions or our dispositions. Because we can accommodate and adapt. The mind adapts and converts to its own purposes the obstacle to our acting." — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (Book V.20, Hays translation)

The Marcus citation is the one Holiday's earlier work has built a career on, and the reread the 2026 essay forces is sharper than the 2014 book's framing. The original popular reading was: the obstacle is something to overcome, and Stoicism gives you the tools to overcome it. The 2026 reread is: the obstacle is not a thing to defeat. The obstacle is what your mind, by adapting, converts into your purpose. The verb that matters is "converts". The obstacle is not the enemy of the mission. The obstacle is the raw material the mission is made of, after the mission has done its actual work of becoming what the conditions require.

The instruction the essay closes with is the one most popularisations of amor fati elide. "It might be less fun, it might require more patience than we planned, more forgiveness than we possessed, demand more creativity than anticipated. But that's the point!" The point is not that you will eventually overcome the obstacle and get back to the original plan. The point is that the original plan has been retired by the obstacle, and the new plan — the one shaped by patience you did not know you had, forgiveness you did not know you possessed, creativity you did not know was available — is the better plan, because it is the one made of you-meeting-conditions rather than you-imagining-conditions. The obstacle is not a detour. It is the path. The original path was the detour, and the detour has now been corrected by life.

The translation to operators in 2026 is uncomfortably direct. The redundancy that scrambled the five-year plan; the relationship that ended on a timeline you did not pick; the diagnosis, the regulatory change, the failed product launch, the unexpected caregiving duty — in each case the popular instruction is "find your way back to the original mission". The Marcus instruction is "the original mission has been retired, and the only useful question is what the new conditions are asking of you". The second framing is harder to swallow and far more workable, because it stops the operator from defending a plan that the universe has already cancelled and starts the work of meeting the actual situation. Most operators spend years on the first move when the second move was always available. The Marcus passage, read in 2026, is one of the cleanest pieces of advice in the literature for releasing the obsolete plan and getting on with the live one.

Essay · Cal Newport · calnewport.com · 11 May 2026

"Easy is Overrated" — The Organization Science Data on AI-Assisted Academic Writing

Newport's Monday post is the most empirically grounded essay he has written in a year and the first piece of mainstream commentary to put numbers on what the academic community has been complaining about for thirty months. The data comes from the Organization Science AI Task Force, which audited the journal's submissions from January 2021 through early 2026 — spanning the pre-ChatGPT baseline, the November 2022 release, and the subsequent three years of generative-AI saturation. The findings are unambiguous and the magnitudes are not small.

The headline numbers, all from the Task Force report Newport cites: submissions to the journal increased sharply after ChatGPT's release. The share of papers written with minimal AI assistance dropped from roughly 100% pre-2023 to about 30% by 2026. Reading-ease scores fell by 1.28 standard deviations over the audit window — a magnitude that, in the educational-assessment literature, would represent the difference between a graduate-level reader and a high-school-level reader becoming the median outcome. The papers got harder to read, not easier. The submissions also got longer, used more jargon, used more nominalisations (verb-derived nouns like "implementation" and "instantiation"), and used more complex sentence structures. The writing, in Newport's phrase, "feels weightless".

"Most people assume that AI produces cleaner, more polished text…but on the measures that capture whether a reader can actually parse and absorb the prose, AI writing is worse." — Cal Newport, "Easy is Overrated" (calnewport.com, 11 May 2026)

The most operationally important numbers are the acceptance figures. Heavy-AI papers face desk-rejection at 70% versus 44% for non-AI papers. The acceptance rate for heavy-AI papers is 3.2% versus 12% for low-AI papers. Translation: a researcher writing with extensive AI assistance is roughly four times more likely to be rejected before peer review and about a quarter as likely to be accepted overall. The tool that was supposed to be a productivity multiplier is, on the empirical record at one of the field's flagship journals, a productivity divider — producing four times more submissions that produce one-quarter the acceptances per submission, which is a roughly sixteen-fold collapse in the throughput-to-publication ratio that the operator-level "AI saved me time" intuition does not capture at all.

The Newport thesis is sharper than the data alone. "Making things faster or easier is not the same as making things better." The category error the 2023–2025 productivity discourse made — treating speed and ease as proxies for quality — is the error the Organization Science data is now correcting. The AI tools are excellent at lowering the effort cost of producing text. They are not excellent at producing text that conveys ideas clearly to the kind of reader whose attention is the actual scarce resource. The two are different measurements, and the dominant productivity narrative of the last three years has been mistaking the former for the latter. The reading-ease collapse is the bill arriving for the mistake. Heavy-AI papers are not winning slower; they are not winning at all.

The implication beyond academia is significant and Newport is too disciplined to overclaim it. The reading-ease metric in academic writing maps imperfectly onto reader comprehension in other domains, but the underlying dynamic — AI lowers the cost of producing text, raises the volume of text produced, lowers the average quality of text reaching the reader, raises the rejection rate at the bottleneck — is structurally the same in legal briefs, in business memos, in marketing copy, and in product specifications. The bottleneck (the reader, the editor, the partner, the customer) does not get faster as the volume rises, and the quality threshold the bottleneck applies does not lower. The arithmetic is the same arithmetic. The "easier is not better" lesson is among the most operationally useful pieces of empirical work to land this quarter on the AI-and-knowledge-work question, and the Task Force report is worth reading directly rather than at second hand.

Newsletter · Mark Manson · The Breakthrough #210 · 11 May 2026

Mark Manson on the Underrated Cheat Code: Be Incredibly Reliable

Manson's Monday issue is shorter than usual and the entire idea fits inside three lines on the page. "An underrated cheat code in life: being incredibly reliable. Show up on time. Do what you say you will. Own your mistakes. It goes so much further than you think." The compression is the point. Most of what passes for personal-development content in 2026 is competing on novelty — the new framework, the new app, the new biohack — and the highest-leverage move available to most operators is the dullest one in the catalogue: do the thing you already said you would do, on time, and tell the truth when you do not.

The cheat-code framing is doing more work than it appears. A cheat code, in the literal video-game sense, is a small input that produces a disproportionate output by triggering a system response the designer did not anticipate. Reliability has the same structure in social and professional systems. The marginal cost of being on time is small. The marginal cost of doing what you said you would is mostly the friction of writing it down. The marginal cost of owning a mistake is approximately one moment of ego sting. The cumulative payoff — trust compounded over a career, the assignment offered without a backup plan, the relationship that survives one bad year because the operator has not previously cost the counterparty time — is large enough that anyone who actually internalises it acquires an unfair advantage over the median professional. The reason the cheat code works is that almost nobody runs it consistently.

"If you asked the people closest to you, would they say you're reliable? Where in your life might you be letting people down?" — Mark Manson, The Breakthrough #210 (11 May 2026)

The two journaling prompts are the operative part of the issue. The first — would the people closest to you say you're reliable? — is the test that cannot be self-administered. The honest answer requires imagining each of five or six specific people answering the question, and most operators discover, on running this honestly, that the answer differs sharply across relationships. The work spouse says yes. The actual spouse says "mostly, except for the standing thing on Sundays you've been bailing on for six months". The senior client says yes. The junior teammate says "you're great when it matters and unreliable when it doesn't, which is its own kind of unreliable because I never know which I'm getting". The asymmetric reliability is usually the operator's actual reliability profile, and it is far worse than the self-report.

The second prompt — where in your life might you be letting people down? — is the diagnostic. Manson's instruction is to pick one such thing this week and do it. Not all of them; just one. The compounding works through specificity. The operator who reliably picks one commitment per week to honour-this-time, sustained over a quarter, recovers thirteen relationships from the slow-decay path. The operator who waits for the energy to do all thirteen at once recovers zero, because the energy never arrives. The instruction is unglamorous in the same way Manson's "remove one thing" instruction last week was unglamorous, and the unglamour is precisely why the practice works: it survives the absence of motivation.

The reader letters Manson published this week, responding to last week's "remove one thing from your calendar" prompt, sharpen the reliability point in an unexpected direction. Three of them describe the same pattern: the operator who is unreliable to themselves first — missing the gym, the sleep, the personal day — and then, downstream, becomes unreliable to others as the depletion compounds. The reliability cheat code, on closer reading, is not actually about being reliable to others. It is about being reliable to yourself first, on the unglamorous things (the sleep, the calendar limits, the small daily promises), and letting the external reliability follow as the byproduct. The compounding starts at the inside. The cheat code is one move; the position from which the move is sustainable is the longer project.

Newsletter · Shane Parrish · Farnam Street Brain Food #680 · 10 May 2026

Shane Parrish on Trusting the Habits Over the Ambitions — and the Conversation Technique That Defuses Disagreement

Parrish's Sunday issue carries three "tiny thoughts" that read like aphorisms but earn their place because each one names a specific failure mode that most operators run for years before noticing. The first is the sharpest piece of behavioural-economics writing Parrish has put on the page this year. "When someone's habits don't match their ambitions, trust the habits." The corollary — "do your habits match your ambitions?" — is the part most readers will skim past and the part doing the diagnostic work.

The instruction is older than the modern productivity literature; James Clear's Atomic Habits framing of identity-driven habits says roughly the same thing in different language. What Parrish's compression adds is the social-evaluation move. When you are watching someone else — an employee, a partner, a candidate, a friend — and you are deciding whether to trust their stated ambition, trust the habits. The stated ambition is what they want you to believe. The habits are what is actually happening. The two diverge constantly, and the politely-default move in most professional contexts is to honour the stated ambition. The Parrish instruction is to honour the habits, because the habits are predictive and the ambitions are aspirational. Most ambitions die unfulfilled not because they were the wrong ambitions but because they were ambitions instead of habits.

The second tiny thought is the corrective for a common professional misjudgement. "Most success comes from 50 small things moving in the same direction, not one big thing." The line cuts against the "one big bet" framing that dominates the 2026 startup and personal-brand literature. The 50-small-things framing is not anti-bet; it is anti-only-bet. The professional who places one large bet and does fifty small things in the same direction usually wins. The professional who places one large bet and does five small things in the same direction, with the other forty-five pointing in random directions, usually loses, regardless of how good the bet was. The fifty things are the substrate that makes the one thing work. Without the substrate the one thing has nothing to land on.

The third tiny thought is the one with the highest immediate operational value, because it converts directly into a single conversational technique that almost no professional uses. "Most beliefs are soft until challenged. When you tell someone they're wrong, the belief hardens." The empirical observation is consistent with the social-psychology literature on attitude polarisation under counter-argument (the work going back to Lord, Ross, and Lepper, 1979) and with the more recent literature on "belief perseverance". What Parrish adds is the technique: "Seems like you have a reason for saying that…" The intervention treats the disagreement as evidence the other person has information you do not, rather than as evidence they have a wrong belief. The interlocutor explains rather than defends, and Parrish reports the result candidly: "half the time, they'll find gaps in their own reasoning before you have time to point them out. The other half, you'll learn something and change your own mind." Either outcome is better than the default.

The issue's middle section — the Aldous Huxley line on carrying the spirit of the child into old age, the Bill Walsh line on aiming for perfection rather than mediocre, and the long Harrison McCain quote on what makes an entrepreneur ("the entrepreneur keeps himself operating on the threshold of excellence because he fears mediocrity…the entrepreneur has learned to dig for facts") — functions as the philosophical scaffolding for the three tiny thoughts. Huxley is the prerequisite (curiosity preserved); Walsh is the calibration (aim high enough that missing still produces good work); McCain is the operating posture (excellence as the threshold, facts as the substrate, the next opportunity as the focus). Read together, the issue is a compressed statement of the Farnam Street worldview, which is unusual in its consistency: the operator who survives forty years in a field is the one who reads carefully, talks less, trusts behaviour over claims, and keeps the door to childlike curiosity slightly ajar.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · From Daily Stoic · "No, This Is The Mission"

The Mission-Adaptation Audit: Retiring the Plan Life Has Already Cancelled

Holiday's restatement of the Marcus passage converts into a personal practice that runs in about forty minutes and is unusually effective for operators in the middle of a forced transition (job loss, relationship change, diagnosis, sector dislocation). The structure has four steps:

  • Name the original mission, in one sentence. The plan you had for the next five years before the recent disruption. The career arc, the family structure, the financial trajectory. One sentence, written down. The discipline is to state it explicitly so it can be examined; most operators carry the original plan as background assumption and never put it on the page.
  • Name the disruption, also in one sentence. The redundancy. The diagnosis. The end of the relationship. The regulatory change. The product that did not work. One sentence, written down. The discipline is to be specific rather than thematic; "everything went wrong" is not the disruption. The disruption is the named thing on the named date.
  • Ask: what does the new condition allow that the old plan did not? Not "how do I get back to the old plan?" — the wrong question. The Marcus question is what the changed conditions are now asking of you, and what they make available that was not available before. The redundancy frees the time. The diagnosis sharpens the priority. The relationship's end reopens the geography. The discipline is to list the new affordances honestly, even if they are unwelcome.
  • Write the new mission, in one sentence. Not a recovery plan. A new mission, derived from the new conditions, that uses the new affordances. "My mission is now to…" — complete the sentence. If the new sentence is identical to the old sentence, the adaptation has not happened; rewrite. If the new sentence requires more patience, forgiveness, or creativity than the old one demanded, the adaptation is probably correct. Marcus's instruction was not "endure the obstacle until you can resume". It was "the mind converts the obstacle to its own purpose". The new sentence is the purpose, converted.
Practice · From Cal Newport · "Easy is Overrated"

The Reading-Ease Test: Auditing Your Own AI-Assisted Output Against the Bottleneck

Newport's Organization Science data converts into a personal-output audit that runs in about thirty minutes and produces a calibrated estimate of where AI is helping and hurting your work product. The structure has four steps:

  • Pick three pieces of professional writing you produced in the last quarter with significant AI assistance. A memo, a proposal, an email thread, a brief. Not the drafts; the things you actually sent. The discipline is to choose pieces where AI did substantive drafting work, not just spellcheck or formatting.
  • Run each through a reading-ease test (Flesch-Kincaid, free online tools, takes seconds per document). Compare against three pre-2023 pieces of yours in the same genre. If the AI-assisted pieces score harder to read than the older work, you have the Organization Science pattern at the personal level. Most operators discover the AI work scores noticeably worse on this metric than their pre-AI baseline, and most are surprised by it.
  • For each AI-assisted piece, identify the response it actually got from the bottleneck. Did the partner act on it, or ask three clarifying questions? Did the customer reply, or go silent? Did the regulator approve, or send follow-ups? The bottleneck's response is the data; your impression of the document's polish is not. The two diverge more often than operators realise.
  • For the next thirty days, run one experiment: write one significant piece per week without AI drafting assistance, and compare the bottleneck response. Not the writing-speed comparison; the response comparison. Most operators discover, within three or four such experiments, that the non-AI version produces faster and clearer responses from the actual reader. The time "saved" by AI on the drafting side is often paid back twice on the response-handling side. The audit makes the trade visible.

The deeper point Newport's data forces is the one most operators have not yet internalised. The reader (the partner, the editor, the customer, the regulator, the academic reviewer) is the actual bottleneck, and the reader's cognitive bandwidth is unchanged by the existence of AI. More text per unit time arrives at the reader; the reader's capacity to read does not rise; the reader's quality threshold does not lower. Every operator who is optimising for ease of production is competing in a market where the constraint is at the consumption end, and the consumption end is responding to the volume by becoming more discriminating and rejecting more. The asymmetric move — produce less, produce better, write so the reader can actually parse you — will continue to outperform the AI-volume strategy, increasingly, for the entire rest of the decade.

Practice · From Mark Manson · The Breakthrough #210

The Reliability Audit: Where You Are Asymmetrically Unreliable, and the One Promise to Keep This Week

Manson's reliability cheat code converts into a relationship-specific audit that runs in about thirty minutes and is unusually durable. The structure has four steps:

  • List the eight people whose trust most matters to your professional and personal life. Spouse or partner; closest two or three friends; immediate team or business partner; principal client or senior boss; immediate family. The number is small on purpose. The discipline is to write the actual names rather than the abstract categories.
  • For each name, rate your reliability on three dimensions, one to five. On time: do you show up when you said you would? Do what you said: do you complete the things you committed to? Own mistakes: when you miss, do you name it cleanly and repair, or do you obscure and rationalise? Twenty-four ratings total. The honest audit usually surfaces two patterns: one person who scores high on all three (the model relationship to study), and one or two who score low on a specific dimension that has been quietly costing trust.
  • Identify the asymmetric pattern. Most operators are reliable to the high-status counterparties (clients, bosses, the people whose evaluation is most consequential) and unreliable to the closer-in counterparties (spouse, kids, longstanding friends) because the latter forgive more. The forgiveness is exactly what makes the unreliability compound, because it does not produce immediate punishment. The honest audit names the pattern.
  • Pick one specific commitment, to one specific person, that you will keep this week. Not a new commitment; one you have already made and quietly let slide. The standing call, the date you postponed, the project you said you would finish, the apology you owe but have not delivered. Schedule it. Do it. Tell no one. Manson's instruction is that the reliability compounding starts with one such act per week, sustained, and that the operator who does this for six months recovers more trust than the operator who runs a "be more reliable" program for six weeks and then quits. The slow version wins on every horizon longer than a quarter.
Practice · From Farnam Street · Brain Food #680

The Conversation Repair Tool: Turning Disagreement Into Inquiry With Six Words

Parrish's "Seems like you have a reason for saying that…" is one of the highest-leverage conversational techniques in current popular circulation, and the reason it works is structural rather than rhetorical. The practice converts directly into a usable script. The structure has four parts:

  • When you notice you are about to say "you're wrong" or "actually, the data shows…", pause. The reflex is to correct, and the correction is what hardens the other person's belief. The Lord-Ross-Lepper 1979 study and its successors all show the same effect: counter-argument under perceived attack produces stronger commitment to the original belief, not weaker. The reflex is the failure mode.
  • Replace the correction with the six-word opener: "Seems like you have a reason for saying that…" The opener does three things at once. It signals you are taking the other person's view seriously rather than dismissing it. It asks them to articulate the reasoning behind the position rather than the position itself. And it positions you as a curious listener rather than an opposing speaker, which removes the ego-defence reflex.
  • Listen for two things: the explicit reasoning the other person offers, and the implicit information you did not have. Most disagreements have one of two shapes — the other person knows something you do not (in which case you are about to learn), or the other person is reasoning from a frame you do not share (in which case you are about to understand). Both outcomes are productive. Neither is available through correction.
  • When you have heard the reasoning, decide which of three responses fits. If you have learned something material, update your view and say so; the credibility cost is small and the trust gain is large. If their reasoning has a gap they will find on their own, ask one clarifying question and let them get there. If the reasoning is intact but you still disagree, name the specific point of divergence calmly — "I think we agree on X but disagree on Y; can I tell you why I see Y differently?" — rather than rebutting the whole position. Most professional disagreements collapse cleanly under the third move because the divergence is narrower than either party initially thought.

The compounding effect of the technique is the part Parrish does not name explicitly but that the empirical record shows clearly. The operator who runs this method sustained over a year acquires a reputation for being someone people can disagree with productively, which is one of the rarest professional reputations available and one of the most valuable. The first-order effect is that individual disagreements go better. The second-order effect is that people bring you their disagreements rather than working around them, which converts the operator into a node through which difficult information flows. The information advantage compounds. The cost is the six words and the discipline to deploy them under emotional pressure, which is harder than it looks but cheaper than almost anything else in the toolkit.

04 On the Horizon
AI & Knowledge Work

The "Reader-Bottleneck" Argument Will Become the Dominant Critique of AI Productivity Through 2027

The Organization Science data Newport cites is the first piece of large-N empirical evidence that the AI-volume strategy underperforms on the metric that actually matters — reader response — and it is unlikely to be the last. Expect the next twelve to eighteen months of academic editorial commentary, professional journals, and high-end business writing (HBR, MIT Sloan Review, the major law-review symposia) to converge on a single thesis: volume is not throughput; throughput is what gets through the reader, and the reader has not gotten faster. The downstream effect on professional services will be a quiet repricing of "human-written" as a premium label, similar to the "hand-stitched" or "single-origin" labels that emerged in the 2010s. The firms that price on this dimension first — legal, advisory, editorial — will capture the early margin, before the label saturates.

Reliability

"Boring Reliability" Will Outperform "AI-Augmented Ambition" as the Default Career Meta-Strategy

Manson's three-line cheat code is part of a quieter shift in how the 2026 careers literature is pricing professional dispositions. The 2021–2024 vocabulary — "10x performer", "ambitious bet", "asymmetric upside" — is giving ground to a vocabulary that the under-35 cohort would have called unfashionable five years ago: dependable, on time, finishes what they start. The shift is partly driven by the AI saturation of the production side: when anyone can produce ten times as much, the scarce input becomes the human who can be counted on to act on the thirty percent of it that matters. Expect "boring reliability" framing to migrate from operator newsletters into mainstream career advice and management literature through 2027. The hedge-fund and Big Law cultures, which have always quietly priced reliability above intelligence, will look prescient in retrospect.

Stoic Practice

The "Adaptive Mission" Frame Will Become the Default Reading of Amor Fati for the 2026–2028 Forced-Pivot Decade

Holiday's "the mission adapts" framing is the cleanest restatement of amor fati in current popular circulation, and it lands in a period that is, on the empirical record, producing more forced career and life pivots than any decade since the 1970s — AI dislocation in white-collar work, the climate-driven relocation of millions, the geopolitical realignment of supply chains, the post-pandemic readjustment of family structures. The Stoic literature has spent fifteen years framing amor fati as a posture toward unwelcome events. The 2026 framing is operationally sharper: it treats the unwelcome event not as something to accept but as the new raw material the operator's actual mission is now made of. Expect this framing to migrate from the Daily Stoic readership into mainstream CBT-Stoic synthesis (Donald Robertson, Massimo Pigliucci) and from there into clinical practice for adjustment disorder and grief work. The doctrinal evolution is fast and the next book or major essay on this will be one of the most-shared pieces of the year.

Behavioural Diagnosis

"Trust the Habits Over the Ambitions" Will Become the Dominant Hiring and Investment Heuristic

Parrish's compression of the habits-versus-ambitions diagnostic into a one-line operating rule is consistent with a broader 2026 shift in how decision-makers are evaluating people and companies. The 2010s and early 2020s privileged the stated narrative — the founder's pitch, the candidate's vision, the executive's strategy memo. The empirical record on those narratives (the seed-stage failure rate, the executive-hire failure rate at eighteen months) has been bad enough for long enough that the smart-money cohort is now privileging the behavioural record: founder's prior behaviour at the previous company, candidate's prior behaviour in interim roles, executive's actual decisions in their last quarter. Expect this to become explicit in due-diligence frameworks and hiring rubrics through 2027, and to depress the multiple paid for charismatic-but-untested operators. The 50-small-things model, paired with the trust-the-habits move, is the operating posture of the late-cycle capital allocators and is becoming widely diffused.

Discourse

The "Easier is Not Better" Lens Will Reshape the Productivity-Tool Discourse Through 2028

Newport's central thesis — that ease and quality are different measurements, and that the 2023–2025 discourse confused them — is part of a broader correction now visible in adjacent literatures. The longevity field has been making the same argument (the "comfort crisis" framing — that physical ease produces deconditioning, and that physiological challenge is the actual input). The education literature has been making the same argument (the "desirable difficulties" research from Robert Bjork's lab: easier learning produces worse retention; harder learning produces durable knowledge). The 2026 productivity discourse is in the early stages of importing this frame against the AI-saturation tools. Expect "desirable difficulty" to become a productivity-discourse term of art by 2027, with explicit application to writing, problem-solving, and decision-making contexts where AI substitution is most tempting. The firms and operators that build deliberately friction-preserving workflows will outperform the friction-eliminating ones on the metrics that matter.

Convergence

The Through-Line: "The Hard Version Is the Real Version" Across Four Otherwise Unrelated Sources This Week

Holiday on the adaptive mission: the original plan was easier because it did not yet contain the obstacle; the real plan is the one with the obstacle absorbed into it, which is harder and which is also the one Marcus is recommending. Newport on AI-assisted writing: the easier version (the AI-drafted manuscript) is empirically worse on the metric that matters (reader comprehension, desk-rejection rate); the harder version (the human-drafted manuscript) is the one that performs. Manson on reliability: the easier version is the rationalised explanation for why you missed; the harder version is showing up, doing what you said, owning the mistake — and the harder version is the cheat code precisely because almost nobody runs it. Parrish on habits over ambitions: the easier version is to honour the stated ambition; the harder version is to honour the behavioural record, which is usually less flattering. Four sources, four domains, one operating posture: the easy version is producing the wrong answer in a system that no longer rewards what easy used to reward. The hard version — harder writing, harder reliability, harder honesty about behaviour, harder adaptation to the actual conditions — is the one whose payoff curve compounds. Read alongside last week's "act inside what cannot be controlled" convergence, the picture sharpens further: control what you can act on, act on the harder version, refuse the easier substitute. The late-2020s operating posture is taking shape.
