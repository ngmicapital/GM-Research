Constraint Decay — backend code generation fragility under accumulating requirements

Research Workflow

A May 2026 arXiv paper introduces "constraint decay" — the empirical finding that LLM agents perform strongly on autonomous code generation under loose specifications, but performance declines substantially as structural requirements accumulate. The argument: each additional constraint (testing, logging, performance, security, style) compounds error-rate exponentially, not additively, in the absence of explicit per-constraint validation.

⚡ Implication: Directly relevant for any briefings-site work where multiple constraints are stacked (HTML structure + no-repeat rule + signal tags + design system + source citation + skim lines). The paper's implicit recommendation aligns with the Outcomes-rubric / Multiagent-orchestration architecture: split the constraint set into per-rubric checks executed by separate grader contexts, rather than asking one agent to satisfy all constraints in a single pass. TPU 8i deep-dive — 80% better perf/$ vs Ironwood, 11.6 exaflops FP8/pod, 3× SRAM ⚡

Update Workflow 1,152 chips/pod 331.8TB HBM/pod 19.2 Tbps scale-up/chip

Per the Google Cloud Blog technical deep-dive and ServeTheHome / NextPlatform / DCD indexed coverage: TPU 8i is the inference-specialised twin to the TPU 8t training chip (announced April 22 at Cloud Next, covered in Issue #41 via the $200B story). The deep-dive numbers worth recording: 1,152 chips per pod, 11.6 exaflops of FP8 compute, 331.8TB HBM capacity per pod, 19.2 Tbps bidirectional scale-up bandwidth per chip, 3× on-chip SRAM vs Ironwood. The architectural significance: the 3× SRAM expansion is specifically designed so the KV cache for long-context decoding can live entirely on-silicon, eliminating the off-chip memory roundtrips that dominate latency on long-context inference. The deliverable: 80% better performance-per-dollar than Ironwood — this is the chip behind the "TPU 40–50% cheaper per token than NVIDIA GPU" claim that was the load-bearing piece of the Anthropic $200B deal (Issue #44).

🔬 Plain-English Explainer — Why TPU 8i actually matters (not just specs)

A regular AI chip ("GPU") spends a lot of its time waiting. When the model is generating an answer, especially for long conversations, it needs to constantly look back at everything that's been said so far — the "context". That memory of the conversation gets stored in something called a KV cache, and on a standard GPU the cache is too big to fit on the chip itself, so the chip has to keep reaching off the chip into slower memory to look at it. That round-trip is what makes inference slow and expensive — most of the chip's time is spent waiting, not computing.

Google's new TPU 8i fixes this specific bottleneck. It has three times more on-chip memory than the previous generation, which is enough to hold the entire KV cache for long conversations on the chip itself. No round trips. The chip computes more, waits less. The headline result: 80% better performance-per-dollar than the previous TPU generation, and reportedly 40–50% cheaper per token than the equivalent NVIDIA GPU.

Why this matters for the bigger picture: Anthropic just committed $200B over five years to buy TPU 8i capacity. That's not a hedge or a diversification play — that's locking in the cheapest inference cost on the market for the entire duration of Claude's 2026–2028 growth curve. Every time you use Claude, the underlying economics get a little better, which is why Anthropic has been able to double Pro and Max rate limits while keeping prices flat. For the AI-DePIN crypto thesis (AKT, RENDER, TAO), TPU 8i changes the competitive landscape but doesn't kill the thesis — decentralised compute networks still win on the workloads that can't run on Google Cloud TPUs (open-source models, data-sovereignty requirements, latency-sensitive edge inference), so the demand floor remains intact.
