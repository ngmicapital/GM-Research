Bullish-Equiniti $4.2 B Definitive Agreement Creates First Blockchain-Native Transfer Agent — 3,000 Issuer Clients, 20 M Shareholders, $500 B Annual Payment Flow Inherits SEC-Registered TA Status and FCA-Regulated UK Operations

"Tokenization is a once-in-a-generation shift in how capital markets operate, the defining infrastructure trend of the next 25 years." — Tom Farley, CEO, Bullish, in 5 May 2026 transaction announcement

Bullish (NYSE: BLSH), the Peter Thiel- and Founders Fund-backed crypto exchange, announced on 5 May 2026 a definitive agreement to acquire Equiniti from Siris Capital in a $4.2 billion transaction comprising $1.85 billion of assumed Equiniti debt and approximately $2.35 billion of Bullish Class A common stock at $38.48 per share. The combined entity will be marketed as the world's first fully integrated, blockchain-enabled, blue-chip issuer-services platform — placing a regulated SEC-registered transfer agent and FCA-supervised UK shareholder-services operator inside the same corporate structure as a CFTC-registered crypto derivatives venue and a tokenisation infrastructure stack. Close is expected January 2027 subject to SEC, FCA, US antitrust, and Equiniti shareholder approvals.

Equiniti currently serves nearly 3,000 issuer clients (15,000 corporate clients in total), administers shareholder records for 20 million end-investors, and processes approximately $500 billion of annual payments. The transaction is the first in which an SEC-registered transfer agent of this scale is being placed inside a tokenisation-native parent — creating a corporate vehicle that can plausibly clear, settle, register, and pay tokenised securities from a single regulatory perimeter. Pro-forma 2026 adjusted revenue is projected at ~$1.3 billion with $500+ million adjusted EBITDA less Capex; 2027–2029 revenue growth is guided at 6–8% annually with a 20% growth contribution attributed specifically to tokenisation services.

The transaction lands inside a regulatory window that finally permits the architecture: the SEC's March 2026 no-action letter to DTCC clearing tokenised-securities pilot operations, the SEC-CFTC March 11 MOU on shared jurisdiction, and the SEC's anticipated Reg Crypto rulemaking (Atkins, March 17 interpretive release; Project Crypto innovation exemption operational since January 2026). Combined, these allow a transfer agent to maintain a tokenised share register on a permissioned ledger and reconcile to DTCC without breaching Section 17A or 17Ad-15 obligations.

📎 Historical Precedent: Closest analogue is Computershare's 2017 acquisition of Equiniti's predecessor business; the difference is that Computershare bought workflow, while Bullish is buying a regulatory wrapper for tokenised-issuer infrastructure that did not legally exist until 2026.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Sullivan & Cromwell's TA-regulation guidance and Davis Polk's January 2026 client alert on transfer-agent tokenisation, the transaction's regulatory ratchet is that an SEC-registered TA carries pre-existing Section 17A authorisations and book-entry recordkeeping obligations — meaning a Bullish-Equiniti tokenised share register can plug into DTCC's existing pilot rails without a fresh exemptive order. The Brief's read: the deal is structurally pre-empting the next 18 months of DTCC tokenisation pilot expansion. Whoever owns the TA layer in 2027 owns the access point to issuer-of-record functions; competitor exchanges (Coinbase, Kraken, Gemini) without a TA will need either to acquire one (Computershare, Broadridge) or to license one — the M&A premium just got materially repriced.

The expo runs 7–9 May with ~20,000 attendees from federal agencies, defence, academia, and the private sector. Per the SCSP schedule and SEC newsroom advance, Atkins' remarks centred on the agency's posture toward AI in capital-markets supervision — extending the "Project Crypto" innovation-exemption framework (operational January 2026, four-pathway taxonomy: digital commodities, digital collectibles, digital tools, tokenised securities) into AI-specific use cases.

The two material policy threads: (1) the SEC is actively considering an internal AI sandbox to test surveillance, examinations, and rulemaking-impact-modelling tools (Atkins floated this at the FSOC AI Innovation Series Roundtable, 4 March 2026); (2) the SEC is signalling appetite for an AI-specific exemption analogue to Project Crypto's startup safe harbour — time-limited, capped, principles-based disclosure for AI-adjacent issuance (e.g., AI compute REIT structures, AI-output tokenised data products). On 4 May at the Milken Institute Global Conference, Atkins separately disclosed the SEC is investigating alleged fraud in private credit, monitoring alongside Treasury and the Federal Reserve — connecting AI-driven private-credit underwriting to the next enforcement wedge.

📎 Historical Precedent: The internal-sandbox concept echoes the FCA's 2016 Regulatory Sandbox (which 850+ firms have entered) and the OCC's 2024 fintech innovation pilot — but no US securities regulator has previously operated an internal AI sandbox for its own staff use.

CFTC Files Federal Preemption Suits Against New York (24 April) and Wisconsin (28 April) — Five-Front Federal-State War Over Event Contracts; New York AG Letitia James 21 April Lawsuit Against Coinbase Financial Markets and Gemini Titan Now the Test Vehicle

"Gambling by another name is still gambling, and it is not exempt from regulation under our state laws." — New York Attorney General Letitia James, official press release, 21 April 2026

The CFTC's federal-preemption strategy is now operating on five fronts: New York (filed 24 April 2026, SDNY), Wisconsin (28 April 2026, WDWI), and previously-filed parallel actions in Illinois, California, and Arizona. The CFTC complaint posture in each is identical: declaratory judgment that Section 5h of the Commodity Exchange Act and Section 1a definitions grant exclusive federal jurisdiction over event contracts traded on CFTC-designated contract markets (DCMs), with permanent injunctive relief barring state gambling-law enforcement against CFTC-registered DCMs. The CFTC press release on the New York filing (release 9218-26) framed it as an action to "preserve" exclusive jurisdiction; the Wisconsin filing (release 9220-26) was filed in direct response to Wisconsin's lawsuits against Kalshi, Polymarket, Crypto.com, Robinhood, and Coinbase.

The catalyst was New York Attorney General Letitia James' 21 April 2026 separate state-court suits against Coinbase Financial Markets, Inc. (NY v. Coinbase Financial Markets) and Gemini Titan LLC (NY v. Gemini Titan), filed in Manhattan state court alleging the prediction-markets product is unlicensed gambling under New York Penal Law §225 and that both companies marketed to 18–20-year-olds in violation of New York's 21+ mobile-betting age floor. AG James seeks forfeiture of profits, restitution, fines equal to three times illegal profits, and operational halts. The same day the CFTC filed its New York suit, AG James joined a 38-state amicus brief supporting Massachusetts' parallel Kalshi suit. State AGs (41 in total, led by Ohio AG Dave Yost) had already filed a coordinated comment in the now-closed CFTC ANPRM docket arguing the CFTC's jurisdiction is not exclusive over sports-related event contracts.

📎 Historical Precedent: Closest analogue is National Labor Relations Bd. v. Catholic Bishop of Chicago (1979) federal-preemption posture — and more directly, Murphy v. NCAA (2018), which held PASPA's anti-commandeering prohibition unconstitutional. The reverse posture here (federal claims preemption over state gambling enforcement) inverts Murphy's analytical frame.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Norton Rose Fulbright's prediction-markets preemption alert and Steptoe's federal-state-clash analysis, the CFTC's posture is procedurally robust because Section 5h(g) of the CEA explicitly declares CFTC jurisdiction "exclusive." The hard question is whether sports-related event contracts qualify as "event contracts" under §1a or as "gaming" under §5c(c)(5)(C) (which the CFTC has not formally exempted). The Brief's read: the SDNY New York suit is the test vehicle. If Judge [TBD] grants the CFTC's preliminary injunction within 60 days, the other four state actions will fold quickly — a pattern Selig anticipated at Consensus Miami when he predicted the fight reaches SCOTUS. If the CFTC loses preliminary relief in even one venue, expect cascading state-attorney-general enforcement and a substantively different prediction-markets compliance pathway by Q4 2026.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Per the 41-state AG joint comment letter and a Brookings Institution analysis on dual-sovereignty enforcement, state gambling laws are core police-power matters under the Tenth Amendment — the CFTC's exclusive-jurisdiction claim has not been tested at SCOTUS for state-court anti-gambling enforcement against federally-registered exchanges. The Brief's read: the strongest counter is Hess v. Port Authority Trans-Hudson Corp. (1994) — the CFTC's preemption claim depends on Congress having clearly displaced state authority, and Section 5h(g) was drafted in 2010 with derivatives in mind, not 2024-vintage retail event contracts. A textualist court could plausibly read the statute as not displacing state gambling enforcement against marketing to under-21 users, even on CFTC-DCM venues. The CFTC's losing scenario is real.

Indirect but material: DraftKings (DKNG), FanDuel (Flutter — FLUT), MGM (MGM) — if state gambling enforcement holds, prediction markets get pushed onto state-licensed sportsbook rails, dramatically narrowing addressable market for unlicensed entrants. State lottery vendors (Scientific Games — private, IGT) — adjacent winners. FinCEN-OFAC PPSI AML/Sanctions Programme NPRM — First-Ever Joint Anti-Money-Laundering and Sanctions Compliance Framework for Permitted Payment Stablecoin Issuers; Comment Window Closes 9 June 2026

"OFAC has explicitly required, for the first time, that a specific category of financial institutions maintain an effective sanctions compliance program — and that PPSIs maintain the capability to block transactions that would violate U.S. sanctions or otherwise be unlawful, including on the secondary market." [paraphrased from Covington & Burling client alert] — Covington & Burling LLP "Five Things to Know" alert, April 2026

FinCEN and OFAC jointly published the Permitted Payment Stablecoin Issuer (PPSI) AML/CFT and Sanctions Compliance Programme NPRM in the Federal Register on 8 April 2026 (vol. 91 FR 20374, document 2026-06963). The rule implements the GENIUS Act's directive treating PPSIs as financial institutions for Bank Secrecy Act purposes. The architecture is novel in three respects: (1) it requires PPSIs to maintain an "effective" AML/CFT programme covering risk-based internal controls, independent testing, designated AML/CFT officer, ongoing training, and customer due diligence — extending not just to direct customers but, expressly, to secondary-market transaction monitoring on permissionless ledgers; (2) it is the first NPRM in OFAC's history that requires a specific financial-institution category to maintain a sanctions compliance programme as a regulatory matter (rather than enforcement-incentivised best practice); (3) it requires PPSIs to maintain the technical capability to block sanctions-violating transactions, including on the secondary market — operationalising the freeze-and-burn obligations that GENIUS imposes statutorily.

Comment period closes 9 June 2026; the GENIUS Act's effective date triggers as the earlier of 18 months after enactment (i.e., 18 January 2027) or 120 days after the primary stablecoin regulators issue final regulations.

📎 Historical Precedent: Closest analogue is FinCEN's 2014 virtual-currency MSB guidance (FIN-2014-R001) — but that imposed AML obligations only on custodial intermediaries, not issuers. PPSI rule extends the perimeter to issuance.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per the Holland & Knight, DLA Piper, and Morrison Foerster client alerts, the substantive load is on the secondary-market sanctions-block requirement — the technical question is whether issuers must maintain a real-time blocklist deployed at the smart-contract level (most likely interpretation) or a post-hoc reconciliation against OFAC SDN updates. The Brief's read: Circle (USDC), USA₮ (Tether/Anchorage), Paxos (PYUSD/USDP), and First Digital (FDUSD if registered as PPSI) need to be running active SDN screening at the address-level by 9 June comment close — not as a compliance gesture, but because comment letters that argue secondary-market screening is "technically infeasible" require the firm to demonstrate it has actually tried. Stablecoin issuers without smart-contract pause functionality already deployed are operationally behind.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Direct: Circle (CRCL), Paxos (private), First Digital (private), USA₮ (Anchorage Digital — Tether-affiliated). Indirect: Chainalysis, TRM Labs, Elliptic — analytical-tools sector gains a regulatory mandate; expect 30–50% pricing power expansion on PPSI compliance contracts. Custodians: Anchorage Digital, BitGo, Fireblocks, Coinbase Custody — competitive pressure intensifies on sanctions-screening native capability. Adverse: stablecoin issuers without programmatic pause/freeze (older-generation USDT contracts on Tron, for example) face fundamental architectural rewrites.

Promoting Innovation in Blockchain Development Act 2026 (HR 7332) — Bipartisan Bill Carves Non-Custodial Software Developers Out of 18 USC §1960 Money-Transmitter Liability; In Judiciary Committee Following Tornado Cash and Samourai Convictions

HR 7332 (Promoting Innovation in Blockchain Development Act of 2026), introduced 26 February 2026 by Reps. Scott Fitzgerald (R-WI), Ben Cline (R-VA), and Zoe Lofgren (D-CA), narrowly amends 18 USC §1960 to exempt developers of non-custodial open-source blockchain software from criminal money-transmission liability. The statutory targeting is deliberate: §1960 traditionally covers entities that take custody of customer funds; the bill clarifies the section "should only apply to entities controlling customer funds, not to developers who merely write code." The bill responds directly to the Tornado Cash and Samourai Wallet prosecutions of 2024–2025, where DOJ argued non-custodial protocol developers fell within §1960's reach. The DeFi Education Fund and Blockchain Association have endorsed the bill. The legislation is currently before the House Committee on the Judiciary; no markup yet scheduled.

📎 Historical Precedent: Closest analogue is the 1996 Communications Decency Act §230 — Congress legislated developer liability protection after litigation patterns made the criminalisation pathway untenable. The bill follows that template for blockchain.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Baker McKenzie's "Code is Not a Crime" client alert and the DeFi Education Fund's policy memo, the bill's strategic payoff is asymmetric: even unenacted, its existence reframes any §1960 prosecution of non-custodial developers as inconsistent with Congress's stated direction. The Brief's read: the bill's most likely fate is incorporation as a manager's-amendment to CLARITY rather than passage as a standalone statute — Lofgren's Democratic sponsorship and the bipartisan triad are precisely the legislative profile needed to attach as a CLARITY rider in conference. DeFi developers' practical posture: assume the bill is moving as a CLARITY rider unless and until the markup goes formally to the floor as standalone.
