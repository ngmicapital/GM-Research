Praxis · Intelligence Briefing · 9 May 2026

Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

Four sources circle the same operating question this week — how to act decisively inside conditions you cannot fully control. Daily Stoic on Cato and Ta-Nehisi Coates's "not even Jackie Robinson was always Jackie Robinson": the moral exemplars were never always exemplary, which is permission to keep struggling toward virtue rather than abandoning the project on the first slip. The companion essay extends asymmetric attention to impersonal forces — the economy, the drought, gravity, cancer are not thinking about you, and the Stoic move is to be a little more indifferent to them. James Clear reframes discipline as design, names the identity cost of bad habits, and surfaces three modes of every relationship: Deepening, Holding, Drifting. Mark Manson closes a fifteen-year course business to merge it into his AI coach Purpose, betting face-to-face human connection becomes the only scarcity once AI delivers infinite advice 24/7.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Daily Stoic · Ryan Holiday · 8 May 2026

"Cato Wasn't Always Cato" — Ta-Nehisi Coates and the Moral Permission of Imperfect Heroes

Holiday's Friday entry is the most quietly subversive piece in the Daily Stoic archive this quarter, and it lands by inverting the standard hagiography. The opening paints the Cato of legend: "He was a towering figure even in his own time. He was known for his honesty, his commitment, his self-discipline, and his incorruptibility. Even his enemies and political adversaries couldn't help but be impressed by it. 'We can't all be Catos,' they would say with a shrug." The line is the one Plutarch records and Holiday's earlier writing has used as a closing flourish; this essay opens by undermining it.

The reversal is sharp. Cato was not perfect. He had flaws. He made exceptions. He made mistakes. He lost his temper. He miscalculated. He had an ego. Even his sense of justice — the asset most often cited as his core virtue — was sometimes unyielding to the point of self-righteousness. Holiday names this directly rather than glossing it. The hagiographic version of the Stoics, popular on social media for the last decade, has produced a particular failure mode: readers who, on noticing their own inconsistency, conclude they cannot do philosophy and quit the project.

"Not all of us can always be Jackie Robinson. Not even Jackie Robinson was always Jackie Robinson." — Ta-Nehisi Coates, cited by Ryan Holiday (Daily Stoic, 8 May 2026)

The Coates line is doing the heaviest lifting in the essay. The Robinson reference works on Holiday's American audience because the public memory of Robinson is monolithic — the dignity, the discipline, the refusal to retaliate — and Coates's correction lands precisely because it disturbs that memory without diminishing the man. Robinson, on his own account in I Never Had It Made, was furious for most of his career. He carried the rage privately and discharged it onto his family. The Robinson the country remembers is the Robinson of the public-facing performance, and the public-facing performance was the product, not the man. The man was someone who, for two and a half hours a day, became the version of himself the era required — and was something else the rest of the time.

The implication for ordinary practice is the one most popularisations miss. The Stoic project is not "become Cato" or "become Marcus"; the Stoic project is to become a person who, more often than not, behaves the way Cato behaved on his good days and forgives himself for the rest. The intermittent excellence is the realistic target. The continuous excellence is a mythology that produces, in actual operators, either fraud or quitting. Holiday closes with the only line that follows from the inversion: "In any case, we'll get better for trying, just as they did." The "as they did" is the entire essay. The exemplars also got better for trying, which means they were not always good — which means the rest of us are not disqualified by being inconsistent. We are doing the same project on the same terms. Permission, granted.

The pairing with Coates is also a small piece of bibliographic strategy worth noticing. The mainstream Stoic literature has been criticised for years — not unfairly — for citing only white men and pre-Industrial sources. Holiday has been quietly correcting this across his last three books (Right Thing, Right Now in particular), and the Robinson-via-Coates reference is one example among many of the broader sourcing the modern Stoic synthesis is undertaking. The point is not virtue-signalling; it is that the moral examples must come from outside the original canon if the doctrine is to remain useful in the present, and the work of finding those examples is part of the work of keeping the tradition alive.

Daily Stoic · Ryan Holiday · 7 May 2026

"It Can't Think About You" — Asymmetric Attention, Now Applied to Impersonal Forces

Holiday's Thursday companion essay is short and does one thing well: it extends the previous day's interpersonal framing to the impersonal terrain that consumes most actual cognitive bandwidth. Wednesday's piece — the Don Draper "I don't think about you at all" entry — was about people who are not thinking about you. The Thursday piece names the larger category. "It does so much damage. It sets us back so much. It's something we desperately don't want to happen. So yeah, we think about it a lot. We fret about it a lot. We dread it a lot." The opening is deliberately unspecific because the move that follows applies regardless of which "it" the reader fills in.

"Just as Nero was not thinking about Seneca, the economy is not thinking about you. This drought is not scheming to disrupt your plans. The airport isn't trying to make you miss your flight and thus your meeting and therefore screw up your career. The highway doesn't care. Neither does gravity or cancer." — Ryan Holiday, "It Can't Think About You" (Daily Stoic, 7 May 2026)

The list is doing more work than it appears. Each item is a force that, for most operators, occupies a meaningful fraction of weekly anxiety: macro conditions, weather, infrastructure, traffic, mortality, biology. The taxonomy is the lesson. None of these forces have intent. None of them are targeting you. None of them are favouring or punishing you. The Stoic distinction Holiday is sharpening here is older than the Stoics — the Bhagavad Gita has the same observation, the Book of Job has the same observation — but the modern operator's confusion of impersonal adversity with personal injury is the source of an enormous quantity of the suffering Holiday's audience reports.

The instruction follows from the diagnosis. "These things are indifferent to you. Your goal is to be a little more indifferent to them." The line is restrained — not "be entirely indifferent", which is impossible and would be a failure of judgement anyway, but a little more indifferent. The calibration is what makes the practice survivable. The economy is not your enemy and not your friend; it is weather. The drought is not a punishment; it is climate. The airport is not personal; it is logistics. The cancer is not deserved; it is biology. In each case the corrective is to extract whatever can be planned and acted on, then to refuse the additional layer of narrative that turns the impersonal force into a personal antagonist.

The synthesis with Wednesday's piece is the move worth keeping. Across the two essays Holiday has named the full perimeter of asymmetric attention: the people who are not thinking about you, the forces that cannot think about you. Most operators waste cognitive bandwidth in the first category; most operators waste much more bandwidth in the second. The annual cost — the hours spent narrativising market conditions, weather, traffic, geopolitics, biology — is staggering when audited. The Stoic instruction is not to pretend these things do not affect you, which is denial. It is to refuse the second-order narrativising that converts a neutral force into a personal grudge. The first hour spent on the macro situation is research; the next three are theatre. The discipline is to notice the transition.

The closing image — "we can't let these inhuman forces steal our humanity" — is the essay's quiet centre. The risk is not that the inhuman forces will hurt you; the risk is that you will respond to them by becoming inhuman yourself: bitter, paranoid, transactional, narratively self-centred. The protection is the refusal to take the impersonal personally. It is one of the few Stoic instructions that becomes more valuable the older you get, because the inventory of impersonal forces compounds with age in ways the inventory of antagonistic people does not.

Newsletter · James Clear · 3-2-1 Thursday · 7 May 2026

James Clear on Discipline-as-Design, the Hidden Identity Cost of Bad Habits, and Three Modes of Every Relationship

Clear's Thursday issue is the densest 3-2-1 he has published in months and contains three distinct, separately useful frames. The first is the cleanest restatement of Atomic Habits's central thesis Clear has put on the page: "The disciplined life looks impressive, but the well-designed life lasts longer. Before you try to find more willpower, design your environment and arrange your day so you're more likely to follow through." The instruction is implicit in the book; the compression is new. The discipline-versus-design distinction matters because the cultural gravity of the 2020s pulls toward the former — the morning routine, the cold plunge, the white-knuckle grind — and the empirical record on willpower (Roy Baumeister's ego-depletion findings, Wendy Wood's habit-formation work) is unambiguous: design beats discipline, every time, over any horizon longer than a quarter.

The corollary, which Clear leaves implicit, is the diagnostic. If a goal requires daily willpower three months in, the design is wrong. The right design routes the desired behaviour through the path of least friction and the wrong behaviour through the path of most friction, then steps out of the way. Operators who keep relapsing into a bad pattern are usually not weak; they are operating in environments engineered, by accident or otherwise, to produce that pattern. The fix is environmental, not characterological. It is also unglamorous, which is why the discipline narrative remains popular: discipline is photogenic and design is invisible.

The second frame is the sharpest piece of habit-theory writing Clear has done since the book's release. "The most expensive part of a bad habit is rarely the action itself, but rather the identity it quietly reinforces underneath." The single instance of the bad habit is cheap; the practice of being a person who skips is expensive, because identity is what generalises across decisions. The cost of one missed gym session is one missed gym session. The cost of fifty missed gym sessions is the formation of a self-concept — "I'm someone who skips" — that will quietly veto a thousand future commitments, in adjacent domains, for the rest of the operator's life. The transaction is not visible at the level of the individual instance and that is precisely why it is so costly.

The third frame is the most under-discussed in popular self-improvement literature and arguably the most useful. Clear names three modes that every long-term relationship is in at any given moment: Deepening (the relationship is more intimate this year than last), Holding (steady-state, comfortable, unchanged), and Drifting (contact is fading, the relationship is sliding). The taxonomy is descriptive rather than evaluative — the point is not to judge the mode, but to name it for each significant relationship in your life. Most operators have never run the audit. The result is that important relationships drift by default, while less important ones are held in place by habit, and the few that genuinely deepen do so almost by accident.

Clear's question is sharper than the standard relationship advice and easier to act on. "Which relationship is due for a deepening period? Which one are you okay letting drift for now?" The honest version of the answer at age forty is unsettling: most operators have one or two relationships actively deepening, four or five holding, and ten or fifteen drifting — and the drift list contains people the operator would, if asked, describe as important. The framing forces the explicit choice: either the drift is the right answer (in which case make peace with it) or the drift is a default (in which case it is correctable by one phone call this week). The compounding over twenty years is enormous in either direction.

The closing piece — Montesquieu via Mes Pensées: "If you only wished to be happy, this could be easily accomplished; but we wish to be happier than other people, and this is always difficult, for we believe others to be happier than they are" — is the eighteenth-century French Enlightenment stating, with characteristic compression, the diagnosis the modern social-comparison literature has been working toward for thirty years. The wish to be happy is achievable. The wish to be happier than the comparison set is not. The mechanism is the perceived (and overstated) happiness of the comparison set. Two hundred and seventy years before Instagram, Montesquieu had the failure mode named.

Newsletter · Mark Manson · The Breakthrough #209 · 7 May 2026

Mark Manson on Adaptation: Closing a Fifteen-Year Course Business to Bet on AI — and on Face-to-Face Scarcity

Manson's Thursday letter is structurally unusual for The Breakthrough — an autobiographical pivot announcement rather than the typical ideas-letter — and it is the most operationally useful thing in the issue precisely because it shows the model behind a real adaptation decision rather than describing one in the abstract. The setup is the early-career Manson: 2008, twenty-person workshops in dust-filled lofts and budget-hotel conference rooms, scrambling month-to-month for next month's rent, doing the work the era's distribution required. The intervention is one mentor's line: "Instead of travelling so much, you should record one of these and put it online and sell it."

"The world's information was aggressively moving online. Distribution was becoming global. And you could either move with the world, or drown fighting against it. I chose to move with it." — Mark Manson, The Breakthrough #209 (7 May 2026)

The line is the operating principle of Manson's career and is doing the work of the entire essay. Sixteen years later he has built fifteen course offerings, five communities, an audience in the tens of millions, and the personal-development brand that produced two of the bestselling non-fiction books of the 2010s. The bet was right. The bet was also unfashionable at the time it was placed — "my friends bought me beers out of pity, my parents told me I needed to go get a real job" — and the social cost is part of why most operators do not place bets of that magnitude until the bet has stopped being controversial, which is precisely when the bet stops paying.

The pivot Manson is now announcing is the second instance of the same decision under different conditions. Two years ago he noticed users of his content asking AI for life advice. He co-founded Purpose, an AI personal-development coach, with the explicit intent to ship the most carefully trained model in the category. The launch outperformed every prior product launch in his career, including his books. The dynamism of the AI assistant has, in his read, made his existing static content (courses, books, communities) feel comparatively frozen, impersonal, and inapplicable. The honest response is the one most fifteen-year businesses will not take: shut the legacy product down and merge its content into the AI offering.

The decision is operationally instructive because it inverts the usual incumbent failure mode. The standard pattern is for a fifteen-year business with millions in trailing revenue to defend the existing product as the new technology eats its underside. Manson's pattern is the opposite: he is closing the trailing-revenue business to consolidate into the disruptor while he still owns the disruptor. The tell is the framing of the decision. He does not describe the legacy products as still working; he describes them as "clearly not the best one to do it". The ego-protective move would have been to keep both running indefinitely; the adaptation move is to retire the inferior one decisively. The discipline is recognising the moment without rationalising it away.

The closing thesis — the part of the essay most worth keeping — is the future-state argument. Manson's read is that within a small number of years, ubiquitous personal AI agents will deliver perfectly personalised advice in every moment. Information will become abundant in a way it has never been. The remaining scarcity, in his model, will be face-to-face human connection — the only thing the AI agents cannot fully replicate. The implication is unfashionable for an AI evangelist: in the AI-saturated future, the premium asset is the dust-filled budget-hotel conference room with twenty people, stale coffee, and an actual conversation. The career arc he is announcing is not a flight from the analogue into the digital; it is a circular trip in which the digital becomes the leverage and the analogue becomes the product. The detail is worth noting because it is one of the few defensible strategies for human professionals in a domain that AI is otherwise positioned to dominate.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · From Daily Stoic · "Cato Wasn't Always Cato"

The Imperfection Audit: Letting the Heroes Off the Pedestal So You Can Get Back On the Field

Holiday's hero-deconstruction converts into a personal practice that runs in about thirty minutes and is unusually effective at restarting stalled virtue projects. The structure has four steps:

  • Name three figures whose example you have stopped using because the gap between you and them has become demoralising. Marcus Aurelius, Cal Newport, your father at his peak, the colleague who has somehow read everything — whoever the live exemplars are. The discipline is to name the people whose example you have quietly retired, not the ones you still cite. The retirement is the symptom.
  • For each, find one well-documented failure, weakness, or moment of inconsistency. Marcus losing his temper with Faustina; Newport's own admissions of email lapses; the colleague's chronic procrastination on the project he refuses to start. The failure does not diminish the example; it humanises it. Most exemplars have published the failure themselves; the discipline is to read it.
  • Re-derive the example from the imperfect version, not the perfect version. The instruction Marcus gave himself was not "be perfect"; it was "be a little better than you were yesterday, on average, with grace for the slips". Newport's instruction was not "always avoid email"; it was "build a system that minimises it most days, with grace for the rest". The exemplar is the imperfect operator who kept showing up. That is the actual model.
  • Resume the practice you stopped, this week, at the lower-bar version. Not the heroic version; the maintainable version. Twenty minutes of writing, not three hours. Two strict gym days, not five. One difficult conversation, not the full backlog. The lower bar is the one the imperfect exemplar would have hit, on average. Hit it. The compounding takes care of the rest.
Practice · From Daily Stoic · "It Can't Think About You"

The Indifference Calibration: Separating Impersonal Adversity from Personal Narrative

Holiday's "a little more indifferent" instruction is, despite its modesty, a precise budgeting exercise. The practice runs once a week and produces a noticeable reduction in ambient anxiety within a month:

  • Name the three impersonal forces consuming the most cognitive bandwidth this week. Macro conditions, geopolitical news, weather, traffic, biology, infrastructure failures, market moves. One short phrase per item. The discipline is recognising the inventory; most operators have never explicitly named the items burning their bandwidth.
  • For each, separate the research minutes from the narrative minutes. Research minutes are the time spent gathering information that will inform an action. Narrative minutes are the time spent constructing a story in which the impersonal force is targeting you. Honest accounting usually puts the ratio at 1:5 or worse. The narrative is what the indifference instruction is aimed at, not the research.
  • Identify, for each force, the one action available to you this week. The macro: rebalance the position, or do nothing. The biology: schedule the test, or do nothing. The traffic: leave fifteen minutes earlier, or do nothing. The discipline is finding the action and recognising that everything beyond it is theatre. Some weeks the right action is "do nothing"; that is also an action.
  • Pre-commit to a redirect when the narrative loop starts. The intrusive thought about the macro gets routed, by rule, to a five-minute walk or to opening a specific file. The redirect is the discipline; the substitution is what extinguishes the loop. "They are indifferent to you; be a little more indifferent to them" is the instruction; the redirect is the mechanism that produces the indifference.

The reason the practice works is not that it eliminates the worry — worry is informative and most operators need a small amount of it. The practice works because it removes the second-order worry: the worry about the worry, the narrative scaffolding, the imagined personal injury where there is only impersonal weather. The first-order worry is research; the second-order worry is theatre. Cutting the theatre frees up two to four hours a week of cognitive bandwidth that mostly goes back into craft and family. Annualised, this is among the highest-leverage personal practices in this newsletter's archive.

Practice · From James Clear · 3-2-1 Thursday

The Relationship Triage: Sorting Your People into Deepening, Holding, and Drifting

Clear's three-mode taxonomy converts directly into a relationship audit that runs once a quarter and is unusually durable because the underlying truth, once seen, is hard to look away from:

  • List the twenty people whose presence in your life matters most. Family, close friends, mentors, the one or two colleagues whose opinion you actually trust, the people who would notice if you went dark for a month. Twenty is not a magic number; the list typically falls between twelve and twenty-five. The exercise breaks if the list is curated; write the names that come up in the first three minutes.
  • For each name, mark D (Deepening), H (Holding), or F (Drifting). Deepening: you know each other better this year than last. Holding: comfortable steady-state. Drifting: contact is fading. The discipline is to mark honestly — the H category is the most over-claimed because it feels safer than F. Most operators discover three D's, six to eight H's, and the rest F.
  • For each F, ask: is the drift the right answer, or the default? Some drifts are right — the relationship has run its course, the values diverged, the geography won. Mark these "accept". Some drifts are defaults — the relationship matters and is decaying because no one has done the small work of maintenance. Mark these "reverse". The reverse list is usually three to six names.
  • Pre-commit to one specific contact per name on the reverse list, this week. Not "I'll call them sometime"; the call, the message, the dinner invitation, scheduled. The reversal of a drift is almost always cheaper than the operator predicts — one specific contact per quarter, sustained, will move a relationship from F back into H, and a small fraction of those will move into D. The compounding over five years is one of the most undervalued sources of life satisfaction in the empirical happiness literature.
Practice · From Mark Manson · The Breakthrough #209

The Adaptation Bet: Inverting the Incumbent Failure Mode in Your Own Career

Manson's "move with the world or drown fighting it" is the operating principle behind a personal-career exercise that takes about ninety minutes and is best run once a year. The exercise forces the question most professionals avoid:

  • Name the technology, distribution change, or cultural shift that will most change your industry in the next five years. AI for knowledge work, generative video for media, on-chain settlement for finance, distributed agents for software. One specific shift, named precisely. If you cannot name one, the audit fails — you have not been paying attention.
  • For each line of revenue or value you currently produce, ask: is this upstream of the shift (it benefits), orthogonal to it (it is unaffected), or downstream (it is eaten)? Be honest. The downstream lines are the ones to name explicitly, because the rationalising voice will resist the naming.
  • For each downstream line, set a sunset date. Not "manage the decline"; a date by which you will have transitioned the time and capital out. Manson's version is closing the courses by month-end and merging the content into Purpose. The discipline is the date. Without a date the line will be defended past the point of usefulness because the trailing revenue is too easy to keep collecting.
  • Identify the one new bet that places you upstream of the shift. The new product, role, skill, or platform that benefits from the change rather than fighting it. Manson's version was the AI coach two years ago; before that, it was the online course in 2008. The bet should be uncomfortable enough that your peers buy you beers out of pity. If it is not, the bet is too small.

The exercise produces, on most operators, two to four downstream lines and one upstream bet. The standard failure mode is to make the bet but never sunset the lines, which produces a portfolio that looks diversified but is actually paying the rent on legacy infrastructure that will be obsolete inside a decade. Manson's pattern — close the legacy decisively, consolidate into the disruptor while you still own it — is the harder discipline. It is also, on the empirical record of fifteen-year careers, the one that produces continued relevance into the next era. Most professionals do this once if they are lucky. Doing it a second time, before the first bet has fully expired, is the operating posture of the careers that compound for forty years rather than fifteen.

04 On the Horizon
Stoic Practice

"Imperfect Exemplars" Will Become the Defining Move of the 2026–2027 Modern Stoic Synthesis

Holiday's Cato-and-Coates pairing is one of several signals that the popular Stoic literature is in the middle of a quiet self-correction. The 2018–2024 era of Daily Stoic, The Obstacle Is the Way, and the broader social-media Stoicism produced a hagiographic register that has been repeatedly criticised for setting unattainable bars and for citing too narrow a canon. The next phase — visible in Holiday's Right Thing, Right Now, in Massimo Pigliucci's recent work, in Donald Robertson's psychotherapeutic synthesis — foregrounds the failures, inconsistencies, and contradictions of the original Stoics, and pulls examples from a much broader cast: Robinson, Coates, Mandela, Stockdale, Frankl, Mary Wollstonecraft. Expect the next twelve to eighteen months of popular Stoic publishing to be dominated by books and essays that explicitly do this work, and a parallel decline in the "be Marcus" hagiography that has become difficult to defend.

Cognitive Hygiene

"Impersonal Indifference" Is Emerging as the Sharpest Modern Restatement of Classical Apatheia

Holiday's two-part essay this week (Wednesday on people, Thursday on impersonal forces) is part of a larger reformulation of the Stoic concept of apatheia — freedom from disordered passion — for a present in which the dominant adversities are not personal antagonists but algorithmic, climatic, biological, and macroeconomic. The classical formulation was abstract; the modern restatement — "the economy is not thinking about you, the drought is not scheming against you" — is concrete and actionable. Expect this framing to migrate from popular philosophy into mainstream cognitive-behavioural therapy in the next two to three years; Donald Robertson's CBT-Stoic synthesis (How to Think Like a Roman Emperor, St. Martin's, 2019) is already the bridge text. The clinical literature on health-related anxiety, climate anxiety, and macro-economic anxiety in particular maps onto this framing with unusual precision.

Relationships

The "Deepening / Holding / Drifting" Taxonomy Is the Senior-Career Skill That Books Have Not Yet Named

Clear's three-mode classification is sharper than the existing relationship-management literature has been because it is descriptive rather than evaluative and because it forces the explicit choice of which drifts are accepted and which are reversed. The forty-and-up cohort is the one that needs this most: empirical work on midlife well-being (the Harvard Grant Study, the work coming out of the Stanford Centre on Longevity, Robert Waldinger's The Good Life) consistently identifies relationship quality as the dominant predictor of late-life satisfaction, and yet there is no serious popular framework for actively managing the trajectory of relationships at the portfolio level. The framework Clear has just named is the cleanest one in current circulation. Watch for the first long-form treatment of it — the form is open, the audience is large, and the empirical scaffolding is already in place.

Adaptation

The "Close the Legacy, Consolidate Into the Disruptor" Move Will Define the Surviving Personal Brands of 2026–2030

Manson's pivot — closing fifteen years of course offerings to merge into his own AI coach — is one of the sharpest moves a major personal brand has made in the AI era and will, on the available evidence, be widely emulated. The standard incumbent failure mode (defend the legacy product as the new technology eats its underside) is well-documented from Kodak through Blockbuster through traditional media, and the personal-brand equivalent is to keep selling the static course while AI-coached competitors quietly absorb the customer base. Manson is making the move while he still owns the disrupting product. Expect at least three major personal-development brands — Tony Robbins's organisation, the Mindvalley network, the larger online-course catalogues — to face the same decision in the next eighteen months. The ones that close legacy decisively will compound; the ones that defend the trailing revenue will be eaten.

Scarcity

"Face-to-Face Human Connection" Is Quietly Becoming the Premium Asset Class of the Late 2020s

Manson's closing thesis — that ubiquitous personal AI agents will commoditise information and personalised advice, leaving face-to-face human connection as the only remaining scarcity — is the sharpest version of an argument now visible in multiple corners. The high-end coaching market is repricing upward against a backdrop of free GPT therapy. The premium conference market (Lambda Conferences, Skybridge events, the surviving private retreats) is hitting record prices and waitlists. The "third place" literature is reviving. The economic structure of high-end residential real estate increasingly reflects the value of physical proximity to interesting people, not the value of the building. Manson is naming, in retail terms, what hedge-fund-adjacent operators have been pricing for two years. Expect the "premium analogue" thesis to become the dominant lens on consumer luxury, hospitality, education, and high-end professional services through 2028, with the AI-saturated digital becoming the substrate against which the analogue is priced as scarcity.

Convergence

"Acting Inside Conditions You Cannot Control" Is the Operating Question Underneath Four Otherwise Unrelated Sources This Week

Holiday on Cato: the conditions you cannot control include your own imperfection, and the move is to act virtuously anyway, on average, with grace for the slips. Holiday on impersonal forces: the conditions you cannot control include the economy, the drought, gravity, biology, and the move is to extract the action and refuse the second-order narrative. Clear on relationships: the conditions you cannot control include drift, decay, and the slow gravity of geography — and the move is to name the modes and pick the reversals. Manson on adaptation: the conditions you cannot control include the technology curve and the death of categories — and the move is to close the legacy and consolidate into the disruptor while you still own it. Four sources, four traditions, one operating posture: locate the agency inside the constraint, act on it decisively, refuse the rest.
