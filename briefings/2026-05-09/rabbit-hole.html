The Outlaws Who Invented Worker Democracy — a Century Before the Labour Movement

How the most violent criminals of the 18th century were forced by market competition to invent constitutions, elected officers, separation of powers, and workplace injury compensation

Golden Age pirates operated under written constitutions with elected captains, separation of powers, and paid compensation for battlefield injuries — a century before such protections existed for most European workers. The reason wasn't idealism: it was market competition for crew, and economist Peter Leeson showed in 2009 that the same invisible hand driving legitimate markets was operating on pirate ships all along.

Section 01

The Story

In 1724, a writer going by "Captain Charles Johnson" published A General History of the Robberies and Murders of the Most Notorious Pyrates — a book that remains the primary source for almost everything we know about the Golden Age of Piracy. Buried inside it, quoted in full, were the "articles of agreement" that pirate ships used to govern themselves. Most readers absorbed them as curiosities: the rules of colourful criminals. What they actually represented was one of the most sophisticated experiments in democratic workplace governance that had ever been written down.

The Golden Age of Piracy ran roughly from 1650 to 1730, but hit its peak in a single extraordinary decade: 1716 to 1726. It was a product of the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713, which ended the War of the Spanish Succession and demobilised tens of thousands of privateers — men licensed by governments to raid enemy shipping — who suddenly found themselves unemployed and adrift in Atlantic ports. Many turned to piracy, and for about a decade the Caribbean and Atlantic trade routes were thick with outlaw ships. Historian Marcus Rediker estimates that between 4,500 and 5,500 pirates were operating at any given moment during the peak years, crewing roughly 25 to 30 ships.

Among them was Bartholomew Roberts — known as Black Bart — who between 1719 and 1722 captured over 400 vessels, making him the most prolific pirate of the entire era. Roberts' ship, the Royal Fortune, operated under a set of ten articles that have survived through Johnson's History. They are worth reading carefully, because they are not the rules of a mob. They are a constitutional document.

Every man had an equal vote in affairs of moment. The captain and quartermaster each received two shares of any prize; the master, boatswain, and gunner received one and a half shares; other petty officers received one and a quarter; everyone else received one share each. Lights and candles had to be extinguished at eight in the evening, and anyone who wanted to drink after that hour had to do so above deck, in the open air. No fighting among themselves was permitted aboard — disputes had to go to arbitration on shore. And then there was Article VI, the one that looks most startling from the vantage of 1724:

"That Man that shall lose a Limb in time of Engagement, shall have the Sum of Six Hundred Pieces of Eight, and remain aboard as long as he shall think fit."

Roberts' articles set out graduated compensation for every major injury: 600 pieces of eight for a right arm, 500 for a left arm or right leg, 400 for a left leg, 100 for an eye, 100 for a finger. This was not a novel idea — it had antecedents in the earlier articles of Henry Morgan and others — but by the early 18th century it had become a standard feature of pirate ships across the Atlantic. The men who made these agreements were illiterate in many cases, formally uneducated in all, and operating entirely outside the law. They had, independently and for purely practical reasons, invented workers' compensation insurance.

Nassau, in the Bahamas, served briefly as the de facto capital of this outlaw world — a harbor town where pirates careened their ships, spent their shares, and recruited new crew. At its peak between roughly 1715 and 1718 it housed some 1,000 pirates and was effectively ungoverned. The British government eventually dispatched Woodes Rogers as governor with a fleet and a standing offer: any pirate who surrendered by September 1718 would receive a full pardon. Some took it. Those who didn't — including Charles Vane and the crew that would later sail with Calico Jack Rackham — were hunted down and hanged. The republic was over in months.

Section 02

The Mechanism

For nearly three centuries, historians treated pirate democracy as an interesting anomaly — perhaps evidence of a counter-cultural spirit among the underclass, or simply a practical adaptation to the peculiar logistics of outlaw life at sea. Then in 2007, an economist named Peter Leeson published a paper titled "An-arrgh-chy: The Law and Economics of Pirate Organization" in the Journal of Political Economy, and in 2009 expanded it into a book called The Invisible Hook. His argument was simple, rigorous, and surprising: pirate democracy was not the product of ideology. It was the product of market competition for labour.

The logic runs as follows. A pirate ship needed a crew. Crew were volunteers — men who chose to join, and who could and did desert when they reached port. This meant pirate captains were in competition with each other, and with the merchant and naval fleets, for the same pool of sailors. To attract and retain skilled men, captains had to offer terms better than the alternatives. The alternatives — particularly the Royal Navy — were notoriously brutal: arbitrary punishment at officers' discretion, no compensation for injury, no share of prize money, impressment (forced conscription) as the recruitment method. A pirate ship, by contrast, offered shares of plunder, a voice in decisions, protection against the captain's whims, and injury pay. These were not generous gifts from idealistic pirates. They were competitive terms in a labour market.

The Principal-Agent Problem, Solved by Articles

In any organisation where one party (the principal) delegates work to another (the agent), there's a risk the agent will shirk or defect when not monitored. Pirate ships faced this acutely: a captain who hoarded plunder or abused crew could not be reported to any authority. The articles were the solution. By binding the captain to written, publicly-known rules — with the quartermaster as an independent enforcer — pirates solved the principal-agent problem through constitutional design two centuries before organisational economists named it.

The separation of powers between captain and quartermaster is especially striking. On a Royal Navy vessel the captain was essentially a sovereign — his word was law, his punishments were immediate and physical, and there was no formal check on his authority short of admiralty court. Pirate ships deliberately split authority. The captain commanded absolutely during chase and battle — moments when unified command was genuinely necessary for survival — but his power reverted to the crew in all other circumstances. The quartermaster, independently elected by the crew, could veto the captain's decisions in peacetime, was chiefly responsible for discipline, and distributed shares of any prize. As Johnson's History records: "the Captain can undertake nothing which the Quarter-Master does not approve." Johnson compared the quartermaster's role to the Roman Tribune of the People — an office created specifically to check the power of the Roman consuls.

The captain himself could be deposed by a majority vote for any reason: cowardice, cruelty, incompetence, or simply losing the crew's confidence. Several documented cases survive. Captain Edward England was deposed by his crew for being too lenient with a captured merchant captain — his men wanted harsher treatment of prisoners, and when England objected, they voted him out, put him ashore on Madagascar with a few supplies, and elected a new captain. The vote was, by all accounts, quick and orderly.

This is Leeson's "invisible hook" — a deliberate inversion of Adam Smith's "invisible hand." Smith argued that individuals pursuing self-interest in competitive markets produce social benefits as an unintended consequence. Leeson's pirates were pursuing self-interest — profit, recruitment advantage, crew retention — and producing democratic governance as an unintended consequence. The same mechanism. A different industry.

Section 03

The Connections

Labour rights predated by a century. The Combination Acts of 1799 and 1800, which formally suppressed trade unions in Britain, were themselves a response to workers organising in the decades after the Industrial Revolution. Chartism — the first mass working-class political movement demanding democratic representation — emerged in the 1830s. Formal workers' compensation legislation arrived in Britain in 1897, in the United States in 1908. Pirate articles containing all of these provisions — elected representation, compensation for workplace injury, written rules constraining employer authority — were in operation on Atlantic ships by at least 1670, and widespread by 1700. The irony is sharp: the most comprehensive workers' rights regime of the 17th and early 18th century was run by criminals who were being hunted and hanged by the governments later credited with inventing those rights.

The limits of the model. Leeson's argument is compelling but worth complicating. Pirate democracy had hard edges. Merchant sailors captured at sea were routinely coerced into joining pirate crews — their "voluntary" enlistment was often a choice between signing articles or being thrown overboard. The egalitarianism of the articles applied to the crew, not to captives or enslaved people taken as prizes. Roberts' articles referred to injury compensation being paid partly in "slaves" — human beings counted as currency equivalent to pieces of eight. The democratic governance that looks so modern from the outside coexisted with practices that were brutally exploitative. This doesn't invalidate Leeson's economic analysis; it contextualises it. The invisible hand produces democratic labour relations among those with market power to demand them, and brutal coercion for those without it.

Governance theory before governance theory. Political scientists today would recognise what pirates designed as a solution to what's called the "commitment problem" in institutional economics: how does a powerful actor credibly commit not to abuse that power? A constitution is the answer — a set of rules that binds the powerful party in advance, enforced by an independent institution. Pirates were writing constitutional solutions to commitment problems roughly 150 years before James Madison formalized the same logic in The Federalist Papers. Madison's No. 51 — "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition" — describes exactly the captain/quartermaster separation of powers that pirate crews had independently arrived at by 1700. The Founders had Montesquieu and Locke. Pirates had competition for sailors.

What the articles can't tell us. Johnson's History is an extraordinary source and almost certainly the most accurate account we have — but it was also, in part, a moral entertainment for a London readership that wanted to be thrilled and reassured simultaneously. Articles are prescriptive documents: they describe what crews agreed to, not necessarily what they did. Enforcement varied. Some captains respected the articles scrupulously; others appear to have ignored them when convenient. What the articles prove is that pirate crews thought carefully about governance, wrote down their solutions, and built independent enforcement mechanisms — even if the gap between law and practice was sometimes wide. That is more than most governments of the period could claim.

Further Down the Hole

📖
The Invisible Hook (Leeson, 2009)
Peter Leeson's Princeton University Press book applying law-and-economics analysis to pirate governance. Dense with primary sources and genuinely funny in places. The chapter on the Jolly Roger as a rational signalling device is alone worth the read.
⚔️
Bartholomew Roberts
The most successful pirate of the Golden Age by any measure: 400+ ships captured between 1719–1722. A teetotaller who enforced his articles strictly. Killed in action off Cape Lopez, Gabon, in February 1722 — the decisive engagement that signalled the end of the Golden Age.
🏝️
Nassau Pirate Republic (1715–1718)
Nassau, Bahamas, functioned as a de facto pirate republic for roughly three years: no tax, no navy, no extradition. Ended when Woodes Rogers arrived in July 1718 with a fleet, a governor's commission, and the King's Pardon. He hanged eight men on his first day.
📜
A General History of the Pyrates (1724)
Published under the pseudonym "Captain Charles Johnson" — possibly Daniel Defoe, though that attribution is disputed. The primary source for the Golden Age, including verbatim articles from multiple ships. Without it, almost nothing of what we know about pirate governance would have survived.
⚖️
Marcus Rediker's Between the Devil and the Deep Blue Sea (1987)
The labour historian's counterpart to Leeson: Rediker reads pirate democracy as class consciousness — a deliberate rejection of the brutal hierarchy sailors experienced on merchant and naval ships. Leeson and Rediker reach the same facts through opposite frameworks. Both are probably partially right.
