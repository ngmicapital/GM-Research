Alpha — Orderbook Depth & Spread

Saturday · 9 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #16 11-min read
TL;DR

Orderbook depth and spread are the two instantaneous readings of a market's liquidity — depth is the thickness of resting limit orders behind the touch, spread is the toll you pay to cross it. The practical edge: depth tells you how much size the book can absorb before price moves, spread tells you whether market makers think it's safe to quote tight; when spread widens while depth thins, the market is pulling back from a fight and the next aggressive print will move price further than the chart suggests.

§01
What It Is
The book's two vital signs · what's resting and what it costs to cross
Depth is how much size sits at each price level on the book; spread is the gap between the best bid and best ask — together they describe the market's instantaneous capacity to absorb orders.

Every orderbook is a stack of resting limit orders, sorted by price. On the bid side, buyers wait passively at successively lower prices. On the ask side, sellers wait at successively higher prices. The best bid is the highest price a buyer will pay right now; the best ask is the lowest price a seller will accept. The gap between them is the spread. Everything above the best ask and below the best bid is depth — the queue of size waiting at each step away from the touch.

Depth and spread are not independent metrics — they are coupled by how market makers manage adverse selection risk. When a maker is confident the next print will not run them over, they quote tight (narrow spread) and post real size close to the touch (deep book). When they suspect informed flow is coming, they widen the spread and pull size back. The two move together, and the relationship is well-documented: "an inverse relation between the limit order book depth and spread" holds at every level of the book ([MDPI, 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7072/9/4/60)). Tight spreads accompany deep books; wide spreads accompany thin ones.

This is why these two readings are foundational — every other orderflow concept rides on top of them. Sweeps only work where there's depth to sweep. Absorption only matters when the absorbing wall is large relative to surrounding depth. Spoofing exists because depth visibly moves price expectations, so faking it is profitable. You cannot read a footprint, a heatmap, or a CVD divergence without an implicit model of what the book looks like underneath.

The practical truth, from Kaiko's spread research: "The smaller the spread the more liquid the market. Wide spreads indicate that liquidity is weaker for an asset." Treat spread as the market's confidence dial — and depth as the size of the bet behind that confidence.

§02
Mechanics
Measuring depth at percent-from-mid · spread in basis points · slippage curves
Depth is measured cumulatively at fixed percentage bands from mid (e.g. ±0.1%, ±1%); spread is measured in basis points of mid; slippage on a market order is the convex sum of every level it eats.

Raw orderbook data is messy — prices update tens of times per second, sizes move, levels appear and vanish. To make depth comparable across assets and time, professional desks measure it as cumulative resting size within a fixed band from mid. Standard bands: ±0.1% (the immediate execution zone), ±0.5% (where most market orders complete), ±1% (the structural defence). The amberdata research for early May 2026 quotes BTC depth at 100 basis points reached $631.1M with ETH at $480.4M — that is, $631M of resting size sat within 1% of BTC mid on aggregate venues.

Spread (in basis points): spread_bps = (ask − bid) / mid × 10,000 Cumulative bid depth at band b: depth_bid(b) = Σ size(p) for all bids where p ≥ mid × (1 − b) Cumulative ask depth at band b: depth_ask(b) = Σ size(p) for all asks where p ≤ mid × (1 + b) Slippage of a market buy of size S: slippage = (avg_fill_price − mid) / mid = (Σᵢ pᵢ · qᵢ) / S − mid, all divided by mid where (pᵢ, qᵢ) are levels eaten ascending until Σqᵢ ≥ S Depth imbalance at band b: DI(b) = (depth_bid(b) − depth_ask(b)) / (depth_bid(b) + depth_ask(b)) → ranges from −1 (all-asks) to +1 (all-bids)
Note: Spread on USDT BTC pairs typically prints in single-digit basis points during normal hours; USD pairs run roughly 3× wider; smaller alts (SOL, ADA) sit at 5–15+ bps even on top venues. Spread alone is not enough — a tight spread on a thin book is a trap. Always measure spread and depth together.

The slippage formula is the one that matters most for sizing. A market order does not get the mid price — it walks up the ask side or down the bid side, eating each level until filled. The fill is the volume-weighted average of every price hit. On a deep book with size at every tick, slippage is negligible. On a thin book — especially one where most of the visible size sits behind the touch as "layered" orders — slippage on the same notional can be 10–50× worse.

The depth imbalance ratio is the bridge to bid/ask imbalance and CVD analysis. A persistent +0.4 imbalance at the 0.5% band means there's significantly more resting bid size than ask size within half a percent of price — bullish lean, but only if the size is real (see Common Traps).

Spread and depth co-move during normal regimes. When they decouple — spread widens but depth holds, or spread stays tight but depth evaporates — the market is in a transition. CME's 2025 liquidity research argues that book depth alone is no longer a sufficient liquidity proxy in modern electronic markets, because so much real size hides as iceberg or latent orders that only appear once aggressors arrive. The visible book is a draft; actual depth is bigger and weirder.

§04
In Practice
Pre-trade liquidity check · regime read · execution sizing
Read spread first to gauge maker confidence, then depth at ±0.5% to size your order — never market into a thin book without checking what the next levels hold.
1

Read spread before depth — it's the leading indicator

Market makers widen spreads before they pull size, because spread widening is reversible and pulling depth is not. A spread that doubles from 1bp to 2bp on BTC/USDT is the first warning that makers are nervous — depth thinning follows within seconds. Watch spread on your execution venue and treat any sustained widening as a regime flag.

2

Size to depth at ±0.5%, not to total book

Cumulative depth out at ±1% looks reassuring on aggregator dashboards, but real execution rarely walks that far. Use depth at ±0.5% from mid as your sizing reference: divide your intended notional by that number — if it exceeds 5–10%, expect material slippage and split the order or use limit orders. Never market a position larger than the visible book at ±0.1% can absorb cleanly.

3

Use depth charts for confluence, not signals

A wall on the depth chart is not a trade signal — it's context. Use it to ask: does my bias agree with what's resting? If you're long and there's a 142-BTC ask wall 5 ticks above, your trade is fighting visible supply. That doesn't mean you're wrong — but you need a thesis for why that wall breaks. Walls that line up with prior structural levels (Wyckoff resistance, prior swing highs) carry the most weight.

4

Watch the spread/depth divergence — that's the alpha

The cleanest signal isn't either metric alone. It's the disagreement between them. Spread tightens but depth hollows out → makers quoting tight to attract takers but unwilling to back it with size; high probability of an air pocket on the next aggressive print. Spread widens but depth thickens → makers preparing for volatility but committed; expect range expansion, not a one-way move.

The 80K Liquidity Wall — Anatomy of a Resistance Cluster

BTC has spent the week of May 4–9 oscillating in the upper $79K–$82K zone, with the $80,000 level acting as a structural ceiling. Amberdata's May 2026 liquidity readout notes aggregate BTC orderbook depth at 100 basis points reached $631.1M, with ETH at $480.4M — meaning the books are structurally deep enough to absorb institutional clip sizes without the kind of slippage that would flag a fragile market.

But aggregate depth hides the geometry. The actual ask side has a single dominant cluster at $80,000 — what Bitget's May 2026 readout called "a massive wall of short-side liquidity at the $80,000 level." That wall is the trade. While it sits, every aggressive market buy gets absorbed and price stalls. When it clears, the cumulative ask curve flattens dramatically — there's almost nothing material between $80K and $82K — and the next print runs.

The spread regime alongside this is informative. Through the chop, BTC/USDT spread on Binance has held at ~0.20 bps (single-digit basis points) — Kaiko's research benchmark for healthy USDT majors. Makers are confident enough to stay tight even against the wall, which is itself a tell: they don't expect the wall to fail until specific flow shows up.

§07
Common Traps
Why most retail readings of the book are dangerously wrong
The biggest mistake is trading off the visible book — most of the real liquidity is either spoofed away on approach or hidden until aggressors arrive.
1
Trusting walls without watching them refresh

A 142-BTC wall at $80K means nothing if it gets pulled the moment price comes within 0.05% of it. Real walls refresh as orders peel off — the size sits there, gets eaten, and is immediately replaced from behind. Walls that sit static and then vanish at touch are spoofs. Watch the size dynamics, not the static snapshot.

2
Reading top-of-book as the whole story

The best bid/ask shown on a CEX UI is one tick of context. Real microstructure is the cumulative curve out to ±1%, plus iceberg orders that aren't visible at all. Top-of-book reads are necessary but never sufficient. Aggregator depth charts (CoinGlass, CoinAPI) are the minimum viable tool.

3
Ignoring spread when sizing

A trader doing 50 round-trips a day on BTC at 0.20 bps spread pays 100 bps in spread alone over a year — meaningful but manageable. The same trader on a venue with 8 bps spread pays 4,000 bps. Spread compounds. Always know your spread cost as a function of trading frequency, and always check it on the venue you're actually executing on, not the aggregate quote.

4
Treating depth as supply & demand

Resting limit orders are conditional intentions, not commitments. They get cancelled, refilled, layered, spoofed. They are not "demand" or "supply" in any economic sense — they are a market maker's quote, withdrawable at zero cost. The CME's institutional take: "book depth alone is no longer a sufficient liquidity proxy." Treat depth as one input, not as the truth.

5
Overhyped: "reading the book" as a standalone strategy

A whole genre of crypto education sells "orderbook reading" as a complete trading system. It isn't. The book is a context layer — vital, but useless without trade flow (CVD), structural levels (Wyckoff, volume profile), and a thesis. People who claim to trade purely off the book either (a) are running HFT-grade infrastructure you don't have, or (b) are blowing up quietly. Use depth and spread to refine entries and stops on trades that already have a thesis. They are not the thesis.
