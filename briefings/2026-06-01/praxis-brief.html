Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

Understanding is not downloaded — it is produced by the friction of grappling, and the modern information environment is engineered to let you skip the friction. Ezra Klein, via Shane Parrish's FS Brain Food #683, reframes knowledge itself: he used to picture learning the way The Matrix does, information uploaded straight into the brain, and now sees reading as the hours of grappling that draw connections you would not otherwise have had — "you can get the answer without much effort, but you can't get the understanding." Tim Ferriss's 5-Bullet Friday supplies the shadow side: the Gell-Mann amnesia effect (we catch the newspaper getting our own field backwards, then trust the next page), his coined term "Hallucinatives" (the first generations to take AI and LLM answers as truth without cross-checking), and Vulture's "Your Feed Is Fake," which reports that an estimated 90% of what you see online is advertising in disguise — one operator ran 65,000 dummy accounts posting 50,000 videos a day. The convergence: the two decisive skills of the moment are doing the cognitive work yourself so that it changes you, and verifying the source so that you are not fooled — and both are precisely the friction the environment now lets you skip.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
FS Brain Food #683 · Shane Parrish, quoting Ezra Klein · "Reading Changes You" · 31 May 2026

"You Can Get The Answer Without Effort, But You Can't Get The Understanding" — Klein and Parrish on Why Knowledge Is Produced By Grappling, Not Downloaded

The week's most load-bearing idea arrives in the quietest container — a single block-quote in Shane Parrish's weekly Brain Food, attributed to the commentator Ezra Klein, on what reading actually is. "Part of what is happening when you spend 7 hours reading a book, is you spend 7 hours with your mind on the topics in the book, grappling with them, drawing connections, having thoughts you would not otherwise have had. And so without that process of grappling, without those hours inside that book, it doesn't get inside you. It doesn't impress itself upon you. It doesn't change you." The claim is not the familiar one that reading is good for you. It is the sharper one that the output of reading — the summary, the takeaway, the fact — is not the thing that does the work. The work is done by the hours of friction that produce the output, and the output detached from those hours is inert.

Klein has elsewhere made the underlying reframe explicit, and it is worth importing because it names the mistake precisely. In a recent interview arguing against writing with AI, he describes how he used to conceptualise knowledge the way The Matrix does — as information downloaded directly into the brain, a file copied into a folder. He now believes that picture is simply wrong about how minds work: what you are doing when you learn is not uploading a file but spending time grappling with material, and it is the grappling, not the material, that integrates the knowledge into the way your mind operates. The distinction sounds academic until it is run against the actual decision the operator faces forty times a day: whether to read the thing or read the summary of the thing, whether to write the memo or have the memo written, whether to sit with the problem or ask for the answer. On Klein's model these are not efficiency trades. They are the difference between an experience that changes you and one that leaves you exactly as you were, now holding a fact you cannot use.

Parrish closes the entry with the one-line gloss that makes the whole thing land in the present tense, and it is the sentence to keep. "This is fascinating to think about through the lens of AI. You can get the answer without much effort, but you can't get the understanding." The reframe the operator should take is that effort is not the price you pay for the understanding — effort is the understanding, in the same way that the miles are the fitness and not a toll charged on the way to it. An age that has made answers nearly free has, by exactly the same motion, made the grappling optional — and the grappling was never the obstacle to learning. It was the mechanism.

5-Bullet Friday · Tim Ferriss · Gell-Mann Amnesia, "Hallucinatives," and "Your Feed Is Fake" · 29 May 2026

The Shadow Side: An Information Environment Engineered To Let You Skip The Verifying — Gell-Mann Amnesia, the "Hallucinatives," and the Fake Feed

If the first card establishes that understanding is produced by grappling with the source, the second supplies the part that makes the first urgent: the contemporary information environment is built, increasingly and deliberately, to relieve you of any contact with the source at all. Tim Ferriss's 5-Bullet Friday surfaces three nodes of this in a single issue, and read together they form an argument the issue does not bother to state explicitly. The first is the Gell-Mann amnesia effect, Michael Crichton's name for a failure everyone commits and no one corrects: "You open the newspaper to an article on some subject you know well… You read the article and see the journalist has absolutely no understanding of either the facts or the issues. Often, the article is so wrong it actually presents the story backward… you read with exasperation… the multiple errors in a story — and then turn the page to national or international affairs, and read with renewed interest as if the rest of the newspaper was somehow more accurate… You turn the page, and forget what you know." The expertise that proves the source unreliable is discarded the instant the topic moves outside it.

The second node updates Crichton's failure for the present and gives it a name. Ferriss has coined "Hallucinatives" — "the first generations to take AI/LLM answers as truth without cross-checking," or in the variant he A/B-tested with readers, "the first generations with unquestioned faith in the truth of AI/LLM answers." The coinage matters because it isolates the exact behaviour the first card warns against, now industrialised: where Gell-Mann amnesia is the failure to extend earned skepticism past the page you happen to know, the Hallucinative's failure is to have abandoned the skepticism entirely, outsourcing not just the answer but the judgment of whether the answer is true to a system that is confidently wrong a measurable fraction of the time. The Klein point and the Ferriss point are the same point from two ends: understanding requires grappling with the source, and a generation that takes the source's output on faith has skipped the grappling and inherited the confidence without the comprehension.

"We've locked ourselves in the stupidest possible version of Plato's cave, where what looks like the spontaneous consensus of the hive mind is often just shadows on the wall, put there by marketers, political operatives, foreign-influence campaigns, or anyone else with a few hundred bucks and something to sell." — "Your Feed Is Fake," Vulture / New York Magazine, surfaced in 5-Bullet Friday (29 May 2026)

The third node supplies the environmental fact that makes the first two non-paranoid. Vulture's "Your Feed Is Fake" reports on Joe Lim, who for three years ran a company, Floodify, that at its peak operated 65,000 dummy social-media accounts and on a typical day posted 50,000 videos across TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, and X, every one engineered to pass for the unscripted output of an ordinary person. Lim's own estimate is that "90 percent of what you see on the internet is advertising in disguise." The operator's correct extraction is not despair but a recalibration of the default: the feed is not a window onto what people think; it is a manufactured surface, and the "spontaneous consensus" it displays is frequently a paid artefact. Which returns the whole section to the first card with the stakes inverted. Klein says the grappling is what changes you; Ferriss documents an environment in which the un-grappled-with surface — the AI answer taken on faith, the viral consensus that is really a media buy — is engineered to be frictionless, persuasive, and false. In that environment the grappling stops being a virtue and becomes a defence.

FS Brain Food #683 · Shane Parrish · "Tiny Thoughts" · 31 May 2026

The Same Avoidance, Two More Places: Why Soft Feedback And Easy Agreement Are The Frictionless Defaults That Quietly Cost The Most

The same Brain Food issue carries a second thread that turns out to be the first one wearing different clothes. Parrish's diagnosis of feedback is unusually blunt about the motive. "Most people give soft feedback because they care more about how the conversation feels than about whether the problem gets solved. This is selfish." And the receiving end is the mirror image: "A lot of people don't actually want direct feedback; they prefer something softer. When they hear direct feedback, they focus on how it makes them feel and not the substance. If you're focusing on how feedback makes you feel and not its accuracy, you're robbing yourself of the opportunity to get better." The structure is identical to the reading insight. Soft feedback is the frictionless path — it optimises for how the exchange feels — and in doing so it forfeits the only thing the exchange was for: the correction that produces improvement. The comfort and the growth are once again on opposite sides of the same friction.

Parrish's adjacent thought extends the pattern from feedback to consensus, and supplies the affirmative half. "If everyone agrees, you won't create much value. Deviate when you have an edge." The line is easy to misread as a licence for contrarianism; the operative clause is the conditional. The value is not in disagreeing — disagreement is cheap — it is in holding the non-consensus position you can actually defend, which requires having done the grappling that earns the edge in the first place. This is where the three cards lock together. Easy agreement, like soft feedback and the un-checked AI answer, is the path that feels good and costs the most: it produces a room in which everyone is comfortable, no one is corrected, and nothing is created. The edge that justifies deviating is manufactured in exactly the place the week keeps pointing to — the hours of friction that the comfortable defaults are engineered to let you skip.

The three cards converge on one architecture, and it is worth stating in a single line. Every frictionless default this week — the AI answer in place of the grappling (Klein), the feed taken at face value in place of verification (Ferriss), the soft feedback and easy agreement in place of the hard correction (Parrish) — optimises for how the moment feels and forfeits the change the moment was supposed to produce. The unifying insight is that effort, friction, and discomfort are not the toll charged on the way to understanding, improvement, and value; they are understanding, improvement, and value, experienced from the inside. An era that has automated the answer, manufactured the consensus, and socialised the feedback has, by a single motion, made every one of these frictions optional — which means the two skills that now separate the competent from the credulous are the two the environment most actively erodes: doing the cognitive work yourself, so that it changes you, and verifying the source, so that you are not fooled. Both are forms of the same refusal — the refusal to mistake the frictionless surface for the thing it imitates.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · From the Klein / Parrish "Reading Changes You" Card

The Grappling Protocol: A Practice For Using AI Without Surrendering The Understanding It Offers To Do Your Thinking For You

Klein's insight converts into a discipline for the specific decision the operator now makes constantly — whether to grapple or to outsource — without becoming a Luddite about a genuinely useful tool. The structure has four steps:

  • Decide, per task, whether you need the answer or the understanding. Not every task is a reading task. For the throwaway lookup — a syntax, a date, a definition you will never need again — the frictionless answer is correct and the grappling is waste. The distinction is whether the output is something you will build on. If you will reason from it, decide with it, or be expected to defend it, it is an understanding task, and the answer-without-the-grappling is a liability dressed as a shortcut.
  • On understanding tasks, grapple first, then consult. Form your own model of the problem before you ask the machine — the draft, the estimate, the hypothesis, the list of what you think the answer is. Only then bring in the AI, and bring it in as an adversary rather than an oracle: ask it where your model is wrong, not what the answer is. The grappling has to come first because its entire value is that it cannot be retrofitted; an answer received before you have thought forecloses the thinking it was supposed to seed.
  • Write to think, not to record. Klein's deeper claim is that writing and reading are how the grappling happens, not merely how its results are stored. The practice is to do the writing that produces thought — the messy first draft, the argument worked out longhand — rather than prompting a clean version into existence and editing it. The clean version produced without the struggle reads well and teaches nothing; the test is whether you could reconstruct the argument with the document taken away.
  • Run the "could I rebuild this?" check before you ship. Before treating any AI-assisted output as your own understanding, ask whether you could reproduce its core reasoning from scratch, without the tool, to a skeptical colleague. If the answer is no, you have the artefact but not the understanding — which is fine for a lookup and disqualifying for anything you will be held to. The check is falsifiable, and it is the single fastest way to catch yourself having skipped the grappling.
Practice · From the Ferriss "Gell-Mann / Hallucinatives / Fake Feed" Card

The Gell-Mann Audit: A Verification Discipline For An Environment Where The Frictionless Surface Is Engineered To Be Persuasive And Often False

Ferriss's three nodes convert into a standing verification practice calibrated for a feed that is, by one practitioner's estimate, 90% disguised advertising. The structure has four steps:

  • Extend the skepticism past the page you know. The Gell-Mann correction is a single deliberate act: when you catch a source getting your field wrong, do not turn the page and forget — downgrade your trust in the whole source by the amount its error in your domain warrants. The expertise that proves a source unreliable is the most valuable signal you will get all day; the failure is throwing it away the moment the topic changes.
  • Treat AI output and viral consensus as claims, not findings. The anti-Hallucinative discipline is to assign a confidence level, never a certainty, to anything a model or a feed hands you — and to cross-check anything you will act on against a primary source. The tell that you have slipped into faith is the absence of a citation you have actually seen: if you cannot point to where the claim comes from, you do not know it, you have merely been told it.
  • Ask who profits from your belief. "Your Feed Is Fake" reframes the default question from is this true? to who paid for me to see this, and what do they want me to do? Manufactured consensus has a beneficiary; genuine consensus usually does not. When a position arrives pre-packaged as the obvious view of all sensible people, the manufactured-consensus hypothesis deserves a hearing before the spontaneous-agreement one.
  • Keep one un-fakeable input. The structural defence against a synthetic feed is to retain at least one information source that cannot be cheaply flooded — a book, a primary document, a person you trust who has direct knowledge, a dataset you can query yourself. The operator who has no un-fakeable input has no fixed point from which to judge the fakeable ones; the feed becomes the only reality, which is precisely the cave Vulture describes.
Practice · From the Parrish "Soft Feedback / Easy Consensus" Card

The Direct-Feedback Discipline: A Practice For Giving And Receiving The Hard Correction, And For Earning The Edge That Justifies Deviating

Parrish's feedback thread converts into a two-sided discipline — one half for what you say to others, one for what you let yourself hear. The structure has four steps:

  • When giving feedback, optimise for the problem, not the temperature. Parrish's charge is that soft feedback is selfish — it protects the giver's comfort at the recipient's expense. The practice is to say the accurate thing plainly and early, and to treat the urge to cushion it into uselessness as the warning sign it is. Kindness is in the accuracy and the timing, not in the softening; the softened version that leaves the problem unsolved is the unkind one.
  • When receiving it, separate the substance from the sting. The recipient's failure is attending to how the feedback feels instead of whether it is right. The discipline is to run a deliberate two-pass: first absorb the substance with the feeling bracketed, then, separately, decide what if anything to do about the delivery. Fusing the two — rejecting accurate feedback because it stung — is, in Parrish's words, robbing yourself of the opportunity to get better.
  • Build a small standing source of candour. Direct feedback does not arrive on its own; the environment is biased toward the soft kind. The operator deliberately cultivates the two or three people licensed to tell him the unwelcome thing, and makes it cheap for them to do so by visibly rewarding candour over comfort. The rarity of direct feedback is a market failure you correct by paying, in graciousness, the people willing to supply it.
  • Earn the edge before you deviate. "Deviate when you have an edge" is a licence with a precondition. Before holding the non-consensus position, do the work that earns it — the grappling of the first practice — so that the deviation is defensible rather than merely contrarian. The disagreement that creates value is the one you can defend under direct questioning; the rest is just noise wearing the costume of independent thought.

The three practices are the same refusal applied in three theatres. The grappling protocol refuses to mistake the AI's answer for your understanding; the Gell-Mann audit refuses to mistake the manufactured surface for the truth; the direct-feedback discipline refuses to mistake the comfortable exchange for the useful one. Each works by reinstalling, on purpose, a friction the environment has made optional — the friction of thinking it through, of checking the source, of saying and hearing the hard thing. The senior practitioner's integration is to notice which of the three frictions he has most quietly stopped paying: the one he is skipping when he prompts instead of thinks, the one he is skipping when he forwards instead of verifies, or the one he is skipping when he softens instead of says. The practice that matters most this quarter is attached to the friction he has been most relieved to be rid of — because that relief is the precise sensation of an understanding, a correction, or a truth being quietly declined.

The source behind the week's central card, and the fuller version of the argument Brain Food compresses into a single quote. Michael Crichton — The Gell-Mann Amnesia Effect

The most portable single idea of the week, and the one most worth installing as a permanent mental check. Crichton's effect — named ironically, he admitted, by "dropping a famous name" (the physicist Murray Gell-Mann) to lend it weight — describes the universal failure to carry skepticism across a page: you see a story get your own field exactly wrong, then trust the next story as if the source had suddenly become reliable. In an environment of AI answers and synthetic feeds, it is the founding bug of credulity, and naming it is the first step to correcting it. The original passage is widely available; it is the conceptual backbone of the Gell-Mann Audit practice above.

04 On the Horizon
Epistemics

"Hallucinatives" and the Arrival of Faith-Based Knowing

Ferriss's coinage is a leading indicator of a shift the rest of the culture has not yet named: a cohort forming the habit of taking machine output as truth without the cross-check, and an older cohort quietly doing the same under deadline. The horizon development is not that AI is sometimes wrong — that is known — but that verification is becoming a distinct, teachable, increasingly scarce literacy, separable from the ability to retrieve an answer and far rarer. Expect "can you verify this?" to harden from a politeness into an explicit competency that organisations screen for, and expect the gap between those who reflexively check and those who reflexively trust to become one of the more consequential divides in knowledge work.

Information Markets

The Synthetic Feed Moving One Layer Up — Toward the AI Agents

The most forward-looking line in "Your Feed Is Fake" is the operator's own forecast: that the manufactured-content business has perhaps three to five years left in its current form, "because in the future, people will stop trusting what they see on social media" — at which point "you'll have to start distributing your content toward AI agents and then they'll teach humans what they want." The horizon theme is the migration of the influence economy from human feeds to the models humans increasingly consult instead. As more decisions route through an AI intermediary, the contest for attention becomes a contest to shape what the intermediary says — a quieter, harder-to-detect layer of the same manufactured-consensus problem, aimed at the system you have started to trust precisely because it felt cleaner than the feed.

Attention

Friction as Deliberate Counter-Practice

Against the frictionless default, a counter-movement is gathering that treats effort as the point rather than the obstacle: deep reading, longhand writing, the deliberate refusal to outsource the grappling. Klein's "case against writing with AI" is one node; the broader pattern is the re-emergence of difficulty as something chosen on purpose — not nostalgia, but a recognition that the cognitive work an answer-rich environment makes optional is exactly the work that produces understanding. Watch for "I read the whole thing" and "I worked it out myself" to acquire the status of a signal rather than an inefficiency, and for the most capable operators to be distinguished less by access to answers — which is now universal — than by their willingness to do the grappling the answers let everyone else skip.

Candour

Direct Feedback as a Recoverable Competitive Edge

Parrish's blunt framing of soft feedback as selfish lands into a workplace culture that has spent a decade optimising for psychological comfort, sometimes at the cost of the correction comfort was meant to enable. The horizon development is a quiet re-pricing: organisations rediscovering that the team able to give and absorb direct feedback compounds faster than the one that cannot, and that candour — properly distinguished from cruelty — is a performance input, not a personality flaw. Expect "radical candour" and its descendants to get a more careful second look, less as a licence for bluntness and more as the specific, trainable discipline of caring about the problem more than the temperature of the conversation about it.
