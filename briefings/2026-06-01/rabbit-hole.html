The Poison in the Air: How Leaded Gasoline May Have Caused the 20th Century’s Crime Wave

In 1921, a chemist named Thomas Midgley Jr. discovered that adding tetraethyl lead to gasoline stopped engine knock. For the next 60 years, the world burned it in every car, breathing the exhaust. A geochemist who discovered what was happening spent 30 years being systematically destroyed by the industry. And then, 20 years after the phase-out began, violent crime fell off a cliff — in every country that had used leaded gasoline, on almost exactly the schedule the lead hypothesis predicted.

TL;DR

In 1921, an American chemist named Thomas Midgley Jr. discovered that adding tetraethyl lead to gasoline eliminated the engine knock that was plaguing the nascent automobile industry. He knew lead was toxic — he had recovered from lead poisoning himself during the research — but he and his employers at General Motors and Standard Oil pressed ahead anyway. For the next six decades, the world burned leaded gasoline in hundreds of millions of cars, dispersing a potent neurotoxin into the air of every city on earth. Children growing up near roads were especially exposed; lead accumulated in their developing brains and specifically damaged the prefrontal cortex, the region responsible for impulse control, planning, and the suppression of aggression. Then, between 1973 and 1986, the United States phased out leaded gasoline. Other developed countries followed. And roughly 20 years later — the exact interval between childhood exposure and peak adult criminality — violent crime fell off a cliff everywhere that had used it. The correlation is not incidental: it appears across every US state, every country that phased out lead, and on the predicted schedule. The most likely explanation for the 1990s crime drop is not policing strategies, mass incarceration, or legalised abortion. It is that the children who grew up breathing leaded exhaust aged out of crime, and the children born after the phase-out had prefrontal cortexes that could do what the previous generation’s could not.

Section 01

The Story

Thomas Midgley Jr. was born in Beaver Falls, Pennsylvania in 1889 and spent his career at the intersection of chemistry and mechanical engineering. He was brilliant, industrious, and decorated: the American Chemical Society gave him its highest honour, the Priestly Medal, in 1941. He was elected to the National Academy of Sciences. He held over 100 patents. He was also, according to environmental historian J.R. McNeill, “the single organism that had more impact on the atmosphere than any other in Earth’s history.” This is because Midgley was responsible for two of the 20th century’s most consequential chemical introductions: tetraethyl lead in gasoline, and chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs, later marketed as Freon) in refrigerants and aerosols. The first poisoned developing brains across six decades. The second punched a hole in the ozone layer.

The lead story began in 1916, when Midgley was hired at the Dayton Research Laboratories — then affiliated with General Motors — and tasked with finding an anti-knock additive for gasoline. Engine knock was the limiting problem of early automobile engines: when the fuel-air mixture detonated unevenly, it produced a sharp metallic sound and damaged the engine. Solving it was worth enormous money to General Motors. Midgley tested hundreds of compounds over five years. On December 9, 1921, he added tetraethyl lead (TEL) to the fuel in a one-cylinder test engine. The knock disappeared instantly.

Midgley knew lead was a poison. He had recovered from lead poisoning during the research. His solution was to demonstrate, at a 1924 press conference, that tetraethyl lead was perfectly safe — by washing his hands in it. He later needed another extended recovery from lead poisoning.

The toxic effects of lead were not unknown in 1921. They had been documented since antiquity; Roman physicians had noted the symptoms; 19th-century occupational medicine had catalogued them in painters and plumbers. What Midgley and his employers at Standard Oil and General Motors did was argue, with some success, that the doses involved in everyday gasoline use were too small to matter. They also had commercial incentives that overwhelmed precaution. General Motors and Standard Oil formed the Ethyl Corporation in 1923 specifically to produce and market TEL.

In October 1924, a production plant at Standard Oil’s Bayway refinery in Bayonne, New Jersey began manufacturing TEL at industrial scale. Within weeks, 49 workers in the TEL building had been hospitalised. Five died. Surviving workers reported violent hallucinations; several had to be restrained in straitjackets, screaming about phantoms they could see in the room. The New York tabloids called it “the loony gas building.” New York City, Philadelphia, and New Jersey briefly banned TEL sales. Then a committee of scientists convened by the Surgeon General in 1925 concluded that no immediate public health hazard had been established — a conclusion critics argued was predetermined — and the bans were lifted. Leaded gasoline went back on sale and stayed there for 60 more years.

Clair Patterson’s Fight

In the 1950s, geochemist Clair Patterson at Caltech was trying to calculate the age of the Earth from lead isotope ratios in meteorites. His measurements were ruined by lead contamination — which was everywhere, on every surface, in every reagent he used. When he traced it back to its source, he found that ambient lead levels in the modern environment were roughly 100 times higher than natural background, and the spike corresponded exactly with the introduction of leaded gasoline. Patterson published his findings in 1965 and spent the next decade being excluded from government advisory panels, refused contracts by organisations funded by the lead industry, and subjected to a sustained campaign of professional discrediting. The Ethyl Corporation tried to have him fired from Caltech. His research eventually helped force the US phase-out; he described himself as the most vilified scientist in America for much of that period.

Section 02

The Mechanism

Lead is one of the most potent neurotoxins known to affect the developing brain. Unlike most environmental hazards, it has no safe threshold: there is no level of childhood lead exposure at which cognitive and behavioural effects are undetectable. The mechanism operates at the cellular level through several pathways simultaneously. Lead mimics calcium and enters neurons through calcium channels, disrupting the signalling processes that neurons use to communicate. It impairs the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor, which is critical for learning and memory. It degrades the dopaminergic and serotonergic pathways involved in mood regulation and executive function. And it reduces grey matter density in the frontal lobe — particularly the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, the region most implicated in impulse control, planning, and the suppression of aggressive responses.

The behavioural consequences documented in children with elevated blood lead levels are specific and consistent: reduced IQ, impaired attention, difficulty with executive function, increased impulsivity, and heightened aggressiveness. These are not subtle effects. A 2012 study found that each 1-microgram-per-decilitre increase in blood lead was associated with measurable reductions in prefrontal cortex volume in adults who had been exposed as children. In populations exposed to the ambient lead levels common in American cities during the 1950s through the 1970s — when blood lead levels averaged between 10 and 20 micrograms per decilitre across the US child population, with urban concentrations often twice as high — the effect on impulse control across the entire population was substantial.

Lead does not make people violent. It removes the neural infrastructure that makes people not violent — the prefrontal governor that normally stands between an impulse and an action.

The crime connection arises not because lead produces violent people but because it degrades the capacity for self-regulation. The prefrontal cortex is, among other things, an inhibitory system: it is what makes a person capable of thinking through consequences before acting. Reduced prefrontal function does not generate aggression from nothing — it removes the brake. In a population where a significant fraction of children grew up with measurably impaired impulse control, the downstream effect on crime rates 20 years later — when those children reached peak criminogenic age, roughly 18 to 25 — would be predictable, even if no one had a mechanism for it in 1970.

The US phase-out of leaded gasoline proceeded in stages. The Clean Air Act of 1970 created the EPA, which in 1973 began requiring phased reductions in gasoline lead content. New cars from 1975 required unleaded fuel to protect catalytic converters. Lead content fell through the late 1970s, accelerated dramatically in 1985 after new EPA regulations, and reached zero for on-road vehicles by the end of 1986. Blood lead levels in American children tracked this decline closely, falling by roughly 78% between 1976 and 1991. Children born from the mid-1970s onward carried substantially less lead in their developing brains than children born in the 1950s or 1960s. Those children grew up. By the early 1990s, the oldest of them were entering their late teens and early twenties — precisely the age window when criminogenic behaviour peaks. And crime fell.

Jessica Reyes, 2007

Harvard economist Jessica Wolpaw Reyes published the most rigorous academic examination of the lead–crime hypothesis in a 2007 NBER working paper. She exploited the fact that different US states phased out leaded gasoline at different times, producing natural variation she could use to isolate lead’s effect from other factors. Her finding: a 10% reduction in childhood lead exposure was associated with an 8% reduction in violent crime approximately two decades later. She estimated that the full elimination of leaded gasoline could account for more than half of the violent crime decline seen during the 1990s. The study has been cited extensively; its core findings have been replicated in multiple countries using similar variation strategies.

Section 03

The Connections

The international fingerprint. If the lead hypothesis is correct, countries that phased out leaded gasoline should have seen crime drops on the same schedule as the US, offset by their own phase-out timelines. They did. Rick Nevin, an economist who first published on this topic in 1999 and expanded his work through the 2000s, showed that the lead–crime correlation holds in the United Kingdom, Canada, France, Australia, New Zealand, Finland, Italy, and West Germany. Each country’s crime curve tracks its leaded gasoline curve with a lag of approximately 20 years. No other proposed explanation for the 1990s crime drop — policing strategies, incarceration, demographic shifts, legalised abortion — can account for this cross-national pattern, because those factors varied enormously between countries. The lead exposure varied too, but in a way that predicted the crime variation.

Why the other explanations came first. The competing explanations for the 1990s crime drop generated more political heat precisely because they were politically legible. More policing, more incarceration, and broken-windows enforcement were Republican proposals; legalised abortion (the Donohue–Levitt hypothesis, published in 2001) generated enormous controversy because it involved Roe v. Wade. The lead hypothesis is politically inert — nobody was on the lead side except chemical companies that no longer exist — but it is also invisible. You cannot see lead in the air. The health effects accumulate slowly and express themselves 20 years later in a population that has no idea why it is behaving the way it is. There was no political entrepreneur with an interest in the lead explanation, and the industry that had every interest in suppressing it spent decades doing exactly that.

Thomas Midgley’s second catastrophe. Midgley did not live to see the consequences of leaded gasoline. In 1940 he contracted polio and was left disabled. He invented an elaborate system of ropes and pulleys to lift himself from bed. In November 1944, at age 55, he was found strangled by the device. The death was ruled a suicide, though some accounts treat it as an accident. He had also, during his career, invented the first chlorofluorocarbons — the compounds that became Freon, that were used in refrigerators, air conditioners, and aerosol cans, and that scientists discovered in the 1970s were destroying the stratospheric ozone layer. The Montreal Protocol of 1987 phased out CFCs globally. Midgley never knew. Bill Bryson, in A Short History of Nearly Everything, wrote that Midgley possessed “an instinct for the regrettable that was almost uncanny.”

What “The Crime Drop” Actually Looked Like

Between 1991 and 2014, the US violent crime rate fell by approximately 73%. The murder rate in New York City fell from 2,245 in 1990 to 328 in 2014 — an 85% decline. These were not statistical artefacts or changes in reporting: they corresponded to a real and observable reduction in violence that criminologists, at the time, genuinely could not explain. The timing was consistent with lead across every demographic and geographic stratum that was analysed — urban, suburban, Black, white, male, female. Lead exposure in the 1960s and 1970s was not uniform: it was highest near highways and in urban areas with older housing stock. The crime drop was also not uniform. The correlation between the two, at the sub-city level, is uncomfortable in its precision.

The current exposure problem. The US and most developed countries removed lead from gasoline, but lead does not disappear. It accumulated in soil alongside roads for 60 years and remains there. It persists in the paint of older housing stock — lead-based paint was not banned in the US until 1978 — and paint lead exposure remains a significant source for children in low-income households in buildings constructed before that date. Studies of blood lead levels by zip code in US cities consistently show that the areas with the highest current childhood lead exposure are the same areas with the highest rates of crime and poverty. The mechanism identified by the lead hypothesis is not a historical artefact. It is operating now, in the houses where lead paint is still flaking, producing the next cohort of children with impaired impulse control 20 years in the future.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

🧪
Kevin Drum — “America’s Real Criminal Element: Lead” (Mother Jones, 2013)
The piece that brought the lead–crime hypothesis to a general audience. Drum synthesised Reyes, Nevin, and the broader literature into a compelling narrative and published it in Mother Jones — an unusual venue for epidemiology. It circulated widely among wonks and policy readers and eventually prompted a follow-up series in 2016 after the Flint water crisis. Drum was not an academic, but his command of the primary literature was serious and his representation of the researchers’ findings was accurate. He has continued updating the piece as new data arrives. It remains the best single-document introduction to the topic for a non-specialist.
📈
Jessica Reyes — “Environmental Policy as Social Policy?” (NBER, 2007)
The academic paper that established the statistical case most rigorously. Reyes used variation in state-level phase-out timing as a natural experiment to isolate lead’s causal effect from confounders. The regression discontinuity design is methodologically sound and has not been successfully challenged. The paper estimates that lead reduction accounts for 56% of the violent crime decline of the 1990s. For readers comfortable with econometrics, it is available free via NBER. For everyone else, Drum’s 2013 summary is a reasonable proxy.
🔬
Clair Patterson — “Contaminated and Natural Lead Environments of Man” (1965)
The paper that started the regulatory fight. Patterson had originally been trying to date the Earth; the lead contamination that ruined his measurements turned out to be a discovery in its own right. His 1965 paper in Archives of Environmental Health established that modern lead concentrations in humans were 100 times higher than natural background, tracked the spike precisely to leaded gasoline, and called for urgent regulatory action. The paper was ignored for a decade and Patterson’s funding was attacked by industry. The story of Patterson’s fight against the Ethyl Corporation is told at length in Bill Bryson’s A Short History of Nearly Everything and was dramatised in a 2014 episode of Neil DeGrasse Tyson’s Cosmos.
🌍
Our World in Data — “How the World Eliminated Lead from Gasoline”
The best single data visualisation of the global phase-out timeline. It shows country-by-country lead removal dates against crime trajectories, making the 20-year offset pattern visible at a glance. Algeria was the last country to phase out leaded gasoline entirely, in 2021 — meaning the full global benefit of the phase-out will not be visible in crime statistics until roughly 2041. Our World in Data keeps the chart updated; it is a good reference for anyone trying to assess where in the world the lead legacy still dominates crime patterns.
⚠️
The Flint Water Crisis as a Rerun
In 2014, the city of Flint, Michigan switched its water source to the Flint River and failed to apply corrosion controls. Lead from ageing pipes leached into the water supply. Blood lead levels in Flint children rose sharply. Researchers studying the cohort exposed in 2014–2016 are now tracking outcomes; the children most heavily exposed are now approaching adolescence. If the lead hypothesis is correct, Flint should see a localised increase in behavioural and cognitive problems followed by a crime bump approximately 15–20 years after the exposure peak — around 2030–2035. The Flint crisis is, among other things, a prospective natural experiment in lead’s criminogenic effects.
