Alpha — Unfinished Auctions

Monday · 1 June 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #38 11-min read
TL;DR

An unfinished auction (unfinished business) is a candle whose extreme — its high or low — printed trades on both the bid and the ask, meaning the move stopped because the bar ran out of time, not because the auction ran out of buyers or sellers. The practical edge: a finished extreme leaves a clean one-sided print (excess); an unfinished one leaves a blunt, two-sided top or bottom that acts as a magnet — price tends to come back to that exact level to complete the auction, which makes unfinished business a target-selection tool, not a fade.

§01
What It Is
A high or low the market never properly closed
An unfinished auction is a bar extreme that traded on both bid and ask — a two-sided top or bottom that signals the auction was cut short by the clock, not by exhaustion, so price tends to return and finish it.

Every candle is an auction. Price ratchets up while aggressive buyers keep lifting the offer, and ratchets down while aggressive sellers keep hitting the bid. A finished auction at the high means buyers ran out: the last few ticks show buying with no sellers stepping in at the extreme, the market rejects, and it leaves a clean, one-sided print — the footprint equivalent of a Market Profile excess tail. The auction completed. Price discovered the level, both sides agreed it was too expensive, and the market moved on.

An unfinished auction is what you get when the bar simply ends mid-fight. At the very top tick, both aggressive buyers (lifting the ask) and aggressive sellers (hitting the bid) are still transacting when the candle closes — neither side is zero. Nobody was rejected. The two-sided print says the price war at that extreme was never resolved; the clock just expired. That leaves unfinished business, and the market has a strong tendency to come back and settle it.

This is not mysticism — it is the mechanical signature of incomplete price discovery. As Steidlmayer's auction framework puts it, price is the market's best guess at a given moment; value is only established when an extreme is properly tested and rejected. A two-sided extreme is a guess the market never confirmed. Until it does, that level sits on the chart like an open loop. Trader Dale describes the resulting behaviour bluntly: the market treats these imperfections "like a magnet."

FINISHEDExtreme prints one-sided (one side zero) — clean rejection / excess UNFINISHEDExtreme prints two-sided (both bid & ask) — auction cut short EXCESSProfile equivalent of a finished extreme — a true tail/rejection POOR HIGH/LOWProfile equivalent of unfinished — blunt extreme, likely revisited MAGNETAn unfinished level price is drawn back to in order to complete
If you only remember one thing A finished extreme is the market saying "done." An unfinished extreme is the market saying "to be continued" — and the market keeps its appointments.
§02
Mechanics
Reading the extreme cell · one-sided vs two-sided · profile equivalents
On a footprint, read the single cell at the bar's high (and low): if one side is zero, the auction finished; if both bid and ask carry volume, it is unfinished.

A footprint chart prints, at every price level inside a bar, the volume traded on the bid (aggressive sells) and the volume traded on the ask (aggressive buys) — conventionally shown as bid | ask. You only need to look at one cell to classify the auction: the cell at the very top of the bar for the high, and the cell at the very bottom for the low.

Extreme cell at the bar HIGH: bid_vol = aggressive sells filled at the high ask_vol = aggressive buys filled at the high FINISHED HIGH (excess): bid_vol = 0 → one-sided, clean rejection / tail UNFINISHED HIGH: bid_vol > 0 AND ask_vol > 0 → two-sided, cut short → MAGNET Mirror at the bar LOW: FINISHED LOW (excess): ask_vol = 0 → one-sided rejection UNFINISHED LOW: bid_vol > 0 AND ask_vol > 0 → two-sided → MAGNET
[Sources differ] Platforms vary on which side "should" be zero at a finished extreme, and some flag unfinished prints automatically (NinjaTrader, ATAS, Sierra, Exocharts). Don't memorise the side — memorise the rule: one-sided extreme = finished; two-sided extreme = unfinished. That holds regardless of display convention.

The intuition behind the rule: for a high to be finished, the last transaction at the extreme has to show one side giving up. If only buyers print at the top and then the market immediately rejects lower, the buyers who chased got punished and no fresh seller had to fight them at that price — the auction is clean, and it leaves a tail. But if both buyers and sellers are still swinging at the top tick when the candle closes, the fight was interrupted, not won. That two-sided print is the tell.

The same idea exists one level up in Market Profile language, which matters because it lets you carry the signal to higher timeframes where footprint data thins out. A finished extreme shows up as excess — a long single-print tail at the edge of the profile. An unfinished extreme shows up as a poor high or poor low: multiple TPOs or heavy volume stacked flat at the extreme with no tapering tail. Both descriptions point at the same structural fact — was the edge of the move properly rejected, or merely abandoned?

One discipline point from Trader Dale: an isolated unfinished print is weak. Probability of a revisit climbs sharply when several unfinished extremes cluster within a tight zone — three or more stacked close together turn a curiosity into a genuine target. A lone two-sided high on a 1-minute bar is noise; a cluster of them at a swing high is an appointment.

§04
In Practice
Targets not fades · clusters · confluence · the completion trigger
The one rule: trade toward an unfinished level as a target, never against it on first touch — the level is a magnet, and the tradeable signal is how the auction finishes when price gets there.
1

Mark Them, Use Them As Targets

Unfinished highs and lows are the cleanest take-profit objectives on the chart, because price has a documented tendency to return and complete them. If you are long, an unfinished high above is a logical TP; if you are short, an unfinished low below is your cover zone. They answer "where does this move go?" far better than they answer "where do I enter?"

2

Demand A Cluster, Not A Lone Print

A single two-sided extreme on a fast timeframe is noise. The probability of a revisit rises sharply when three or more unfinished prints stack inside a tight band, or when one survives on the 1h/4h. Weight clusters; ignore isolated 1m prints. The cluster is what turns a curiosity into an appointment the market keeps.

3

Stack Confluence Before You Trust It

An unfinished level earns its weight when it coincides with something else: a naked POC, session/anchored VWAP, a prior swing high or low, or a visible liquidation / stop cluster. The magnet pull and the resting liquidity reinforce each other. An unfinished level floating in the middle of nowhere is the weakest version of the signal.

4

Enter On The Completion, Not The Touch

When price returns to the level, read the footprint as it finishes. A clean one-sided rejection (excess) at the level is your reversal entry; a two-sided print that breaks straight through means the magnet has been consumed and you trade continuation instead. The unfinished level tells you where to stand — the completion print tells you what to do.

§07
Common Traps
Five ways traders misread unfinished business
The single biggest mistake: fading an unfinished extreme on first touch — it is a magnet that pulls price toward it, not a wall to lean against.
1
Fading The Level Instead Of Targeting It

The reflex is to short an unfinished high or buy an unfinished low on the first touch. That inverts the logic. The level is a magnet price is drawn toward — so use it as a target for an existing position, and only consider a reversal once price arrives and the auction visibly completes with a one-sided print. Leaning against it on the approach gets you run over.

2
Trading Isolated Prints On Fast Bars

A lone two-sided extreme on a 1m candle is statistical noise — almost every fast bar has them. The signal lives in clusters (three-plus stacked) and in prints that survive on the 1h/4h. If you mark every unfinished cell, your chart becomes a forest and the real magnets disappear into it.

3
Confusing Unfinished Business With Single Prints

They are opposites. A single print (low-volume node) marks a zone price ripped through with no interest — an acceleration band on the revisit. An unfinished auction marks an extreme price left without resolving — a magnet it returns to complete. One is a launch pad, the other is a destination. Trading them the same way is a category error.

4
Trusting Approximated Footprint Data

The whole signal depends on correctly classified bid-versus-ask volume at the extreme. Lower-tier or aggregated feeds infer the aggressor side and can mislabel a one-sided extreme as two-sided, or vice versa. Use a tool that ingests true tick data — ATAS, Bookmap, Sierra Chart, Exocharts — and know what your data source actually measures before you trade off a single cell.

5
Treating "Markets Always Fill Unfinished Auctions" As Law

Honest framing: this is partly survivorship bias. Inside a range, price revisits most levels eventually, so "the unfinished low got filled" often proves nothing the concept specifically predicted. Unfinished business is a real, mechanically sound microstructure tell — but as a standalone, blind signal it is weak. It earns its keep only as a target-selection and confluence tool layered on structure, never as a setup you trade on its own.
