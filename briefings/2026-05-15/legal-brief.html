Kevin Warsh Confirmed as Fed Chair 54-45 Wednesday 13 May — Closest Confirmation in Modern Era, Only Sen. John Fetterman (D-PA) Crosses; Powell's Chair Term Ends Today Friday 15 May But He Remains on Board of Governors, First Outgoing Chair Since Marriner Eccles in 1948; Warsh's First FOMC 16-17 June

"I will use my own judgment in setting monetary policy and I will not take orders from the White House." — Kevin Warsh, Senate Banking Committee confirmation testimony (paraphrased by NPR, 13 May 2026)

The Senate confirmed Kevin Warsh as the 17th Chair of the Federal Reserve on Wednesday 13 May on a 54-45 vote, with Sen. John Fetterman (D-PA) the sole Democratic crossover. NPR characterised the margin as the closest confirmation in the modern era for a Fed Chair, reflecting how thoroughly the institution has become a partisan flashpoint under the second Trump administration. Warsh, a Bush-era Fed governor who served 2006-11 and was a key author of the 2008 emergency lending playbook, was Trump's pick after Jerome Powell publicly declined to seek a third term in April.

Powell's term as Chair ends today, Friday 15 May 2026 — but in a sharp break with modern practice he will remain on the Board of Governors as a regular governor (his governor term runs until 2028). The last outgoing Chair to do this was Marriner Eccles in 1948, who stayed on after Truman declined to renominate him. Powell's stated reason, per his 29 April press conference, was to "safeguard the institution from political pressure" during the leadership transition. The practical effect: Powell retains his FOMC vote, his voice in policy debate, and — critically for the GENIUS Act implementing rules — a vote on the Fed's payment-stablecoin supervisory framework currently being co-drafted with the OCC and FDIC.

Warsh's first FOMC meeting as Chair is 16-17 June 2026. He has consistently argued there is room to cut rates and has been a public advocate for stablecoin equivalence with bank deposits and for federal trust-charter expansion for digital-asset custodians.

📎 Closest precedent: the Greenspan-Bernanke transition (Jan 2006) followed the standard pattern of the outgoing Chair leaving the Board. Powell's decision to stay echoes Eccles 1948 and is unprecedented in the post-Volcker era.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Davis Polk's 13 May client alert on the Fed leadership transition, the institutional impact runs through three channels practitioners should watch: (i) GENIUS Act implementing rules — Warsh's pro-stablecoin posture aligns with the OCC's payment-stablecoin NPRM (comments closed 1 May) and signals fast-track adoption of a final rule before the GENIUS Act's 18 July 2026 statutory deadline; (ii) federal trust-charter expansion — Augustus N.A. (approved 11 May, covered in Tuesday's brief) becomes the template, with Warsh expected to push reciprocity arrangements that let state-chartered crypto banks access Fed payment rails; (iii) monetary policy and crypto-collateral — Warsh's prior writings explicitly contemplate tokenised Treasuries as monetary-policy collateral, which puts the BlackRock BUIDL / Ondo OUSG / Franklin BENJI universe directly in the path of Fed open-market operations. Powell's continued presence on the Board is the load-bearing variable: he can dissent on any of the above, and his vote matters in the 7-member Board universe even if he no longer chairs the FOMC.

Warsh dissented from QE2 and was publicly skeptical of unconventional easing — meaning a Warsh-led Fed could surprise to the hawkish side on the monetary-policy track even while liberalising digital-asset infrastructure rules. The cleanest read: liquidity easing for crypto-banking, monetary tightening relative to expectations on the macro track. Those two crosscurrents could cancel out for risk assets.

House Ways & Means Convenes Closed-Door Bipartisan Crypto-Tax Meeting Thursday 14 May on Miller-Horsford Digital Asset PARITY Act — $200 Stablecoin De Minimis (GENIUS-Compliant Issuers Only), Five-Year Staking Reward Deferral, Wash-Sale Loophole Closure; Miller Targets Pre-August 2026 Floor Action

"The IRS hasn't caught up to the way digital assets actually work — staking rewards, stablecoin payments, mining. We are losing US tax base every day this stays unfixed. I expect this bill to advance before August." — Rep. Max Miller (R-OH), prepared remarks accompanying the PARITY Act release [paraphrased by The Block; verbatim quote pending committee transcript]

The House Ways and Means Committee convened a closed-door bipartisan working session Thursday 14 May on the Digital Asset PARITY Act, introduced jointly by Rep. Max Miller (R-OH) and Rep. Steven Horsford (D-NV) — both Ways and Means members. The bill is the House's tax-side counterpart to the Senate's CLARITY Act market-structure work, and the parallel-day scheduling was deliberate: it forces TradFi-adjacent Democratic Ways and Means members to engage on the substantive merits of crypto-tax modernisation at the same moment Banking Democrats are being held accountable on market structure.

Three core provisions, per the bill text and the Miller release: (i) a $200 de minimis exemption for retail payments made in GENIUS Act-compliant stablecoins — effectively making USDC, RLUSD, PYUSD, and any future US-qualifying-issuer stablecoin tax-free for everyday consumer purchases under the threshold; (ii) a five-year tax deferral on staking and mining rewards, shifting the taxable event from receipt to disposal — aligning crypto with how stock dividend reinvestment plans treat realisation; (iii) closure of the wash-sale loophole that currently lets crypto holders harvest losses then immediately repurchase — bringing crypto in line with §1091 securities treatment.

The de minimis figure is the contentious one — Coin Center had pushed for $600 (aligned with the 1099-MISC threshold) but the Joint Committee on Taxation's revenue score forced the lower number. The five-year deferral structure was modeled directly on the Lummis-Wyden 2024 staking-tax bill and survives intact.

📎 Closest precedent: the Cryptocurrency Tax Fairness Act (2017, never enacted) proposed a $600 de minimis. PARITY is the first version to combine de minimis with realisation-event reform in a single vehicle.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per a 13 May BDO digital-asset tax alert and a 14 May Davis Polk tax-committee memo: the GENIUS-compliance precondition on the $200 de minimis is the most consequential provision in the bill, not the headline numbers. By limiting the exemption to qualifying-issuer stablecoins, the PARITY Act effectively builds a tax preference into the GENIUS Act's regulatory perimeter — non-qualifying stablecoins (USDT, algorithmic stablecoins, offshore issuers) face the full capital-gains regime for every transaction, while qualifying issuers get a meaningful retail-payments moat. This is the same regulatory architecture the EU used to drive volume from USDT to USDC under MiCA, and it works because tax treatment is a sharper distribution lever than licensing alone. For tax counsel: clients holding USDT for transactional rather than treasury purposes should model the post-PARITY cost basis carefully — the conversion path matters.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Per a 14 May Sullivan & Cromwell tax-policy note: the bill's biggest risk is not Democratic opposition — Horsford is a meaningful crossover and Ways and Means tax bills historically attract broader bipartisan support than financial-services bills — but rather scoring. The Joint Committee on Taxation's revenue score on the wash-sale closure will be net-positive (closes a real loophole), but the five-year staking deferral scores as negative revenue at a moment when the 2025 tax cuts are being baselined for 2027 reconciliation fights. If JCT scores PARITY at >$2 B in lost revenue over the 10-year window, Speaker-side leadership may use it as a chip in broader 2027-baseline negotiations rather than passing it standalone in 2026.

The Brief's read: The most consequential second-order effect is on Visa and Mastercard — the $200 stablecoin de minimis makes USDC-Visa rails directly competitive with card interchange for small-ticket retail purchases, and the merchant-side economics flip.

"He's done the exact opposite, which I find very duplicitous. He's leading the CFTC down this path to fully embrace prediction markets... [The CFTC is] completely unequipped to regulate this industry, and [Kalshi's sports contracts are] pretty indistinguishable from gaming and sports betting." — Sen. Sen. Adam Schiff — who sits on the Senate Agriculture Committee (the CFTC's authorising committee) — used a Brookings Institution panel on Wednesday 13 May to escalate the prediction-markets jurisdictional fight into a public personal-credibility attack on CFTC Chair Mike Selig. Schiff alleged that during a prior Senate hearing Selig had committed to allowing federal courts to resolve the federal-preemption question on state sports-betting laws, but had instead pursued an administrative path "to fully embrace prediction markets." Schiff's word for the alleged reversal: "duplicitous."

The substantive vehicle is two bipartisan bills running on parallel tracks. (i) The Prediction Markets Are Gambling Act (Schiff-Curtis, introduced March 2026) would prohibit CFTC-registered Designated Contract Markets from offering sports-related or casino-style event contracts, returning regulatory authority over those products to state and tribal gaming regulators. (ii) The Public Integrity in Financial Prediction Markets Act of 2026 (S.4188) (Curtis-Slotkin-Young-Schiff, lead sponsor Curtis) would bar federal officials and federal employees from trading prediction-market contracts on information obtained through their official duties — directly responsive to the April 2026 CFTC-charged Army service member who traded Polymarket on classified military intelligence.

The procedural fight matters because the CFTC has been moving on its own track: Selig confirmed at FINRA on 12 May that the agency has signed an MOU with MLB and is in "active talks with all major US pro sports leagues" on insider-trading surveillance cooperation (yesterday's brief). Schiff's argument is that surveillance cooperation does not solve the categorisation problem — sports event contracts, in his framing, simply are not commodity derivatives.

📎 Closest precedent: PASPA (Professional and Amateur Sports Protection Act 1992), struck down by the Supreme Court in Murphy v NCAA (2018), is the analogical structure Schiff's bill leans on — but Murphy invalidated PASPA on anti-commandeering grounds, which cuts the other way for a CFTC-specific federal ban.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Sidley's 2 April 2026 prediction-markets bulletin and Davis Polk's 14 May "CFTC Authority" client alert: the legal architecture turns on whether sports event contracts are "swaps" under §1a(47) of the CEA or are gambling under the Wire Act and state gaming statutes. The Schiff bills assume the categorisation is severable — that Congress can carve out sports/casino-style contracts from the CFTC's jurisdiction without unwinding the agency's broader event-contract framework. Selig's position, as articulated in his 10 March CFTC speech and the 12 March 2026 SEC-CFTC joint interpretation, is that the categorisation is unitary — once an event contract clears the CEA's manipulation/contrary-to-public-interest test, the agency has exclusive jurisdiction. The Brief's read: The five-state federal-preemption litigation pipeline (NJ, NV, OH, MA, plus a fifth state Selig referenced in March) is still the load-bearing track; Schiff's bills are a parallel legislative pressure campaign that will only matter if Democrats retake the Senate in 2026.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Per a 13 May Coin Center policy memo: the public Selig-Schiff exchange is good news for Kalshi and Polymarket, not bad news. Schiff's "duplicitous" framing presupposes that Selig has the authority to do the thing Schiff doesn't want him to do — which is itself a tacit concession on jurisdiction. The bipartisan bills face the same August 2026 calendar wall as CLARITY: with Senate Agriculture distracted by market-structure reconciliation and the Schiff bills opposed by the platforms' considerable lobbying spend, the realistic path is that nothing passes this Congress and the courts continue to resolve federal preemption case by case. CFTC Chair Selig's FINRA Annual Conference Speech 13 May — Innovation Task Force Engaging LLM Developers and Trading Firms; Flags "Agentic Finance" (Autonomous Software Agents Trading Without Human Intermediary) as Top Emerging Risk Category Requiring New Rules

"Our Innovation Task Force is hard at work ensuring those developing the next generation of blockchain technologies, AI, prediction markets, and more have good reason to build them in America." — CFTC Chair Mike Selig, FINRA Annual Conference 2026 (13 May 2026)

Selig used his FINRA Annual Conference keynote — same conference where he confirmed the MLB MOU on Tuesday (yesterday's brief) — to lay out the most detailed framework yet for how the CFTC plans to regulate AI in derivatives markets. Three structural points: (i) the Innovation Task Force is in active dialogue with LLM developers, trading-software firms, and blockchain-application teams to map where existing rules fall short; (ii) the agency is explicitly contemplating "agentic finance" — autonomous software agents that execute trades without continuous human intermediation — as its top emerging risk category; and (iii) the CFTC is studying blockchain-based attestation systems as a verification rail for AI-generated content in market communications (responding to deepfake-driven market-manipulation risk).

The agentic-finance framing matters because it reaches well beyond "AI assists a trader" — it contemplates fully delegated execution authority, raising live questions about CFTC §4d(a)(1) supervisory duties when there is no natural-person trader to supervise, and about Reg AT-style algorithmic pre-trade risk controls being reframed for stateless smart-contract execution. Selig did not name a specific NPRM date but indicated rulemaking is on the agenda for the second half of 2026.

📎 Closest precedent: the CFTC's 2015 Regulation Automated Trading (Reg AT) proposal, withdrawn in 2020 after industry pushback; agentic finance reopens the same questions on the smart-contract side.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per a 14 May Sidley client alert on Selig's speech: the Innovation Task Force's posture is consultative-first, rulemaking-second — meaning the firms that engage with the Task Force this summer will shape the eventual NPRM in a way that late-stage commenters will not. Per Sullivan & Cromwell's 13 May derivatives memo: the agentic-finance category is the right framing because it tracks how the actual technology is developing (Anthropic's MCP, OpenAI's agents framework, on-chain agent kits like Coinbase Agents) but it cuts across CFTC, SEC, and prudential-bank supervision in ways that will require inter-agency coordination — exactly the kind of work the 11 March 2026 SEC-CFTC MOU was designed to enable. The Brief's read: Watch for a coordinated SEC-CFTC AI-in-finance request for information in Q3, mirroring the Project Crypto rollout.

Indirect: any high-frequency trading firm with an autonomous-execution layer (Citadel Securities, Jump, Wintermute) gains an authoritative regulatory framework rather than the current ambiguity. NEC Director Kevin Hassett provided the most concrete description on 7 May: a mandatory federal review process before public release, focused on the cybersecurity and bioweapons risk surface, with the FDA's drug-approval framework as the structural model.

The trigger was Anthropic's disclosure that its breakthrough Mythos model autonomously discovered and exploited network vulnerabilities at a rate sufficient to constitute a global cybersecurity risk. Within 48 hours of the leak, the White House softened the framing to "studying" rather than imminent — but the draft 16-page EO text reportedly contains provisions on (i) pre-release safety attestations, (ii) open-weight model export controls, (iii) federal contracting preferences for evaluated models, and (iv) a CAISI-administered review process. CAISI (NIST's Center for AI Standards and Innovation) already has pre-deployment evaluation agreements with OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Microsoft, and xAI (covered in Thursday's brief).

⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per a 12 May Wilmer Hale AI-governance alert: the FDA-style framing is legally awkward because the FDA's authority rests on the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act's specific delegation, while frontier-AI authority would have to rest on either the Defense Production Act, an interagency cybersecurity authority, or new legislation. Per Hunton's 13 May Trump-AI tracker: the path of least resistance is to layer the EO on top of the existing CAISI agreements rather than creating a new statutory framework — which means lab-by-lab voluntary attestations with federal-contracting consequences for non-compliance. The Brief's read: The walk-back is consistent with how this administration has handled other AI EOs — float the maximal version, gauge industry reaction, then narrow. Expect the final EO (if it issues) to look more like the existing CAISI MOU framework with statutory teeth added.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Frontier labs (Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI, Google DeepMind, Microsoft): existing CAISI agreements get formalised; compliance cost rises modestly. Open-weight model publishers (Meta Llama, Mistral, AI2): export-control provisions on open weights are the genuine binding constraint and could materially reshape release strategies. Federal contractors: a "evaluated-models-only" procurement preference would force vendor selection by AISIC certification, advantaging Anthropic and OpenAI over open-weight alternatives. The Brief's read: The procurement lever is the only one with real teeth — the public-release ban path is constitutionally challenging, but withholding federal contracts from non-evaluated models is administratively durable.

This week resolved two of the three biggest structural overhangs in US crypto-financial regulation. By any measure, this was the most consequential single legislative week for US digital-asset law since the GENIUS Act enactment in July 2025.

This week's single most important development: Not CLARITY itself, but the two-Democrat crossover at committee. The bill always had Republican unanimity in committee — that wasn't dispositive. Gallego and Alsobrooks voting yes at the committee level tells you the floor whip count starts at 60-eligible, not 53-eligible. That is the dispositive structural change.
