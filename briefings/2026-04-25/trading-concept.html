Alpha — Bid/Ask Imbalance

Saturday · 25 April 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #3 10-min read
TL;DR

Bid/Ask Imbalance — also called Order Book Imbalance (OBI) — is the real-time snapshot of asymmetric pressure sitting in the limit order book: how much resting volume is on the buy side versus the sell side, expressed as a ratio from −1 (pure ask dominance) to +1 (pure bid dominance). The practical edge: a heavy positive OBI at a known support level confirms that resting size is absorbing downside — the market is structurally resistant to selling. A negative OBI building at resistance tells you the book is fighting price before a single candle of rejection prints. With BTC grinding at $77,520 into the $79K–$80K resistance zone right now, the order book is already showing that story.

§01
What It Is
The pre-trade snapshot · resting volume asymmetry · structural lean
OBI is the book's opinion of value before anyone throws a punch — it measures the imbalance in resting limit orders, not what has already traded.

The order book at any given moment contains thousands of resting limit orders on both sides of the market. On the bid side: buyers willing to purchase at or below the current price. On the ask side: sellers willing to sell at or above it. Bid/Ask Imbalance (OBI) measures whether those two sides are in balance or whether one dramatically outweighs the other — and whether that asymmetry is concentrated at the top of the book (best bid/ask) or spread across deeper levels.

The critical distinction from CVD (yesterday's concept): CVD measures what has already happened — the running score of aggressive market orders crossing the spread. OBI measures what is pending — the structural weight of resting limit orders before any aggression occurs. CVD is the scoreboard; OBI is the crowd noise before the opening bell. You need both, and they tell different parts of the same story.

OBI is genuinely pre-trade information. It exists in the order book before a single transaction occurs at a price level. This makes it one of the few leading indicators that isn't derived from price action — it precedes the price action. Research across major equity markets has shown OBI explains ~65% of short-interval mid-price changes (Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov, 2014) — a figure that is high for any single microstructure metric. In crypto, with thinner books and higher concentration of passive walls, the predictive window is often even shorter but more extreme in magnitude.

Practically: a deep bid wall at $77,000 doesn't guarantee that price won't go there. But it does tell you that if price visits $77,000, someone large has pre-committed to absorbing it. Whether that wall holds or gets pulled (spoofing) is a separate question — and one of the core traps covered in §07.

If you only remember one thing OBI tells you which side of the market has pre-committed structural weight. It's the book's lean — a lean that can confirm, warn, or mislead you depending on whether the orders are real.
§02
Mechanics
Formula · three measurement approaches · depth weighting
One formula, three flavours: top-of-book ratio, cumulative depth ratio, and distance-weighted ratio — each reading a different slice of structural pressure.

The base formula is straightforward: subtract ask-side volume from bid-side volume and normalize by total. This produces a value between −1 and +1 that can be applied to any depth window you choose.

Order Book Imbalance (core): OBI = (Bid Volume − Ask Volume) / (Bid Volume + Ask Volume) Range: +1.0 → 100% bid dominance (no asks in window) 0.0 → perfectly balanced −1.0 → 100% ask dominance (no bids in window) Example — BTC/USDT.P top-of-book: Best bid: 14.2 BTC at $77,400 Best ask: 3.8 BTC at $77,450 OBI = (14.2 − 3.8) / (14.2 + 3.8) = 10.4 / 18.0 = +0.578 → Strong bid lean at the top of book Bearish signal threshold (common): OBI < −0.30 at a known resistance → ask side structural dominance OBI > +0.30 at a known support → bid side structural dominance
Note: Some platforms and professional tools define OBI using queue-weighted depth (volumes weighted by their distance from mid-price, so inner levels count more). The basic formula above uses raw volume — most retail tools implement this version.

The three measurement approaches serve different purposes. Top-of-book OBI (best bid vs best ask only) is the most reactive — it changes in milliseconds and is what HFT algorithms read for short-horizon edge. Cumulative depth OBI averages across multiple levels (e.g. top 10 bids vs top 10 asks), smoothing out transient noise and better capturing structural walls. Weighted depth OBI discounts levels far from mid-price on the basis that volume sitting $500 away from current price is less immediately relevant than volume sitting $5 away.

Research by Mastrolia and Ranaldo (2023) found something asymmetric and practically important: negative OBI (ask dominance) predicts downward price moves more reliably than positive OBI predicts upward moves. In other words, the book is better at telling you when to not be long than when to aggressively buy. This fits with crypto market structure — large passive sellers (exchanges, OTC desks, miners hedging) create visible order flow that absorbs rallies, while the bid side is more commonly spoofed by market-makers gaming the queue.

§04
In Practice
Four rules · worked entry · timeframe fit
The one rule: OBI is a confirmation tool, not an entry trigger — it grades the context, not the candle.
1

Confirm Entries at Known Support

Before taking a long at a structural level, check whether cumulative depth OBI is positive. A positive OBI at a Wyckoff spring or Value Area Low tells you the book agrees with your thesis — resting size is there absorbing. Negative OBI at "support" is a red flag: the bid walls may be thin or manufactured.

2

Fade Entries Into Negative OBI at Resistance

When price approaches a known resistance zone and top-of-book OBI is running below −0.25, the ask side has pre-loaded structural pressure. This is not the moment to chase long. Research consistently shows negative OBI predicts downward mid-price moves more reliably than positive OBI predicts upward — so the asymmetry favours fading resistance into a negative book.

3

Watch for the OBI Flip Intraday

An OBI flip from positive to negative at a key level is higher-signal than OBI in either direction independently. The flip means the market-making community has rotated: they're now leaning short. On a 5m chart, three consecutive negative OBI readings into resistance is a clean short trigger with a stop above the wall. Reverse for flip from negative to positive at support.

4

Layer OBI With CVD for the Full Flow Picture

OBI (resting orders) + CVD (aggressive orders) together give you the complete state of market pressure: the snapshot and the running score. The highest-conviction setup is when both agree: positive OBI at support + rising CVD = buying pressure on both passive and aggressive sides. Divergence between the two (e.g. positive OBI but falling CVD) is a warning that the visible book may not reflect actual flow.

§07
Common Traps
Spoofing · time horizon decay · snapshot vs flow confusion · hidden orders
The biggest mistake: treating OBI as a durable signal. It's a snapshot — it can disappear in milliseconds if the orders are fake.
1
Spoofing — The Bid Wall That Evaporates

Large resting orders that appear before price approaches and vanish before they can be hit are common in crypto. A positive OBI of +0.80 driven by a 50 BTC bid wall that gets pulled when price gets close is not structural support — it's a market-maker or manipulator gaming the queue. The tell: watch whether the large order remains as price approaches it, or disappears. Persistent walls that hold under pressure are real; walls that vanish 20–50 ticks before price arrives are not.

2
Confusing OBI (Snapshot) with CVD (Flow)

OBI measures resting orders; CVD measures executed orders. They describe completely different phenomena. A market with positive OBI but sharply declining CVD is telling a contradictory story — the visible book looks supportive but actual aggressive selling is overpowering it. That divergence is the real signal: someone is selling into the bid wall. Never treat OBI in isolation from what is actually trading through it.

3
OBI Signals Decay Fast — Using Wrong Timeframes

Research is consistent: top-of-book OBI has strong predictive power over seconds to a few minutes. It degrades rapidly beyond that. Using a 5-minute-old OBI reading to justify a 1-hour trade is applying the wrong tool to the wrong horizon. If you are swing trading, OBI is contextual colour (is the book structurally bid or ask heavy at this zone?), not a timing trigger. Don't take entries at H4 S/R solely because OBI looked good 20 minutes ago.

4
Ignoring Hidden and Iceberg Orders

OBI only sees what is visible in the order book. Institutional participants routinely use iceberg orders (which show only a small slice of their full size) or dark pools and OTC venues (which are off-exchange entirely). A book that looks balanced or even ask-heavy can have a massive passive buyer sitting behind an iceberg at the bid. This is precisely why OBI needs context: volume profile, CVD, and tape speed together help reveal whether the visible book is complete or just a fragment of the real positioning.

5
Honest Callout: OBI Is Genuinely Powerful — But Only in Context

Unlike some rebadged orderflow terminology that overpromises and underdelivers, OBI is academically robust (R² ~65% for short-interval price changes in equities) and practically useful in crypto. The anti-hype caveat is about application: OBI alone is not a trade. It is a grade of the context. Every high-conviction OBI-based entry also needs a structural level, a confirmation from flow (CVD), and a clear invalidation. Traders who use OBI without the other two layers get the right concept and still lose — because context is the concept.
