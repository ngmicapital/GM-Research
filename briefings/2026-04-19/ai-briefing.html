What Is Mixture of Experts — And Why It Powers Most of the Models You'll See in 2026

Every major model release this quarter — Meta's Llama 4 Maverick, NVIDIA's Nemotron 3 Super, DeepSeek V3.2 — uses an architecture called Mixture of Experts (MoE). GPT-6 almost certainly does too. Understanding it changes how you interpret benchmark numbers and pricing.

Standard "dense" transformers activate all their parameters on every token they process. If a model has 70 billion parameters, all 70 billion are computing simultaneously for every word you type. That's powerful but expensive — both in electricity and inference cost. MoE splits the model into specialised sub-networks called "experts", each good at different types of reasoning. A routing mechanism (a learned gate) decides which 2–4 experts to activate for each token. Most experts sit idle most of the time.

The result: a model can have the capacity of a 400B-parameter dense model but the cost of running a 40–50B model, because only a fraction of parameters fire per token. Meta's Llama 4 Maverick is a 400B total-parameter model with roughly 17B active parameters per forward pass. That's why it runs fast enough for consumer hardware and API pricing that beats dense alternatives.

The tradeoff is real: MoE models can be harder to train stably (expert routing collapses are a common failure mode), they require more memory to load even when most of it is idle, and quality on tasks that require all knowledge simultaneously can lag dense models at equivalent active parameter counts. The industry solution — currently being adopted across labs — is better routing training and larger expert pools.

Why this matters for the AI landscape: MoE is the reason the cost-per-token curve keeps falling without model quality regressing. As routing improves, you'll see 1T-parameter total models with active compute equivalent to a 30B dense model. When GPT-6 ships and the architecture is disclosed, this framework will tell you immediately whether OpenAI's cost claims are credible — and whether Claude Opus 4.7's dense architecture is being priced fairly relative to its compute.

[Fundamentals]

What an Agent Loop Is — And Why It Keeps Breaking

Every AI tool marketed as "agentic" in 2026 is built on the same underlying pattern: an agent loop. Understanding it explains why agents succeed on some tasks and fail spectacularly on others — and it's directly relevant to how Claude Code Routines work.

The loop has four steps: (1) Observe — the model receives the current state of the world (a task, a codebase, a conversation). (2) Plan — it generates a sequence of actions to take. (3) Act — it calls tools: reading files, running code, fetching URLs, writing to databases. (4) Reflect — it receives the result of those tool calls and updates its understanding. Then the loop restarts. What makes it "agentic" rather than just "chatting" is that step 3 can have real-world consequences — code gets written, PRs get created, emails get sent.

The failure modes are structural. Context window pressure: each loop iteration adds tokens to the context. Long-running agents eventually hit the limit and either truncate their own history (losing context) or stop entirely. Error propagation: a wrong assumption in step 2 of loop 1 compounds through every subsequent loop iteration. Unlike a human who notices when they've gone down the wrong path, a model following its own prior reasoning tends to double down. Tool reliability: if any tool call returns unexpected output (a network timeout, an auth error, a changed API schema), the agent often doesn't gracefully recover — it either hallucinates a result or halts.

Claude Code Routines addresses the context problem by giving agents a fresh session per run. Cloudflare's Code Mode (Issue #22) addresses tool reliability by narrowing the tool surface. MemPalace (Issue #22) addresses the context retention problem between sessions. These aren't independent innovations — they're solving the same three structural failure modes from different angles.

Why this matters for the AI landscape: The entire industry is converging on agent-loop infrastructure. The next two years of AI product development is less about making smarter models and more about making the loop reliable enough to trust with consequential tasks. Any tool that claims to be "agentic" should be evaluated against all three failure modes: context, error propagation, and tool reliability.

What LoRA Is — The Technique That Made Open-Source AI Models Actually Viable

Every time you see a HuggingFace model with "fine-tuned on [domain]" in its description, or a news story about a small team training a specialised AI model on a consumer GPU, LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is almost certainly the reason it was possible at all.

Fine-tuning a large language model from scratch means adjusting billions of parameters — GPT-scale training runs cost tens of millions of dollars and take months on specialised hardware. LoRA sidesteps this by observing that model adaptations for specific tasks tend to have a low intrinsic dimensionality: the change needed to shift a general-purpose model into a specialised one can be represented by a much smaller set of numbers than the model itself. Instead of modifying all the original weights, LoRA inserts small, trainable matrices (rank decompositions) at each layer. The original model is frozen; only the LoRA adapters are trained.

The practical result: fine-tuning a 70B-parameter model with LoRA can be done on a single A100 GPU in hours for a few hundred dollars, versus millions of dollars for full fine-tuning. The adapters — the trained LoRA weights — are typically 10–100MB files, not the 40GB+ of the base model. They snap onto any compatible base model and can be swapped in and out, enabling things like "coding LoRA" and "medical LoRA" running on the same base model with a simple switch.

Variants have proliferated: QLoRA adds quantisation to reduce memory requirements further; DoRA improves quality by separating magnitude and direction updates; LoRA-the-Explorer (a 2026 research direction) is attempting to make the rank selection automatic rather than a hyperparameter. The HuggingFace PEFT library has made all of this one-command accessible.

Why this matters for the AI landscape: LoRA is the reason the open-source AI ecosystem is competitive with proprietary labs. Without it, you need billion-dollar infrastructure to specialise a model. With it, a domain expert with a dataset and a consumer GPU can produce a model that outperforms GPT-5 on their specific task. The entire wave of specialised AI applications in healthcare, legal, finance, and trading is built on this foundation.

RAISE Act — First Binding Federal Frontier AI Transparency Law, Effective March 19

The RAISE Act (Responsible AI Safety and Explainability) took effect on March 19, 2026 — the first binding federal law imposing specific requirements on frontier AI developers. Not a framework document; actual compliance obligations. The law requires:

  • Transparency — public disclosure of training data sources and safety evaluations
  • Compliance documentation — internal records of testing against defined capability thresholds
  • Safety reporting — incident disclosure requirements for harmful outputs above threshold severity

The law applies to developers of "large frontier AI models" — defined by compute thresholds that currently capture OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, xAI, and Meta. Smaller open-source projects are below the compute threshold. Separately, the White House National Policy Framework (March 20) is moving through legislative consultation, with its federal preemption recommendation — one national AI standard vs. 50 state laws — as the core political fight for 2026.

The RAISE Act changes Anthropic's transparency obligations, not its product roadmap. Practically: expect more detailed safety evaluation disclosures in model release notes going forward. The federal preemption fight will determine whether state-level AI laws (California SB 1047-style) survive — a longer-term story to monitor.

AI Scientist v2: Autonomous Scientific Discovery via Agentic Tree Search
Sakana AI · April 2026 · arXiv + conference accepted

Why it matters in 6 months: Passing peer review is the first credible benchmark for AI-generated scientific output. Mathematical Methods and Human Thought in the Age of AI
Terence Tao · arXiv · April 2026

One-line finding: Tao argues for a "Copernican View of Intelligence" — positioning AI not as a replacement for human mathematical reasoning but as a new computing substrate that changes what mathematicians should focus on, shifting human effort toward creative insight and conjecture, with AI handling verification and search.

Why it matters in 6 months: When one of the world's most capable mathematicians publishes a framework for human-AI collaboration in his own domain, it's worth reading. The practical implication for AI: Tao's framework suggests the highest-value human contribution in any AI-augmented field is now at the conjecture layer, not the execution layer. For trading specifically, this maps directly: AI handles pattern search and rule execution; human insight is the conjecture (what to look for, which market structure is playing out). Wyckoff methodology fits this division cleanly — the methodology is the conjecture layer; the scanner is the execution layer.
