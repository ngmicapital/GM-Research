01 Key Ideas & Insights
Newsletter · Mark Manson Breakthrough #202

Stop Chasing Happiness — You're Preventing It

Mark Manson (Breakthrough #202, April 13) leads with one of the most economical reframes in recent memory. Happiness, he argues, doesn't work like a goal you pursue — it works like sleep. The harder you chase it, the more it eludes you. The moment you stop forcing it and remove the obstacles in its way, it arrives on its own.

"Trying to be happy is like trying to fall asleep. Forcing it prevents it. You don't make it happen; you remove the impediments to it happening on its own."

The payoff idea is even more striking: "If you stop chasing happiness for long enough, you will find it was there all along." This isn't passive acceptance — it's an active shift in methodology. The question changes from what do I need to acquire? to what am I doing that blocks what's already here?

Manson's reader stories this week add texture. A physician near burnout realised that imagining the loss of her career immediately restored her sense of meaning in it. A woman in recovery used the same lens — what if her sobriety disappeared? — to recognise how completely her life had transformed. The thread running through all three stories: gratitude isn't a feeling you cultivate in the abstract. It surfaces when you properly reckon with what absence would look like.

  • Invert the goal. Instead of asking "how do I get happier?", ask "what am I currently doing that makes happiness harder?" The obstacle is the strategy.
  • Use negative visualisation as a diagnostic. Pick something you're complaining about. Imagine it disappearing. If that imagined loss shocks you, you've found something you're taking for granted.
  • Stop treating happiness as a destination. It's a side-effect of living well — not a reward for achieving enough.
Newsletter · School of Practical Philosophy — Weekly Wisdom #019

See Clearly First, Judge Second: The Discipline of Dropping Your Views

The School of Practical Philosophy (Weekly Wisdom #019, April 14) opens with a quote from Thich Nhat Hanh that reads as a near-complete epistemology in one sentence: "For things to reveal themselves to us, we need to be ready to abandon our views about them."

"For things to reveal themselves to us, we need to be ready to abandon our views about them." — Thich Nhat Hanh

The editorial develops this into a critique of how modern information culture operates. We are under constant pressure to form instant evaluations — to sort, categorise, and respond before we've actually seen what's in front of us. The result is that most people are reasoning from inherited conclusions, applying old templates to new situations, and wondering why their decisions keep producing the same outcomes.

The practice they're pointing at is subtle but specific: clear perception before judgment. Not the suspension of all judgment — but the deliberate pause between observation and conclusion that creates space for reality to show itself on its own terms. This is closer to phenomenological attention than to mere open-mindedness. It requires actively loosening the grip of your existing framework before engaging a new one.

  • Notice the template you're applying. When you encounter a new problem or person, ask: am I actually seeing this situation, or am I running a pattern from somewhere else?
  • Slow the evaluation step. Separate observation from conclusion by even a few seconds. The gap is where accurate reasoning lives.
  • Treat your existing views as hypotheses. They may be correct — but holding them as provisional keeps your perception sharper.
Newsletter · Daily Stoic — Meditations Month, Week 2

Marcus Aurelius on What to Do When Things Go Wrong: Get to Work

Ryan Holiday (Daily Stoic, April 14) zooms in on one of the more demanding passages in Meditations: Marcus's instruction to respond to setbacks not with lament, complaint, or analysis, but with immediate acceptance and action. The phrase is terse to the point of being almost confrontational — "Something happened. Good."

"Something happened. Good. It was meant for you by nature, woven into the pattern from the beginning. Instead of lamenting it, instead of fighting it, instead of wondering why it went this way instead of that — embrace it. See it as something happening for you and not to you."

Holiday frames this as the core operating instruction for April's Meditations Month — a community reading of the text alongside thousands of participants globally, timed to Marcus's birthday on April 26. The practical insight is this: the cost of resistance to what's already happened isn't just emotional. It's cognitive. Every minute spent lamenting a fixed outcome is executive bandwidth not applied to the situation as it now stands.

Life is short, Marcus writes. Don't waste time wishing it were otherwise. Get to work on what's actually in front of you.

  • Distinguish the event from the response. The event is fixed. Only your response to it is still in play. Allocate attention accordingly.
  • Replace "why did this happen?" with "what do I do now?" The second question is the only one that moves anything.
  • Resistance to reality is a budget item. It consumes attention, energy, and time that could go toward the actual problem. Price it accordingly before choosing it.
Newsletter · High Performance Founder — Dan Go

The Breakfast Protocol That Eliminates 200 Daily Decisions Before 9am

Dan Go (High Performance Founder, April 14) makes a case that reads as much like a systems-design argument as a nutrition one. The core claim: eating the same breakfast every single day for a year was one of the primary drivers behind dropping to 12% body fat at 46 — not because of the macros alone, but because of the decision overhead it eliminated.

The research he cites is striking: a 12-week study found that people who ate the same meals repeatedly lost around 40% more weight than those who constantly varied their diet. The mechanism isn't willpower — it's cognitive load reduction. We make approximately 200 food-related decisions per day. When your first meal is already solved, you've removed an entire category of friction before the day has started.

"Once you have a system, you stop spending energy on a problem that's already solved. The fittest people I know eat the same things most days — not because they're robots, but because for 80–90% of their meals, the guesswork is gone."

Dan's specific protocol — low-fat Greek yogurt (300g), 1–2 scoops whey protein, 1 tablespoon psyllium husk, 100g frozen fruit, prepped the night before — comes out to ~350 calories, 40–60g protein, 10g fibre. The psyllium husk forms a gel that slows digestion; the protein stack triggers satiety hormones that reduce total daily intake by 80–100 calories without tracking. The deeper insight is the zeitgeber concept: eating a real meal early in the day synchronises your metabolic clock, regulating hunger hormones for the full 24 hours that follow.

  • Solve breakfast once. Prep it the night before. The first decision of the day should be taken care of before you wake up.
  • Routinise the easy 80%. Save cognitive variation for the meals and decisions that actually benefit from it. Most don't.
  • A high-protein, high-fibre first meal is a day-long leverage point. Ghrelin suppression from the morning compounds into evening craving reduction — the investment pays dividends 12 hours later.
02 Strategy & Practice
Synthesis

The Common Thread: Remove Friction Before You Apply Force

This week's material, taken together, points at a single underlying principle: the most effective interventions work by subtraction, not addition.

Mark Manson finds happiness not by adding more of what you want, but by identifying what blocks it. The School of Practical Philosophy finds clearer thinking not by adding stronger opinions, but by loosening your grip on the ones you have. Marcus Aurelius finds equanimity not by adding resistance to setbacks, but by stripping out the self-defeating habit of wishing they hadn't happened. Dan Go finds better body composition not by adding more food choices, but by removing 200 daily decisions from the equation.

The actionable version of this principle: before building something new, ask what you're currently doing that is working against your stated goal. The answer is almost always more tractable than it appears, and fixing it is almost always faster than building around it.

Practice · This Week

Three Subtraction Experiments to Run Before Friday

  • Happiness audit. List the three conditions you're currently telling yourself you need before you can be properly satisfied. Then ask, honestly: is each of those a genuine requirement, or a moving goalpost? Manson's prompt: what would happen if you stopped chasing each one for a week?
  • Perception pause. Pick one recurring interaction or situation where you notice yourself applying a template ("this person always...", "this meeting is just..."). For one week, delay that categorisation by 30 seconds. Observe what you see in the gap.
  • First-decision elimination. Identify one repeating decision in your morning — breakfast, workout, what to read first — and systematise it tonight. Make the decision once, remove it from tomorrow's cognitive budget, and track what that freed bandwidth actually goes toward.
"We suffer more in imagination than in reality." — Seneca — A recurring Stoic framing, relevant to both happiness-chasing and resistance to setbacks

Framework · Decision-Making

The Inversion Principle (Farnam Street / Charlie Munger)

Instead of asking "how do I achieve X?", ask "what would guarantee I fail at X?" then avoid those things. This week's material is essentially an applied inversion: don't chase happiness (ask what blocks it); don't layer more on your day (ask what consumes your decisions). Inversion is particularly powerful for goals involving internal states — where direct pursuit activates the very mechanisms that prevent attainment.

04 On the Horizon
AI · Coaching

AI Life Coaching Going Mainstream

Mark Manson launching "Purpose" as an AI accountability coach signals a broader shift. When high-credibility personal development brands start staking distribution on AI-mediated coaching, the question is no longer whether AI coaches will become common — it's whether the coaching relationship itself changes when the coach is infinitely patient, available at 3am, and never has bad days. The quality floor rises; the human ceiling gets harder to justify at price.

Biology · Nutrition

Circadian Nutrition Entering the Mainstream

Dan Go's "zeitgeber" framing — breakfast as a time cue that synchronises your entire metabolic clock — reflects a larger shift in nutritional science. Circadian biology is increasingly shaping how researchers think about when we eat, not just what. The implication for performance: meal timing may matter as much as macros, and morning eating is likely under-valued as a metabolic anchor. Watch for this framing to accelerate in fitness culture over 2026.

Philosophy · Psychology

Stoicism–CBT Convergence Deepening

The alignment between Stoic practice and Cognitive Behavioral Therapy is well-documented, but the current Meditations Month moment — Ryan Holiday running a global community reading alongside a structured coaching program — points to something more: the institutionalisation of ancient philosophy as a mental health modality. As CBT faces access bottlenecks in the mental health system, structured Stoic practice is increasingly being positioned as a self-directed alternative with an evidence base.

Productivity · Systems

The "Decision Budget" as a Performance Metric

The implicit logic in Dan Go's breakfast protocol — each repeated decision routine frees cognitive bandwidth for higher-order work — is becoming a more explicit design principle for high performers. The question is moving from "how do I make better decisions?" to "how do I reduce the total number of decisions I need to make each day?" Systematising the bottom 80% of your decision stack to protect capacity for the top 20% may be one of the most underrated leverage points available.

Culture · Wellbeing

Gratitude as Subtraction, Not Addition

The reader stories in Mark Manson's newsletter this week all share a structural feature: gratitude arrived not through affirmations or positive framing, but through reckoning with absence. A physician imagined losing her career. A recovering addict imagined returning to addiction. A caregiver imagined losing the last months with her mother. All three experienced a sudden and overwhelming appreciation for what was already present. This "negative visualisation" technique — borrowed directly from Stoicism — may be more powerful than any additive gratitude practice.
