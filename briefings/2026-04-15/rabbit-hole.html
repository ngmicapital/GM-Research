Why Brilliant People Become More Likeable When They Screw Up

15 April 2026 · Wednesday

In 1966, psychologist Elliot Aronson proved that highly competent people become significantly more attractive to others after making a blunder — while mediocre people become significantly less attractive after the same mistake. The mechanism is counterintuitive: perfection creates distance, and a well-timed stumble closes it. JFK's approval rating rose from 78% to 83% after the Bay of Pigs disaster, and modern influencer research confirms the effect still holds — but only if you already have a reputation worth humanising.

TL;DR

In 1966, social psychologist Elliot Aronson ran an experiment in which a highly competent person spilled a cup of coffee at the end of a recorded quiz — and became significantly more attractive to observers as a result. The same accident made a mediocre person significantly less attractive. The phenomenon, which Aronson called the Pratfall Effect, reveals a deep structural feature of human likability: exceptional competence creates an alienating "superhuman" distance, and a visible blunder collapses it. JFK's approval actually rose after the Bay of Pigs. Jennifer Lawrence tripping at the Oscars became a career-defining moment of relatability. KFC running out of chicken turned into their most celebrated marketing campaign. But the effect is highly conditional — it requires a pre-established reputation, an incidental (not domain-central) error, and an audience that doesn't perceive the vulnerability as performed.

Section 01

The Story: A Coffee Spill That Changed How We Think About Likability

In 1966, Elliot Aronson was a social psychologist at the University of Minnesota with a question that seemed almost too obvious to investigate: do people prefer someone who makes no mistakes, or someone who makes one? The intuitive answer — that everyone prefers the error-free person — turned out to be wrong in a way that reframed how we understand human attraction, trust, and status.

Aronson's experiment was simple. Male students listened to tape-recorded interviews of a fictional quiz contestant. In one version, the contestant was highly competent: he answered 92% of questions correctly, described himself as an honour student, yearbook editor, and track team member. In another version, he was mediocre: 30% correct, average grades, tried out for track but didn't make it. Both versions had a twist: in some recordings, at the very end, the contestant accidentally spilled a cup of coffee on himself and apologised. In the control versions, no spill occurred.

The results were not subtle. The competent person who spilled coffee was rated significantly more attractive than the competent person who did not. The mediocre person who spilled coffee was rated less attractive than the mediocre person who did not. The quantified scores were stark: for the competent contestant, the pratfall added roughly 9.4 attraction points. For the mediocre one, it subtracted 20.3 — a collapse from a score of 17.8 to -2.5.

"If a person seems really terrific to begin with, they may be a little intimidating. When they make a pratfall, it makes people feel closer to them." — Elliot Aronson, reflecting on his 1966 findings in subsequent interviews

Aronson's paper — co-authored with Ben Willerman and Joanne Floyd and published in Psychonomic Science — coined the term "pratfall effect" after the theatrical pratfall: a deliberately exaggerated fall taken by a comic actor. The theoretical framing was direct: exceptional people are perceived as superhuman and therefore alienating. They create an implicit comparison that makes observers feel inadequate. A small blunder does not undermine the competence signal — it punctures the intimidating aura while leaving the underlying ability intact. The stumble, paradoxically, produces closeness.

The experiment had two deeply counterintuitive findings packed into one design. The first is obvious in retrospect: that perfection can be off-putting. The second is subtler and darker: that the same mistake which endears the excellent makes the mediocre repellent. The distance between these two outcomes — determined entirely by the pre-existing competence baseline — would prove to be one of the most replicated and extended findings in social psychology.

In 1970, Helmreich, Aronson, and LeFan extended the finding to observer personality. They found that high self-esteem observers actually preferred the non-blundering competent person — they had no need for the humanising signal and simply preferred unalloyed competence. Low self-esteem observers preferred the competent blunderer strongly: the gap between the star and themselves felt less vast, less threatening. The pratfall effect, it turned out, was also a mirror.

Section 02

The Mechanism: Three Reasons the Stumble Works

Why does a visible mistake make a competent person more attractive? Subsequent decades of research produced three interlocking explanations, each illuminating a different dimension of what is happening when an audience watches a star stumble.

The Humanisation Problem. Aronson's original theory was that highly competent individuals create an implicit social comparison that makes observers feel diminished. Admiration is not the same as warmth. When someone is so clearly superior — at the quiz, at the job, in the room — the natural response includes both positive evaluation and a subtle aversion: being near them is uncomfortable because it reminds you of your own limitations. A blunder doesn't destroy the competence. It closes the distance. You can now admire them and feel connected rather than diminished. The stumble converts a comparison relationship into a fellowship relationship.

Cognitive Dissonance Resolution. We want to admire the star, but we also want to relate to them. These two impulses — upward admiration and horizontal connection — are in tension when someone seems perfect. The blunder provides a resolution mechanism: it allows us to maintain the positive evaluation ("they're still brilliant") while achieving the relational connection ("they're human, like me"). The dissonance collapses in a favourable direction. Without the blunder, the tension remains unresolved and produces a vague discomfort that registers as reduced warmth.

Self-Esteem Threat and the Reversal Mechanism. The flip side — why the mediocre person's blunder backfires — is explained by self-threat dynamics. When a mediocre person blunders, observers who perceive similarity to that person feel their own self-concept implicated. The blunderer becomes a mirror of feared inadequacy. The result is derogation: observers rate the mediocre blunderer sharply lower as a defensive self-protection mechanism. The more the observer resembles the mediocre blunderer, the harsher the response. This is why the same action — one coffee spill — produces entirely opposite outcomes depending on a single variable: how impressive you were before the spill.

"The worse I do, the more popular I get." — John F. Kennedy, after his approval rating rose from 78% to 83% following the Bay of Pigs disaster, April 1961

A fourth consideration emerged from marketing research: the effect is strongest under low-effort cognitive processing. When people are thinking heuristically — making quick, intuitive judgements — the humanisation signal from a blunder registers powerfully. When they are processing information carefully and deliberately, the effect diminishes. This has practical implications: pratfall effects are strongest in emotional, high-stakes, public contexts — exactly the situations where they tend to occur naturally.

The conditions under which the effect works versus fails are precise enough to be useful:

When It Works

  • Person has a strong, pre-established competence reputation
  • Blunder is incidental to core function (a coffee spill, not a surgical error)
  • Mistake is clearly accidental, not manufactured
  • It happens once, not repeatedly
  • Audience is processing heuristically (emotionally engaged)

When It Fails

  • Person is unknown or unproven — mistake just signals incompetence
  • Error touches the exact domain they're trusted for
  • Vulnerability looks staged or strategically deployed
  • Audience is high in self-esteem (they prefer unalloyed excellence)
  • Blunders accumulate into a pattern
Section 03

The Connections: From Oval Offices to Oscar Stages

The pratfall effect is not a laboratory curiosity. Its most striking real-world demonstrations involve some of the highest-stakes moments in modern political and cultural history — and what they share is the counterintuitive pattern: a blunder by an already-respected person that somehow left them more trusted rather than less.

JFK and the Bay of Pigs, 1961. On 17 April 1961, a CIA-organised invasion of Cuba by 1,400 Cuban exiles collapsed catastrophically on the beaches of the Bay of Pigs. The operation was conceived under Eisenhower, approved by Kennedy, and executed disastrously. It was a humiliation of the first order: the invaders were captured, the new administration looked incompetent, and Khrushchev read it as a sign of weakness. Kennedy's response was the textbook pratfall move, possibly instinctive rather than calculated: he held a press conference and accepted total, unequivocal personal responsibility. "I'm the responsible officer of the Government," he stated. No scapegoats were identified. No agencies were blamed. The gallup poll taken immediately after recorded his approval at 83% — up from 78% before the invasion. Kennedy remarked privately: "The worse I do, the more popular I get." His aide Richard Goodwin noted the admission "was received with relief, thought courageous, began the restoration of confidence."

Jennifer Lawrence at the 2013 Oscars. Lawrence had already won critical acclaim and a devoted audience through The Hunger Games and Silver Linings Playbook. When she tripped on her gown ascending the stairs to accept the Best Actress Oscar, the stumble became an immediate cultural event — not a source of mockery but of affection. In the weeks and months that followed, her relatability ratings surged. The trip did not undermine her perceived competence as an actress. It closed the alienating gap between a 22-year-old Academy Award winner and an ordinary human being. The fall was, in pratfall effect terms, structurally identical to Aronson's coffee spill: the pre-existing competence signal was strong enough that the stumble only added warmth.

Tim Cook and "We Screwed Up," 2012. When Apple Maps launched in 2012 with severe inaccuracies — routing people into lakes, misidentifying landmarks, omitting transit data — Tim Cook published an open letter that began with one sentence: "We screwed up." The statement was striking precisely because Apple's brand had been built on the mythology of flawless product execution. Cook's acknowledgement of failure, far from triggering a crisis of confidence, was widely perceived as honest and trust-building. It worked because Apple's underlying competence reputation was unimpeachable — a blunder, openly admitted, didn't destroy the signal; it humanised it.

KFC's "FCK" Campaign, 2018. When KFC UK ran out of chicken — an absurdity that threatened both operations and reputation — their creative response was a full-page newspaper ad rearranging the letters of their logo to read "FCK," beneath which they apologised with deadpan British understatement. A survey of UK marketers voted it the best campaign of the year. The pratfall effect explains why: KFC's underlying product reputation was strong enough that an honest, self-deprecating admission of spectacular failure converted a crisis into brand affinity. The "FCK" ad was not a management of the failure so much as a celebration of it.

What connects these cases is not the blunder itself but the pre-existing competence that gave it meaning. The stumble only works as humanisation when there is something superhuman to humanise. Kennedy without his Harvard education and wartime heroism is just a president who failed. Lawrence without her Oscar is just a woman who fell. The coffee spill requires the 92% quiz score.

Further Down the Hole

🤖

The Pratfall Effect in Robots

In 2017, researchers at Frontiers in Robotics and AI ran the pratfall experiment with a NAO humanoid robot. Participants interacted with a robot that either performed flawlessly or made minor errors and apologised. The faulty robot was rated significantly more likeable — and crucially, was not rated as less intelligent or less anthropomorphic. The finding has a sharp implication for AI design: deliberate minor imperfections may be a feature, not a bug. A robot that is too perfect triggers the same "superhuman distance" problem as an exceptionally capable human. Subsequent research extended this to AI chatbots that "forget" and acknowledge it, finding higher user trust ratings than for bots that never erred.

📱

Influencer "Flop Videos" and Purchase Intent

A 2025 peer-reviewed study examined a growing influencer trend: posting deliberate "fail videos" — cooking disasters, workout failures, technology blunders. For macro-influencers (800K+ followers), failure disclosure boosted purchase intent for recommended products through perceived authenticity. Crucially, the effect was negligible for micro-influencers with smaller followings. The finding replicates the core pratfall logic with precision: you need a pre-established competence reputation for the blunder to convert into warmth rather than damage. For small accounts without that foundation, failure disclosure simply reads as failure.

🧠

Impostor Syndrome's Deadly Inversion

The pratfall effect offers a counterintuitive intervention for impostor syndrome. The syndrome's core drive is to hide mistakes and project flawless competence — because exposing error will trigger "exposure" of fraudulence. But the pratfall research suggests that for genuinely competent people, visible mistakes reinforce rather than undermine standing. Impostor syndrome is, in this framing, exactly backwards: it leads high-performers to suppress the very signal that would most humanise them and increase their social capital. The fear of being found out as flawed produces the one behaviour most likely to maintain the alienating distance that makes them feel isolated in the first place.

📖

Brené Brown and the Vulnerability Thesis

Brené Brown's grounded-theory research (2006 onward) arrived independently at vulnerability as the core mechanism of authentic human connection — making her framework essentially the cultural deployment of pratfall effect logic at scale. Brown's claim: perceived perfection creates relational distance, and strategic disclosure of weakness creates genuine bonds. The overlap with Aronson's 1966 findings is precise. Critics note Brown's samples skew middle-class and her qualitative method blurs the line between narrative illustration and systematic evidence. But her cultural reach made the pratfall effect's core mechanism a mainstream idea — even if most readers encountered it without knowing Aronson's name.

💡

The Ben Franklin Effect: Related But Distinct

The Ben Franklin Effect is the pratfall effect's sister phenomenon. Franklin discovered — and modern research confirmed — that people like you more after doing you a favour, not less. The mechanism: helping someone produces a cognitive dissonance that resolves in their favour ("I helped them, so I must like them"). Both phenomena demonstrate that human likability judgements are far more cognitively constructed than folk wisdom suggests — our feelings about other people are shaped not just by what they do, but by what we do in relation to them, and by the social comparisons their attributes trigger in us.
