Alpha — VPIN & Flow Toxicity

Sat · June 6, 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #43 11 min read
TL;DR

VPIN is a volume-bucketed gauge of order-flow toxicity — it scores how lopsided buying is versus selling, which proxies the probability that informed traders are running over the people providing liquidity. When VPIN climbs toward 0.7 and holds, market makers widen spreads and pull depth, and that liquidity withdrawal is what turns an ordinary sell-off into an air-pocket. Trade it as a regime filter, never a timing trigger: high toxicity says fade setups are dangerous and stops need room — it tells you the water is choppy, not the exact second the wave breaks.

§01
What It Is
Definition & first principles
VPIN scores how one-sided the flow is over equal-volume chunks of tape — a high reading means one side is steamrolling the other, the statistical fingerprint of informed money.

Toxicity is not a moral judgment — it's a market-maker's word for getting run over. Every time a passive limit order fills, the counterparty either knew something or didn't. VPIN — Volume-Synchronized Probability of Informed Trading — is Easley, López de Prado and O'Hara's attempt to put a real-time number on that adverse selection, without modelling a single hidden variable or fitting a likelihood.

The insight is mechanical, not magical. When buys and sells arrive in roughly equal size, liquidity providers run a balanced book and earn the spread. When flow goes lopsided — far more aggressive selling than buying, or the reverse — those providers are systematically on the wrong side, soaking up inventory into a move that keeps going. VPIN measures that imbalance and reads it as the probability the flow is informed (toxic) rather than two-sided noise.

Crucially, VPIN runs on a volume clock, not a wall clock. It chops the tape into buckets that each hold the same traded quantity, so a frantic minute and a dead minute get equal weight. That is the whole trick: information arrives in volume-time, so you sample in volume-time.

§02
Mechanics
Buckets · imbalance · the rolling average
Bucket the tape by equal volume, split each bucket into buy versus sell, then average the absolute imbalance over the last ~50 buckets — that rolling number is VPIN.

Step 1 — bucket by volume. Pick a bucket size. The original work used roughly 1/50th of average daily volume; the Mt. Gox bitcoin study used 500 BTC, about a fifteenth of daily turnover. Stream trades in and close a bucket every time cumulative volume crosses the threshold. Each bucket is a unit of activity, not time.

Step 2 — classify buy versus sell. Two ways. Use the actual aggressor side per trade (in crypto this is ground truth — on Binance the taker side is tagged), or use Bulk Volume Classification (BVC), which estimates the buy fraction of a bucket from its price change run through a normal or Student-t CDF. ELO chose BVC because it survives at high frequency where you cannot cleanly tag every print — and that choice is exactly where the controversy lives (see §07).

Per bucket τ:  imbalanceτ = | Vbuy − Vsell | / Vbucket
Rolling:      VPINt = (1 / N) · Σ imbalanceτ   over the last N buckets
N = 50 buckets is standard. Vbucket is constant by construction, so VPIN is simply the average absolute buy/sell imbalance. Range: 0 (perfectly balanced) → 1 (totally one-sided).

Step 3 — the BVC split (if you go that route). Rather than counting aggressor flags, BVC infers them from how far price moved inside the bucket:

Vbuy = Vbucket · Z( ΔP / σΔP ) ,  Vsell = Vbucket − Vbuy
Z = CDF of a Student-t (or normal) · ΔP = bucket price change · σΔP = stdev of price changes
A bigger price move per bucket implies a larger estimated buy (or sell) share. Note what this does: it bakes volatility into VPIN by construction — the critics' central objection.

Reading the number: ~0 is balanced two-sided flow; ~0.5 is normal for a trend; above 0.7 is the danger zone — heavily one-sided, the regime where liquidity providers start retreating.

§04
In Practice
Regime filter, not a trigger
Trade VPIN as a regime filter — high toxicity says "don't fade, give stops room," never "short here, now."
1

Filter, don't trigger

Elevated VPIN is context, not a signal. It says informed flow dominates, so mean-reversion and fade setups have a poor edge right now. Stand the contrarian playbook down until imbalance normalizes.

2

Pair it with price — it's sign-blind

VPIN spikes identically on toxic buying and toxic selling. Overlay it on CVD or plain price direction to know which side is being run over before you act on the reading.

3

Watch the cross, then the retreat

The setup that matters is VPIN pushing above 0.7 and staying there while book depth thins. Toxicity up plus depth down precedes air-pockets far more reliably than either alone.

4

It edits size and stops, not entries

In a high-toxicity regime, widen stops and cut size — liquidity is fragile and slippage compounds. The edge VPIN gives you is risk management, not a fresh reason to click.

Worked read — computing VPIN from five buckets (illustrative)
Bucket 152% buy / 48% sell → |0.52 − 0.48| = 0.04
Bucket 261 / 39 → 0.22
Bucket 374 / 26 → 0.48
Bucket 483 / 17 → 0.66
Bucket 588 / 12 → 0.76
VPIN (N=5)(0.04 + 0.22 + 0.48 + 0.66 + 0.76) / 5 = ≈ 0.43, rising fast

Note the lag the rolling average builds in: bucket 5 is already screaming 0.76, yet the 5-bucket VPIN reads only 0.43 because the early buckets were calm. Shorten N for responsiveness and noise; lengthen it for a smoother but slower signal. The number you watch is always a trailing average, never the live tape.

Timeframe fit: bucket size sets the clock — sub-hour buckets for intraday execution, multi-hour buckets for a swing-grade regime read. Bias from 4h+, manage risk on the read.

§07
Common Traps
Where VPIN misleads
The biggest mistake is trading VPIN as a timing signal — it's a regime gauge that lags by construction, not a turn-caller.
1
Treating it as a short trigger

High VPIN tells you flow is toxic, not that the top is in. Shorting the instant VPIN crosses 0.7 gets you steamrolled, because the toxicity often is the trend continuing. Use it to manage risk, not to pick the turn.

2
Ignoring sign-blindness

VPIN reads identically on a melt-up and a melt-down. Without a direction overlay you'll mistake aggressive buying for danger and aggressive selling for the same thing, with no idea which way to lean.

3
Forgetting the classification dependency

Swap the buy/sell classifier — true aggressor tags versus Bulk Volume Classification — and VPIN can flip behaviour entirely. The number you trust is only as good as how you split buys from sells.

4
Over-tuning bucket size and N

Small buckets and short windows make VPIN twitchy and prone to false alarms; large ones make it lag the move it's meant to warn about. There's no universal setting — it's a fitted parameter, so treat it like one.

5
Overhyped: the "two-hour flash-crash warning"

This is contested, and Tony-frank: Andersen & Bondarenko showed VPIN is largely mechanically driven by volume and volatility, and that under standard trade classification it spiked after the 2010 crash, not before — with no incremental predictive power once you control for those. VPIN is a real, useful description of adverse-selection conditions. Sold as a crystal-ball crash predictor it's overhyped; used as a regime filter it earns its keep.
