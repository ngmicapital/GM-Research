Headline CFTC Release 9233-26 PRIMARY FEDERAL LITIGATION
🟢 High — federal complaint filed; preliminary-injunction motion docketed against statute taking effect Aug 1

CFTC v. State of Minnesota — Commission Files Federal Preemption Suit 19 May Against SF 4760 Prediction-Markets Ban Signed By Governor Walz 18 May; Statute Criminalises Operating Or Facilitating CFTC-Designated Contract Market Activity As Of 1 August, Triggering First Direct Federal-State Court Confrontation Over Event Contracts

"This Minnesota law turns lawful operators and participants in prediction markets into felons overnight. Minnesota farmers have relied on critical hedging products on weather and crop-related events for decades to mitigate their risks. Governor Walz chose to put special interests first and American farmers and innovators last." — Michael S. Selig, Chairman, U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC Press Release 9233-26, 19 May 2026)

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission filed suit in federal district court on Monday 19 May seeking a preliminary injunction blocking enforcement of Minnesota Senate File 4760, an omnibus public-safety bill that Governor Tim Walz signed Sunday and that, on its 1 August effective date, will impose criminal-felony liability on any party operating, brokering or facilitating a "prediction market" inside Minnesota — language that, on its face, captures every CFTC-designated contract market and its participants.

Press Release 9233-26 frames the action squarely as a Commodity Exchange Act exclusive-jurisdiction case. The complaint argues that Section 5h of the CEA and the Commission's existing event-contract framework establish a uniform federal regime for trading on Designated Contract Markets, and that Minnesota's criminalisation regime is therefore field-preempted under Geier v. American Honda and Crosby v. NFTC. This is the fifth state-government action the CFTC has formally challenged in court, after Arizona (where Judge Michael Liburdi converted a TRO into a permanent injunction on 5 May), Connecticut, Illinois and New York, but it is the first against a statute that goes beyond cease-and-desist orders to impose felony exposure on participants.

The political optics are sharp. Walz, the 2024 Democratic vice-presidential nominee, signed the bill as part of an omnibus package containing unrelated public-safety measures, which means the prediction-markets ban will not be severable from the rest of the bill in a typical line-item way — the Eighth Circuit will, if the case reaches it, have to weigh full-statute injunction relief. Kalshi, Polymarket and CFTC-cleared event-contract intermediaries are not formally party to the suit but file alongside the CFTC under the agency's amicus posture taken in KalshiEx v. Schuler (Sixth Circuit, briefed 12 May).

📎 Historical precedent: Closest analog is National Bank of Commerce v. Dow Chemical (8th Cir. 1999), holding that the CEA preempts state-law claims against derivatives contracts traded on CFTC-regulated markets; the Liburdi 5 May permanent injunction in Arizona is the most recent and most expansive application.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The shift from amicus filings (Mass. SJC, 6th Cir.) to a direct CFTC-as-plaintiff complaint is the most consequential procedural escalation in the prediction-market wars to date. Per the Sidley Austin client alert of 19 May, this changes the burden allocation: as plaintiff seeking a preliminary injunction, the CFTC must demonstrate likelihood of success on the merits, but it also gains control of the venue and timing — and Minnesota cannot run out the clock to the 1 August trigger date without a court ruling on the preemption question. Davis Polk's Klein/Glatzer memo (20 May) reads the Selig statement as a deliberate dare to other state AGs considering similar legislation, intended to deter copycats in Vermont, New Mexico and Maryland where similar bills are at committee stage. The Brief's read: the strongest legal vulnerability is Minnesota's argument that wagering on the outcome of a Minnesota gubernatorial election is not a "commodity" within Section 1a(9) of the CEA — a question the Liburdi opinion sidestepped by focusing on weather contracts. Expect Walz to seek expedited Eighth Circuit review.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Per Adam Levitin (Georgetown Law) on Credit Slips, 20 May: the CFTC's preemption theory is broader than the case law actually supports. The Supreme Court's Cantero v. Bank of America framework (2024) recently raised the bar for federal preemption of state consumer-protection laws, and the Roberts Court has shown limited appetite for sweeping field-preemption arguments. Minnesota's response will lean on Cantero to argue that state gambling laws are an exercise of traditional police power and require a clearer congressional intent to displace. If Minnesota wins below or in the Eighth Circuit, the case becomes the cleanest vehicle for the Supreme Court to draw a federal-state line on event contracts — a path Chairman Selig himself floated on 6 May.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Per The Block Research 20 May market note and Jefferies' "Prediction Markets — State Conflict Tracker" (May 2026): Kalshi (privately held, last valued at $2B by D1 Capital) and Polymarket (UMA-affiliated, ~$1.2B last raise) gain near-term protection from felony exposure but lose the ability to onboard Minnesota retail users until injunction issues — adding to the ~12% of US population already in litigation zones. Listed derivatives venues — CME (CME.US), Cboe (CBOE.US), Intercontinental Exchange (ICE.US) — benefit indirectly: a CFTC win expands the addressable market for event-contract products they could list. DraftKings (DKNG.US) and FanDuel parent Flutter (FLTR.L) face the opposite directional bet: if event contracts win federal protection, state-licensed sportsbooks lose the regulatory moat they paid Minnesota lobbyists to defend.

High SEC Comment File S7-2026-09 PRIMARY RULEMAKING

Consensys 11 May Comment Letter Asks SEC For Commission-Level Safe Harbor Confirming Self-Custodial Wallet Interfaces Like MetaMask Do Not Require Broker-Dealer Registration — Five-Condition Test (Noncustodial, No Order Matching, User-Signed Transactions, No Investment-Contract Representations, Clear Disclosure) Targets March Interpretive Release On Crypto Asset Securities

"The Commission's March interpretive release leaves intermediary status for noncustodial wallet software ambiguous. Self-custodial interfaces should not be required to register as broker-dealers simply because their software lets users discover, view, or initiate transactions in non-security crypto assets." — Consensys comment letter to SEC, File No. S7-2026-09 (11 May 2026, filed via SEC EDGAR)

Consensys, the Joe Lubin-controlled Ethereum infrastructure company behind the MetaMask wallet, filed a formal comment letter with the Securities and Exchange Commission on Monday 11 May 2026 in response to the SEC's March 2026 interpretive release on the application of federal securities laws to crypto assets (Release 33-11412). The letter — Consensys's first major regulatory submission since the agency dismissed its enforcement case in February 2025 — asks Chair Paul Atkins's Commission to formally adopt a Commission-level safe harbor for self-custodial cryptoasset interfaces.

The proposed safe harbor turns on five conditions, all of which Consensys argues MetaMask currently satisfies: (1) the interface remains noncustodial — the provider never takes custody of user assets and is not a counterparty to any trade; (2) the interface does not match orders or perform discretionary routing; (3) the user initiates and signs each transaction with their own private keys; (4) the provider makes no representation that the underlying tokens are or are not investment contracts; and (5) the provider clearly discloses that it is not registered as a broker-dealer and that users bear smart-contract and counterparty risk directly.

This is the first major industry response to the March interpretive release that goes beyond requesting clarification and instead proposes specific exemptive language. It also lands ahead of the SEC's broader broker-dealer status rulemaking signalled in Chair Atkins's 8 May SCSP AI+ Expo remarks identifying "crypto vaults" as the next regulatory frontier and committing the agency to formal rulemaking on onchain trading and decentralised exchange interfaces.

📎 Historical precedent: The 13 April 2026 SEC Trading & Markets staff statement under which the Staff would not object to a "Covered User Interface Provider" operating without broker-dealer registration provides the soft-law template Consensys is now asking the Commission to codify by formal rule.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per WilmerHale's "MetaMask Safe-Harbor Proposal" alert (20 May): filing a comment letter proposing specific safe-harbor text is significantly more aggressive than typical industry feedback. It functions as draft regulatory language that Commissioners Peirce and Atkins can pick up wholesale, and it forces opposing commenters to argue against concrete proposals rather than abstract principles. The five-condition test is also a deliberate signal to DeFi projects (Uniswap Labs, Aave Labs, Curve) that the Consensys "frontend operator vs. protocol developer" distinction is the framework Consensys is willing to defend in court if needed. The Brief's read: the Commission is highly likely to adopt the substantive logic of the proposal — but probably via no-action relief or a 2026 final rule rather than codified safe harbor — because Peirce has publicly endorsed the noncustodial/order-matching distinction since her 2018 "Safe Harbor 2.0" paper, and Atkins has every political reason to deliver a fast win to the industry ahead of the broker-dealer rulemaking.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Per Better Markets ("Five Conditions, Zero Substance") rapid response 19 May: the five conditions are designed to be effectively self-certifying, particularly the "user-signed transaction" prong, which any non-custodial frontend satisfies by definition. The deeper question is whether interfaces that intermediate access to clearly fraudulent token launches or wash-traded markets should benefit from the same status as a passive wallet. Better Markets argues the Commission must add anti-fraud and surveillance conditions or risk turning the safe harbor into a blanket immunity for retail-facing crypto frontends. The Consensys proposal contains no equivalent of FINRA Rule 4530 reporting obligations or suspicious-activity monitoring.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Per Galaxy Digital Research's 21 May "Wallet Layer Re-Rating" note: Consensys's IPO — now slipped to autumn 2026 per Lubin's 13 May CoinDesk interview — becomes materially more underwritable if the safe harbor lands before the S-1 filing window opens. Ledger (private, Paris-based) and Trust Wallet (Binance subsidiary) benefit directly. Listed wallet-adjacent names: Coinbase (COIN.US) gains an indirect tailwind because Coinbase Wallet runs under the same architecture, but loses some moat differentiation as broker-dealer registration becomes a less meaningful gating factor. Galaxy (GLXY.TO) and Robinhood (HOOD.US) are exposed via crypto custody arms but not the wallet layer specifically.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish COIN.US (wallet tailwind), GLXY.TO, pre-IPO Consensys — formal safe harbor reduces regulatory drag on the wallet/DeFi UI stack.

🔴 Bearish Traditional broker-dealer franchises with crypto trading arms (SCHW.US, IBKR.US) — narrower differentiation if noncustodial UIs operate outside Reg ATS.

⚪ Neutral ETH price level — second-order positive but already priced; near-term flow driver is more likely the spot ETP staking decision than wallet policy.

High ESMA Statement (April 2026) + April 17 Update PRIMARY SUPERVISORY DEADLINE
🟢 High — fixed regulatory deadline; ESMA convergence statement published; NCA enforcement expected immediately

MiCA Pan-EU Transitional Period Ends 1 July (T-41 Days) — ESMA Confirms National Competent Authorities Must Verify Wind-Down Plans And Withdraw Authorisation From Unlicensed CASPs; Last-Minute Authorisation Applications Subject To "Heightened Scrutiny"; Reverse-Solicitation Carve-Out Treated As Narrow Exception, Not Strategy

"Last-minute authorisation applications should be subject to heightened regulatory scrutiny. NCAs are expected to verify the existence and adequacy of orderly wind-down plans for unauthorised crypto-asset service providers, and ensure these are implemented in a timely manner without causing undue economic harm to clients." — ESMA Statement on the end of transitional periods under MiCA (Ref. ESMA75-113276571-1679, 17 April 2026)

The European Securities and Markets Authority's April 2026 supervisory-convergence statement places the rest of the EU crypto industry on a 41-day glide path to mandatory MiCA authorisation. After 1 July 2026, any entity providing crypto-asset services to clients established in the EU without a MiCA licence will be in breach of EU law and must cease all such activity. ESMA's text is sharper than typical convergence guidance: national competent authorities (NCAs) are expected to verify wind-down plans, take supervisory action against unauthorised providers, and scrutinise migration strategies that would let firms continue serving EU clients via group restructuring or outsourcing.

Three jurisdictions matter for blast radius. France (AMF) has consolidated approximately 14 PSAN (former Provider) registrations and is processing roughly 25 MiCA conversion applications. Germany (BaFin) has the largest backlog at ~40 in-progress applications, dominated by retail brokers (Bitpanda, Coinpass) and stablecoin issuers. Italy (CONSOB), where the transitional grandfathering period was the most generous, has approximately 10 applicants in queue, with at least three institutions — Conio, Young Platform, Checksig — disclosing public concern they will not receive authorisation before the 1 July cutoff.

The reverse-solicitation carve-out remains the only narrow escape valve for unauthorised non-EU entities, but ESMA's statement specifically warns NCAs to treat outsourcing and delegation arrangements that would effectively let unauthorised non-EU entities continue serving EU clients indirectly as evasion. Practitioner advisories from White & Case, Linklaters and Norton Rose Fulbright have aligned on the position that reverse-solicitation alone is not a viable long-term operating model.

📎 Historical precedent: Pattern resembles the MiFID II "passport withdrawal" sequence of January 2018, where firms that missed the transposition deadline faced immediate cease-of-business obligations and no grace period. ESMA explicitly referenced that 2018 enforcement pattern in its 17 April text.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Linklaters MiCA tracker (20 May 2026 update): the supervisory-convergence framework gives NCAs substantial discretion to handle applicants on a case-by-case basis, but the language "heightened scrutiny" of late filings effectively shifts the burden onto applicants to prove they began the authorisation process in good time and were delayed by NCA-side capacity constraints. White & Case's 19 May client alert reads ESMA's statement as a green light for Italian, German and French NCAs to publicly name unauthorised firms after 1 July — the EU-style equivalent of an SEC warning-list approach. The Brief's read: the biggest enforcement risk is not penalties against firms but liability transfer to EU-based custodians and payment partners. Banks and TradFi custodians servicing unauthorised crypto firms will face indirect supervisory action under MiCA Article 81 and existing CSDR custody rules, creating a strong commercial incentive to terminate EU servicing relationships within the 41-day window.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Per Aurelien Menant (Gatecoin founder, ex-CASP applicant) on Bloomberg Crypto, 19 May: ESMA's enforcement rhetoric exceeds what NCAs can actually deliver operationally. France's AMF and Germany's BaFin do not have the supervisory headcount to process the backlog plus enforce against unauthorised firms simultaneously. Practical outcome will be implicit forbearance for the largest applicants (Bitpanda, Coinpass) and faster termination only for small firms that lack TradFi banking relationships. The "1 July cliff" is more likely to be a "1 October de-facto deadline" once enforcement capacity is honestly assessed.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Per The Block Research "MiCA Authorisation Tracker" May update: Bitpanda (German-Austrian, ~$4.1B last valuation), 21Shares (Swiss ETP issuer with German MiCA application), and Kraken's European subsidiary are best-positioned to clear the deadline with margin. Binance Europe (already licensed in France) and OKX Europe (Malta MiCA) operate without overhang. Tether (USDT) remains the structural loser — without an EU-licensed e-money issuer for USDT, exchanges including Binance EU and Kraken have delisted USDT for EU clients, redirecting flow to Circle's USDC and to euro-denominated MiCA-compliant stablecoins (Circle EURC, Société Générale-FORGE EURCV). Capital-flows winner is Circle (CRCL.US): every percentage point of EU stablecoin share migrating from Tether to USDC is roughly $1.5B in net new circulation.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish CRCL.US (USDC EU share gain), 21Shares parent CoinShares (CS.PA), and EU MiCA-licensed banks (SocGen GLE.PA via FORGE, ING ING.AS via Quantoz).

🔴 Bearish Tether (private but indirect via Bitfinex parent iFinex) — locked out of EU retail spot pairs; smaller non-EU CASPs without licensing pathway forced to exit by 1 July.

⚪ Neutral COIN.US — EU revenue contribution still under 10% of total; primary listings already migrated via Coinbase Europe (Ireland).

High EU Council Presidency Press Release PRIMARY LEGISLATION
🟢 High — political agreement reached; formal adoption needed but on glide path

EU AI Act Omnibus Political Agreement Reached 7 May Postpones Annex III High-Risk AI Systems By 16 Months To 2 December 2027, Annex I Regulated-Product Embedded Systems By 12 Months To 2 August 2028, And National Regulatory Sandboxes To 2 August 2027 — Article 50 Watermarking Deferred From 2 August To 2 December 2026; GPAI Articles 51–55 Unchanged And Continue To Apply From August 2025

"Today, the Council presidency and European Parliament negotiators reached a provisional agreement on a proposal to streamline certain rules regarding artificial intelligence. Given that provisions on high-risk AI systems are due to enter into force on 2 August 2026, the co-legislators have treated the proposal with utmost priority." — Council of the EU Press Release, "Artificial Intelligence: Council and Parliament agree to simplify and streamline rules" (7 May 2026)

The Belgian Council presidency and European Parliament rapporteurs concluded a provisional political agreement on the AI Act Omnibus on Wednesday 7 May 2026, providing the first substantive certainty about the most consequential AI deadline of the year. The agreement is the centrepiece of the "Omnibus VII" simplification package and changes four operational dates within the AI Act framework.

Annex III high-risk AI systems — the broadest category covering recruitment, credit scoring, biometric identification, education, critical infrastructure and migration — move from a 2 August 2026 application date to 2 December 2027, a 16-month deferral. Annex I covered systems (high-risk AI embedded in regulated products like medical devices and vehicles) move from 2 August 2027 to 2 August 2028. Article 50 transparency obligations covering watermarking and synthetic-content disclosure shift from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2026, with the four-month grace recognising the technical readiness gap on AI-generated content detection. National regulatory sandboxes — the principal opportunity for AI startups to test high-risk systems with member-state regulator engagement — move from 2 August 2026 to 2 August 2027.

Crucially, GPAI obligations under Articles 51–55 are not touched. The transparency, copyright and risk-management duties already binding general-purpose AI providers since August 2025 — covering OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Mistral, xAI and the larger open-source providers — continue without modification. The May 7 agreement also tightens the AI-generated intimate-imagery framework and clarifies the threshold for "AI-generated content" labelling.

📎 Historical precedent: Closest analog is the 2022 Digital Services Act compliance grace period, where similarly broad deferrals were granted only after the Council confirmed standards-development gaps; precedent is favourable to applicants seeking further individual extensions on narrow technical grounds.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per White & Case 8 May client alert: the most consequential element is not the date shift itself but the principle that the EU is willing to revisit AI Act deadlines once. That makes future targeted deferrals — for biometric identification or for sectoral high-risk systems where harmonised standards lag — significantly more politically achievable. Taylor Wessing's 12 May analysis flags the open question of whether existing Annex III in-scope products that providers had been racing to launch before 2 August 2026 can now be deferred without contractual breach to enterprise customers under existing AI-clause commitments. The Brief's read: the Article 50 watermarking deferral is the under-reported headline. The four-month slip to 2 December 2026 lands precisely when generative-AI providers are pushing the next major model releases (OpenAI's GPT-5.5, Anthropic Opus 5, Google Gemini 3.5), removing a binding technical-architecture constraint from the most consequential model launches of the year.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Per Mozilla Foundation Public Policy 9 May statement: the omnibus deferral effectively suspends consumer-protection benefits that the AI Act was specifically designed to deliver on the original 2026 schedule, including transparency for AI-driven hiring decisions and disclosure obligations in education. The framing of the deferral as "simplification" obscures the substantive deregulatory effect. Future of Life Institute argued in a 10 May press release that the Commission's willingness to push deadlines under industry pressure will weaken the Act's credibility against future lobbying campaigns and creates a precedent for AI Act deferral becoming an annual exercise.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Per JPMorgan European Tech Research "EU AI Act Re-Rating" 9 May note: the principal beneficiaries are the European AI infrastructure stack and the recruiting-tech / credit-scoring providers most exposed to Annex III, including SAP (SAP.DE) — whose SuccessFactors recruiting product was on accelerated compliance timeline — and Adyen (ADYEN.AS) for AML/fraud screening systems. US providers serving EU enterprise customers (Workday WDAY.US, Salesforce CRM.US, Palantir PLTR.US) gain 16 additional months to operationalise AI Act compliance. Mistral, Aleph Alpha and the open-source GPAI providers are unchanged because the May 7 agreement explicitly did not touch GPAI obligations.

🟢 Bullish SAP.DE, ADYEN.AS, WDAY.US, CRM.US — direct compliance-cost relief on Annex III systems; reduced near-term Capex on AI Act audit functions.

🔴 Bearish European AI compliance/audit boutiques (CompliFor, Holistic AI, Credo AI) and Big-4 AI audit practices — addressable market deferred by 12–16 months.

⚪ Neutral US foundation-model providers (NVDA.US, OpenAI private, Anthropic private) — GPAI Articles 51–55 unchanged, no incremental cost or benefit.

Medium ASIC + AUSTRAC PRIMARY COMPLIANCE DEADLINE
🟡 Medium — fixed compliance dates with ASIC forbearance window expiring 30 June

Australia ASIC Digital-Asset Implementation Timeline — Compliance Officer Notification To AUSTRAC Due 30 May (T-9 Days); Crypto Travel Rule Effective 1 July; ASIC No-Enforcement Forbearance For Good-Faith Applicants Expires 30 June Under Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Bill 2025 Passed 1 April

"ASIC will not take enforcement action against unlicensed providers making genuine efforts to comply with new requirements through June 30, 2026. We will consider this transition period when reviewing past conduct but will continue acting against serious misconduct causing significant consumer harm." — ASIC Commissioner Joe Longo, statement accompanying Updated Information Sheet INFO 225 (25-250MR, 19 November 2025)

The Australian digital-asset framework hits its first hard compliance deadline within 10 days. Under the AUSTRAC AML/CTF amendments operationalising the Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Bill 2025 — which received Royal Assent on 1 April 2026 — every digital-asset service provider operating in Australia must notify AUSTRAC of its designated compliance officer by 30 May 2026 (T-9 days). The Travel Rule for crypto-asset transfers — recipient-identifying data attached to every transaction above the AU$1,000 threshold — becomes binding from 1 July 2026, aligning Australia with the FATF Recommendation 16 update of June 2025.

Two parallel ASIC actions matter to operators. ASIC's transitional no-enforcement posture for AFSL applicants making good-faith progress expires on 30 June 2026 — five weeks after the AUSTRAC compliance-officer deadline. Firms not registered or in active processing by 30 June will lose the forbearance protection and become enforcement targets under the new AFSL perimeter for digital-asset platforms and tokenised custody platforms. Separately, ASIC's in-principle decision to grant proposed regulatory relief for distributors of stablecoins and wrapped tokens (and certain relief for custodians of digital assets that are financial products) under the Corporations Act remains in draft, with the final relief instrument expected in June 2026.

The political backdrop is the Treasury's parallel work on a payment-service-provider framework that incorporates stablecoins as a regulated payment instrument, which closed its first consultation round on 24 October 2025 and produced the Bill that passed 1 April. The combination effectively gives Australia a unified digital-asset regulatory perimeter — AFSL for trading platforms, payment-service-provider licensing for stablecoin payments, and AUSTRAC AML rules layered across both — three months ahead of the EU MiCA cliff and roughly six months ahead of expected US final stablecoin rules under the GENIUS Act.

📎 Historical precedent: Follows AUSTRAC's 2018 Phase 1 digital-currency-exchange registration regime structure, where AUSTRAC enforcement followed roughly 60 days after the initial registration deadline.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Gilbert + Tobin 20 May client alert: the structural risk is the 30-day overlap between AUSTRAC compliance-officer notification (30 May) and the ASIC enforcement forbearance expiry (30 June). Operators who fail the AUSTRAC test cannot rely on the ASIC forbearance because the two regimes are independent and one cannot cure the other. Piper Alderman's "INFO 225 implementation" tracker (19 May) flags that small-to-mid AFSL applicants — particularly Australian crypto exchanges Independent Reserve, BTC Markets, Swyftx and CoinSpot — are still finalising compliance-officer appointments and may risk a forced operational pause if AUSTRAC notification slips. The Brief's read: ASIC has signalled it will use the 30 June expiry as the natural breakpoint for its first wave of formal AFSL determinations — expect a public list of granted, denied and provisional licenses to be released within two weeks of that date as a deliberate market-discipline mechanism.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Per King & Wood Mallesons "Digital Asset Framework Implementation" briefing 18 May: Australian-licensed exchanges that have already completed AFSL upgrades (CoinJar, Independent Reserve) gain a meaningful first-mover advantage over non-Australian competitors that must establish a local entity. The losing population is the offshore-direct-to-AU model — Binance, OKX, Bybit serving Australian retail clients without a local AFSL — which loses the existing "transitional relief for foreign financial services providers" by 31 March 2026 and must now choose between authorisation, local subsidiary, or AU exit. Listed Australian financial names with crypto custody/payment optionality (Macquarie MQG.AX, ASX Group ASX.AX via Synfini) gain incremental optionality on tokenised collateral and settlement.

CP26/13 is the perimeter map for the UK crypto regime — the foundation on which authorisation, supervision and enforcement decisions will be made from 25 October 2027." — Sarah Pritchard, Executive Director, Markets and International (FCA opening remarks, "A new regime for cryptoasset regulation" webinar, 28 April 2026)

The Financial Conduct Authority's CP26/13, published 28 April 2026, closes for written submissions on 3 June 2026. The paper defines the authorisation boundaries for the six new UK-regulated activities created by the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Cryptoassets) Regulations 2026, which received parliamentary approval on 4 February 2026 and come into full force on 25 October 2027. The six new activities are: (i) issuing UK qualifying stablecoins (UKQS); (ii) safeguarding (or arranging safeguarding of) qualifying cryptoassets and relevant specified-investment cryptoassets; (iii) operating a qualifying cryptoasset trading platform; (iv) dealing in qualifying cryptoassets as principal; (v) dealing as agent; and (vi) arranging deals — explicitly including arranging qualifying-cryptoasset staking.

The most contested perimeter question is DeFi and Web3 user-interface treatment. The Skadden 12 May client alert highlighted that the FCA proposes to treat noncustodial UIs that present trading functionality and that earn a fee from order routing as in-scope under "arranging deals" — a narrower formulation than the SEC's March interpretive release but broader than the Consensys position taken in its parallel 11 May SEC comment letter. Baker McKenzie's joint UK/Treasury consultation analysis (May 2026) noted that the FCA's draft perimeter is broadly consistent with the parallel HM Treasury Statutory Instrument on the Cryptoassets Regulations 2026 (final submission window for that SI closed today, 22 May, T-1).

📎 Historical precedent: Drafting follows the structure of PERG 2 (Authorisation and Regulated Activities) used for the original FSMA 2000 perimeter; expected to read as a new PERG chapter rather than standalone guidance.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Skadden client alert 12 May: the staking-arrangement perimeter is the most economically consequential element. By bringing "arranging qualifying-cryptoasset staking" inside the regulated activity definition, the FCA effectively requires custodians, exchanges and DeFi gateways offering UK retail access to liquid-staking tokens to obtain authorisation — a sharper position than the SEC's August 2025 staking guidance, which treated protocol staking as outside Howey. Norton Rose Fulbright's 19 May briefing flagged the parallel inconsistency with the November 2025 Bank of England consultation on systemic sterling stablecoins (closed 10 February 2026), where the BoE proposed bringing UKQS into the systemic-stablecoin perimeter without resolving how the FCA non-systemic UKQS regime maps onto the BoE systemic regime. The Brief's read: the BoE/FCA "joint approach" document signalled for 2026 (covered in 5/19 brief) is the single most important deliverable for resolving the systemic/non-systemic split — until it lands, UK sterling-stablecoin business cases sit in regulatory limbo.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Per Cleary Gottlieb UK FinTech Briefing 19 May: the principal beneficiaries of perimeter clarity are FCA-authorised legacy firms positioned to expand crypto activity (HSBC HSBA.L, Barclays BARC.L, Lloyds LLOY.L, NatWest NWG.L) and Bitstamp UK (Robinhood subsidiary HOOD.US since 2024). The losing population is non-UK platforms that have used the financial-promotions regime as their only UK regulatory exposure: Bitfinex, Bybit, Bitget. Pre-IPO Revolut and Monzo are positioned to convert existing FCA permissions into the new cryptoasset perimeter quickly. London-listed crypto-adjacent names (Argo Blockchain ARB.L, Greenidge Generation GREE.US — secondary UK exposure) gain only marginal benefit because mining isn't in the new perimeter.

AI Governance
