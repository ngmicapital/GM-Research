Alpha — Microprice

Thursday · 21 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #27 11-min read
TL;DR

Microprice is the volume-weighted midpoint between the best bid and best ask — the single number that turns orderbook imbalance into a leading estimator of the next tick. Where regular mid-price treats both sides symmetrically, microprice cross-weights the bid price by ask-side size (and vice versa) so that a heavier resting bid pulls the fair-value reading toward the ask, anticipating the lift before it prints. It's the worst-kept secret of HFT market making, and on a thin 2026 BTC book it's a sharper read than mid for any execution shorter than a 1m candle.

§01
What It Is
The fair-value reading market makers actually use
Microprice is what you get when you stop pretending bid and ask sizes are equal — a single fair-value number that leans toward whichever side is about to break.

If you watch a Level-2 orderbook for a few minutes you'll notice something the mid-price hides: the book is almost never balanced. There's nearly always more resting size on one side than the other, and price almost never just sits politely between them — it gets pulled toward the heavier side because the lighter side has less ammo to absorb crossing orders. Microprice is the cleanest single-number summary of that pull.

The formula is small but the intuition matters. You weight the bid price by the size resting on the ask (and vice versa). When the bid has more size, that big bid pushes microprice up toward the ask — because heavier bid size means more pressure to lift the offer. It's the cross-multiplication that throws most people on first read; once you see it, you can't unsee it.

Sasha Stoikov formalised this in a 2017 paper at Cornell that has quietly become the reference inside every serious market-making shop. He defined microprice as "the limit of a sequence of expected mid-prices" — a martingale by construction, meaning that conditional on current orderbook state, it represents the price you'd expect to settle at if the book just sat there and resolved itself. In practice it's used as the instantaneous fair value against which makers skew quotes and takers measure whether they're paying a premium.

For a Wyckoff-native discretionary trader the value is narrower but real: at decision points (test of a low, retake of a level, anticipated absorption), microprice tells you whether the book is already leaning in the direction you want to trade — before any candle confirms it.

MID-PRICE(P_bid + P_ask) / 2 — symmetric, ignores size MICROPRICEVolume-weighted mid — leans toward the heavier book side IMBALANCE (I)(V_bid − V_ask) / (V_bid + V_ask) — the skew driver FAIR VALUEWhat a maker believes price 'should' be right now SKEWMaker shifting bid/ask quotes asymmetrically based on inventory + microprice
If you only remember one thing Heavier resting bids push microprice toward the ask, not toward the bid — because bigger bid size is pressure to lift, not pressure to stay flat. That single counter-intuitive sign is the whole concept.
§02
Mechanics
The formula, the cross-multiplication, the N-level extension
Cross-weight bid price by ask size, ask price by bid size; divide by total best-level size. That's first-order microprice. Stoikov's contribution is the recursion that corrects for noise.

Start with the L1 (best bid / best ask) version. The formula is short enough to memorise and reproduce on a napkin:

L1 Microprice (BBO): MP = (V_bid × P_ask + V_ask × P_bid) / (V_bid + V_ask) Equivalent imbalance form: MP = P_mid + (I × S/2) where I = (V_bid − V_ask) / (V_bid + V_ask) S = P_ask − P_bid (the spread) Reads: I = 0 → MP sits exactly at mid I = +1 → all size on bid → MP = P_ask (max skew up) I = −1 → all size on ask → MP = P_bid (max skew down)
The imbalance form is the one to internalise. Microprice = mid + (signed imbalance × half-spread). It cannot exceed the bid or the ask — by construction, MP lives between them. That bounded range is exactly why it's a useful fair-value proxy for makers: it's a directional adjustment within a known band, not an unbounded forecast.

The L1 version captures the front of the book but ignores everything stacked behind it. A 50 BTC bid with nothing behind it is structurally weaker than a 50 BTC bid backed by another 200 BTC at the next three ticks. The N-level extension (sometimes called VAMP, Volume-Adjusted Mid Price) sums across depth:

N-Level VAMP: VAMP_N = (Σ P_bid_i × Q_ask_i + Σ P_ask_i × Q_bid_i) / (Σ Q_bid_i + Σ Q_ask_i) where i = 1..N and N is usually defined as a fixed depth (e.g. 5 levels) or as a percentage band around mid (e.g. ±0.05%)
In crypto, ±5 bps depth is a common default for liquid pairs. On a thin book (current 2026 BTC) you want a wider band — ±10 to 15 bps — because the L1 size is small and noisy relative to true resting interest.

Stoikov's own contribution goes further. The L1 formula assumes the next mid-price move is fully explained by current imbalance; in reality, imbalance evolves stochastically and the book has memory. He models it as a discrete-time Markov chain over (spread, imbalance) states and computes microprice as the limit of expected mid-prices under that chain. Empirically the recursion converges in roughly six iterations and produces a tighter predictor than the static L1 calculation, especially when the spread is wider than usual.

The conceptual cousin is Order Flow Imbalance (OFI), which we covered indirectly across the Bid/Ask Imbalance and Passive vs Aggressive briefings. OFI tracks changes in bid/ask size over time (the dynamic flow); microprice takes a snapshot of current state and converts it to a price. They're complementary — OFI tells you the book is being repriced; microprice tells you where the next print is likely to land.

§04
In Practice
Three uses, one rule, an entry/exit example
Treat persistent microprice skew (|I| > 0.7 for >5 seconds) as a directional pre-print signal; treat a flickering skew as noise to be ignored.
i.

Quote skew (for makers)

If you're providing liquidity, your bid and ask should orbit microprice — not mid. When microprice drifts 60% of half-spread toward the ask, narrow your ask quote and widen the bid; you're getting paid to be on the side that's about to be lifted, and you avoid getting run over on the side that's about to be hit.

ii.

Execution timing (for takers)

If your model says buy, don't lift the offer when microprice is sitting at mid or below. Wait for microprice to lean toward the ask — then your aggressive fill is happening into pressure that's already there, not against it. Same logic inverted on the sell side. This is worth 2–4 ticks per fill on a typical BTC perp, which compounds fast at scale.

iii.

Discretionary confluence at Wyckoff decision points

At a spring or upthrust test, the question is whether the book has rotated. Open Bookmap or a depth-aware tool; if microprice is sitting persistently on the opposite side of where the candle is closing, the book is already disagreeing with the print — that's confluence with whatever your structure read is suggesting.

iv.

Divergence as exhaustion (microprice vs price)

If price prints a fresh leg up but microprice fails to follow — best bid is small, best ask is heavy, MP drifts back toward mid — the aggressive flow is doing the work alone with no resting bid support. That's a thin print, vulnerable to a reversal as soon as the aggressors pause.

Worked example — BTC perp, microprice-led entry
SetupBTC perp 80,200 / 80,210 (10-tick spread, mid = 80,205). Book: 65 BTC bid, 18 BTC ask.
Imbalance (I)(65 − 18) / 83 = +0.566 → MP = 80,205 + 0.566 × 5 = 80,207.8
ReadMP is 78% of the way from mid to ask. Persistent (>5s, holding). Heavy bid not getting cancelled.
ActionLift 80,210 (or post 80,207 limit aggressive). Stop 80,180 (below previous swing low + 1 tick noise buffer). Target 80,260 next minor level.
R mathRisk = 30 ticks (80,210 → 80,180). Reward = 50 ticks (80,210 → 80,260). 1.66R — acceptable when the book is leaning your way.
InvalidationIf MP collapses back through mid before fill, cancel. If 65 BTC bid gets pulled, exit at market — the lift pressure was fake.
Timeframe fit: Sub-1m execution edge (where mid hasn't ticked yet) · 1–5m confluence at structure points · loses utility above 15m where candle-close mechanics dominate microstructure.

The thin-book regime makes microprice both noisier and more valuable

The structural condition matters as much as the formula. Per early-2026 liquidity scans, the aggregated BTC orderbook is sitting roughly 40% below pre-crash depth levels — meaning the L1 size on either side is small, gets eaten quickly, and gets refilled stochastically by makers. In that regime, raw L1 imbalance is noisy: a single 5 BTC market order can flip imbalance from +0.6 to −0.4 within 200ms.

What survives is the persistent skew. When microprice holds toward one side for 5+ seconds while the book refills on the same side, that's not flicker — that's a real resting bid (or ask) being defended by an inventory-managing maker who wants to fill into the move. That's the signal.

Today's tape: BTC ~$80K, Binance funding has just flipped positive (+0.0043%) after a 30-day average of roughly −5%, and the Binance long/short ratio is 36.7% long / 63.3% short — the most bearishly skewed major in the perp universe. OI surged into the $80K test, the largest single-month OI build of 2026 to date.

That positioning configuration is exactly the regime where microprice earns its keep. Crowded shorts mean a heavy bid at any sustained level is likely a maker absorbing forced cover flow; a persistent positive imbalance (MP leaning ask) is the short squeeze beginning to print before any 1m candle confirms it. On the other side: if MP starts leaning bid-ward persistently into a rally, the bid is thin and the offer is being built — meaning the squeeze fuel is exhausted and someone's distributing into it.

§07
Common Traps
Where retail uses of microprice fail
The single biggest mistake: trading flicker. L1 imbalance changes 50 times a second on a liquid book — most of it is noise that means nothing.
1.
Trading the flicker, not the persistence

L1 imbalance updates on every cancellation, every replenishment, every HFT quote re-jig — dozens of times per second. A momentary I = +0.8 reading means nothing. The signal is persistent skew (5+ seconds, surviving multiple market-order hits). Smooth your reading with a rolling window before acting on it.

2.
Ignoring spoofing

Spoofers explicitly engineer the false microprice reading they want you to see. A massive bid posted only to be pulled before it can fill produces a textbook microprice skew — and a textbook trap. Cross-reference with Time & Sales: if the heavy size never absorbs any aggressive print, treat it as decoration, not depth. (See Spoofing & Iceberg Orders and Time & Sales.)

3.
Using L1-only in a thin book

On 2026's BTC depth, the best bid and best ask are often individually small — using only L1 throws away most of the actual resting interest. Use VAMP across ±10 bps (or ±5 levels) instead. The N-level estimator is structurally less noisy and harder to spoof.

4.
Treating microprice as a forecast horizon longer than seconds

Microprice predicts the next tick, not the next candle. It has no information about flow on a 15m timeframe, no macro context, no positioning data. It is a microstructure tool — use it as one. Trying to extrapolate "MP is leaning bullish so I'm long for the day" is a category error.

5.
Honest call: most retail "microprice" indicators are weighted mid in disguise

Many TradingView Pine scripts marketed as "microprice" actually implement the weighted mid formula — same-side weighting (V_bid × P_bid + V_ask × P_ask) / (V_bid + V_ask) — which drifts in the wrong direction. Same-side-weighted bid drags the value toward the bid, not away from it. Read the source code before you trust the label. If you can't read the code, assume the indicator is wrong until proven otherwise.
