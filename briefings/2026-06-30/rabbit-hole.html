The Crystal That Cannot Exist

How a single diffraction pattern on 8 April 1982 broke a 150-year-old law of crystallography — and eventually rewrote it.

A material with fivefold rotational symmetry was physically impossible — until Dan Shechtman saw one. The spacing between atoms in a quasicrystal is governed not by a simple repeating grid but by the golden ratio, embedding one of mathematics' most irrational numbers into solid matter.

TL;DR

On 8 April 1982 Dan Shechtman peered at an electron diffraction pattern of an aluminium-manganese alloy and wrote three words in his lab notebook: "10 fold ???" The tenfold rotational symmetry he was seeing was supposed to be mathematically forbidden in any crystal. What he had found — a quasicrystal — ordered matter without periodicity, its atomic spacings governed by the golden ratio — eventually forced the International Union of Crystallography to rewrite the definition of what a crystal even is.

Section 01

The Forbidden Pattern

Crystallography in 1982 rested on a law so fundamental it was considered mathematical bedrock: crystals must be periodic. Their atoms must tile space with a repeating unit cell, the way bathroom tiles cover a floor. And the same geometry that governs tiling imposes a strict rule — only 2-, 3-, 4-, and 6-fold rotational symmetries are possible. A snowflake's perfect sixfold symmetry obeys the rule. A pentagon's fivefold symmetry violates it. You cannot tile a floor with pentagons without leaving gaps. Therefore, a crystal with fivefold symmetry could not exist.

On 8 April 1982, Dan Shechtman was on sabbatical at the U.S. National Bureau of Standards, studying rapidly solidified alloys with a transmission electron microscope. He cooled a mixture of 86% aluminium and 14% manganese so fast — melt-spinning at tens of thousands of degrees per second — that atoms had no time to settle into their usual arrangements. When he fired an electron beam at the resulting foil and read the diffraction pattern, he saw ten bright dots arranged in a perfect ring. Ten is the double of five. He stared at his sample number 1725 and wrote in his notebook: "10 fold ???"

There ain't no such animal.
— Dan Shechtman, to himself in Hebrew, 8 April 1982 — his own first reaction to the diffraction pattern he had just recorded

He spent two years trying to disprove himself. His research group leader told him to leave the group, suggesting he was bringing disgrace on the team. When he finally convinced the physicist John Cahn to look at the data seriously, Cahn arranged for proper crystallographers to review it. They confirmed what the pattern showed. The paper by Shechtman, Blech, Gratias, and Cahn appeared in Physical Review Letters in November 1984 — and landed like a grenade into a calm discipline that had not revised its foundations since 1848.

Section 02

Why the Golden Ratio Lives in Metal

The mechanism that makes a quasicrystal possible is aperiodic order — a structure that is perfectly ordered (producing sharp diffraction spots, like a crystal) but never repeats. The closest two-dimensional analogy was already sitting in the mathematical literature: Penrose tiling, invented by Roger Penrose in 1974. Two diamond-shaped tiles — a fat one and a thin one — can cover an infinite plane with no gaps and no overlaps, following simple matching rules at their edges. The result looks locally fivefold symmetric everywhere, yet the overall pattern never repeats. It is ordered without being periodic.

The golden ratio in the diffraction pattern

When you measure the spacing between any two consecutive peaks in a quasicrystal's diffraction pattern along a fivefold axis, the ratio of successive spacings converges on τ = (1+√5)/2 ≈ 1.618… — the golden ratio. In Penrose tiling, the ratio of fat-tile to thin-tile frequency is also τ. In the Fibonacci sequence (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13…), the ratio of consecutive terms converges on τ. All three are the same mathematical object expressed in geometry, number theory, and now solid-state physics. The diffraction peaks of Shechtman's Al-Mn alloy are spaced in a geometric series with base τ — an irrational number, which is precisely why the structure can never periodically repeat: τ cannot be expressed as a fraction.

The three-dimensional version is built from a single rhomboidal unit — a solid with six diamond-shaped faces, each diamond having diagonals in the ratio of τ. Stack these rhombohedra according to matching rules and you fill space with icosahedral symmetry: atoms arranged on the vertices of twenty-faced polyhedra, a shape beloved by mathematicians and apparently by certain metal alloys cooled fast enough. The icosahedron has exactly the fivefold rotational symmetry that classical crystallography said was forbidden, because icosahedra cannot tile space periodically — but they can tile it quasiperiodically, if you allow the Fibonacci-ratio spacing.

A deeper explanation comes from higher-dimensional geometry. Mathematicians showed that a quasicrystal in three dimensions is actually a projection of a regular, perfectly periodic crystal in six dimensions, sliced through our three-dimensional world at an irrational angle. The irrational angle — related to τ — is what produces the aperiodic pattern we observe. It is as if the atoms are following a perfectly regular schedule in a space we cannot directly see, and the shadow they cast into our space looks irregular. This "cut-and-project" formalism, worked out by Nicolaas de Bruijn in 1981, gave quasicrystals their mathematical foundation before anyone had confirmed they existed physically.

Section 03

The Connections

Linus Pauling's last great battle. The most ferocious opponent of quasicrystals was the two-time Nobel laureate Linus Pauling, arguably the greatest chemist of the twentieth century. From 1984 until his death in 1994, Pauling maintained that Shechtman had misread his data — that what looked like icosahedral symmetry was actually a phenomenon called twinning, where many small ordinary crystals grown together at angles fool the eye into seeing impossible symmetry. Pauling's alternative models required unit cells containing up to 20,000 atoms and became steadily more baroque as evidence mounted against them. His famous dismissal — "There is no such thing as quasicrystals, only quasi-scientists" — is now one of the most quoted wrong statements in modern science. When even Pauling's persistence failed to dislodge the new results, the scientific community's capitulation was total: the International Union of Crystallography formally rewrote its definition of a crystal in 1992, dropping the periodicity requirement.

Medieval Islam got there first. In 2007 physicists Peter Lu and Paul Steinhardt published a study in Science showing that Islamic architects had been decorating mosques with quasiperiodic Penrose-like tilings since at least the thirteenth century. The Darb-i Imam shrine in Isfahan, Iran (1453 CE) contains girih tiles — a set of five shapes that, when assembled following traditional design rules, produce a pattern with tenfold near-symmetry that does not repeat across the entire visible surface. Medieval craftsmen working centuries before formal crystallography had empirically discovered aperiodic tiling by aesthetic intuition. Their pattern prefigured Penrose's mathematics by 500 years and Shechtman's metal by 529.

A meteorite settled the question of naturalness. The remaining nagging doubt after the laboratory synthesis — could quasicrystals only be manufactured? — was laid to rest in 2009 when Paul Steinhardt and colleagues identified the mineral icosahedrite (Al₆₃Cu₂₄Fe₁₃) inside the Khatyrka meteorite from the Koryak Mountains in Russia. Formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago, likely during a high-velocity collision between asteroids in the early solar system, it is the oldest known quasicrystal. Nature, it turns out, had been making "impossible" crystals since before the Earth formed. A second natural quasicrystal — decagonite (Al₇₁Ni₂₄Fe₅), with tenfold symmetry — was confirmed from the same meteorite in 2015.

The practical payoff and the modern parallel. Quasicrystals are hard, have low friction, conduct heat poorly, and resist corrosion — a set of properties rarely combined in the same material. Al-Cu-Fe-Cr quasicrystalline coatings were commercially developed as non-stick, scratch-resistant cookware surfaces (harder than stainless steel, stable above 1,000°C) and as low-friction coatings for surgical instruments and bearings. The deeper lesson maps directly onto how paradigm shifts happen in any field driven by data: the data preceded the theory (Shechtman had no model, only a pattern), the theory preceded acceptance (Penrose tiling gave the structure), and institutional acceptance lagged both by a decade. In financial markets, the same sequence — anomalous data, post-hoc model, delayed repricing — is the standard anatomy of every large mispricing. The quasicrystal story is a clean case study in why the words "impossible by theory" should be treated as a flag, not a refutation.
