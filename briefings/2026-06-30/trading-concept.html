Alpha — Sign of Strength (SOS)

Tuesday, 30 June 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue 64 12 min read
TL;DR

Sign of Strength (SOS) is the Wyckoff event that separates a genuine accumulation base from a distribution trap masquerading as one. After a prolonged phase of absorption — where the Composite Operator has been quietly building a position against the crowd's panic selling — the SOS is the first unambiguous upward thrust that crosses the Creek (the resistance formed by prior reaction highs inside the range) on expanding volume and widening spread. Effort and result finally align: large volume produces large upward price movement. The mechanics are a direct microstructure read — the passive bid that was absorbing supply throughout the accumulation now flips to aggressive lift, and selling pressure collapses because supply has been exhausted. In practice: an SOS without a subsequent pullback test (the Last Point of Support, or LPS) is not actionable — you wait for the retest on declining volume, confirm supply is absent, and enter there. Builds on Absorption (Issue #59) and Effort vs Result (Issue #58).

§01
What It Is
The advance that proves accumulation is complete
Sign of Strength (SOS) is the high-volume, wide-spread advance through the top of an accumulation range — the first bar or swing that proves supply has been exhausted and the Composite Operator is committed to markup.

The Sign of Strength is the moment accumulation becomes visible. Up to this point, the Composite Operator — Wyckoff's label for the aggregate of well-capitalised, informed participants who move markets — has been absorbing supply quietly and methodically. Retail traders see a choppy sideways range, rising and falling volume, and no sustained direction. They get bored, they sell for a small loss, or they short it assuming the range will break down. The Composite Operator is on the other side of every one of those trades. When the supply is finally exhausted — when there are no more weak hands willing to sell cheaply — the SOS fires: a thrust upward on expanding volume and expanding spread that crosses the resistance (the Creek) formed by the prior reaction highs inside the accumulation range. For the first time in the base, effort and result are aligned.

The SOS is the first move in the markup phase, but it is not the entry point. Wyckoff's original schema identifies the SOS as a signal, and then expects a pullback — the Last Point of Support (LPS) — where price retraces toward the broken resistance-now-support on declining volume. The LPS is the entry: you are confirming that supply has not re-emerged, that sellers who might have defended the old resistance have been absorbed, and that the new demand structure holds. An SOS without a clean LPS is an invitation to buy strength; an LPS after an SOS is an invitation to buy confirmed structure. One of those is significantly higher probability. Builds on Absorption (Issue #59) and Effort vs Result (Issue #58).

The microstructure translation is direct. Throughout accumulation, the Composite Operator's passive bids absorb every taker sell — creating the CVD divergence and OFI stability seen in the prior two Alpha issues (Issues #62 and #63). On the SOS bar, that passive bid flips: accumulated inventory now drives aggressive lift, taker buys overwhelm the offer, and the spread widens because sellers can no longer refresh their offers fast enough. The delta on an SOS bar is strongly positive and the volume is the highest in the recent range — the footprint equivalent is stacked bid imbalances at every price in the candle body, offers being consumed without restocking. The SOS is not magic — it is what absorption looks like the moment it switches from passive accumulation to active distribution on the other side of the trade.

If you only remember one thing: The SOS is confirmation, not entry. The trade is the LPS — the retest on light volume after the Creek is crossed. An SOS tells you what happened; an LPS tells you it held.
§02
Mechanics
Volume, spread, and the anatomy of an SOS bar
SOS fires when expanding volume produces expanding upward spread — effort and result align after a prolonged absorption phase where supply was eaten without price going anywhere.

An SOS has three simultaneous conditions that must be present. Miss any one and you are looking at a rally within distribution, a bear flag rip, or an un-tested breakout that will fail. All three together constitute the Wyckoff confirmation:

SOS conditions = Wide Spread + Expanding Volume + Creek Crossing
Wide spread: the bar's range (high − low) is materially larger than the average bar range of the preceding 10–20 bars. Expanding volume: volume on the SOS bar is the highest in the current range, or among the top 3. Creek crossing: close is above the resistance line connecting the prior reaction highs inside the accumulation structure.

The Creek is not a single price level — it is a zone defined by the reaction highs of the range, typically the Automatic Rally (AR) high and the highs of subsequent tests within the trading range. In practice, draw a gently sloping line across those highs; the SOS must close above it, not just wick through it. A wick through on declining volume is the Upthrust After Distribution (UTAD) failure pattern — the opposite signal. Only a close through the Creek on heavy volume is an SOS.

Volume condition: VSOS ≥ 1.5 × Vavg(20)
Where V_avg(20) is the 20-bar average volume inside the trading range. The specific multiplier varies by market and timeframe — the key is that it must be visually obvious: the SOS bar should be the tallest volume bar on the range chart without having to squint.

The spread condition is equally critical. In Wyckoff's effort-versus-result framework: high volume + narrow spread = absorption (still in accumulation); high volume + wide spread upward = demand winning, markup beginning (SOS). This distinction is what separates a climactic buying bar that distributes supply into demand from a genuine SOS that burns through supply. Tom Williams' Volume Spread Analysis (VSA) codified this exact distinction: any high-volume upward bar must show a close in the top quartile of its range and a spread wider than recent bars to qualify as genuine strength rather than supply being dumped into an enthusiastic crowd.

After the SOS, the expected pullback (LPS) should occur on notably declining volume and narrowing spread. If the pullback brings volume back to SOS levels and spread remains wide, supply has re-entered — the SOS was not clean and the trade is off. The LPS is only valid when volume dries up on the retest, confirming the absence of supply.

Two key visual rules to burn in: (1) the SOS volume bar must dwarf everything in the trading range — it should visually jump off the chart without prompting; (2) the LPS volume bar must be obviously smaller than the SOS, confirming that supply did not return on the pullback. If those two bars are similar in height, the SOS was not a clean structural event and the LPS retest is not confirmed. This is the volume-spread analysis test at its most direct.

§04
In Practice
Four rules for trading the SOS confirmation
Never enter on the SOS bar itself — the trade is the Last Point of Support (LPS) pullback on declining volume that confirms supply is genuinely gone.
1

Identify the trading range structure before looking for an SOS

An SOS without a prior accumulation context is just a strong candle. You need to identify: the Selling Climax (SC) low that started the range, the Automatic Rally (AR) high that defines the upper boundary, and the Creek resistance line connecting subsequent rally highs inside the range. Only when that structure is visible and the range has had enough time to absorb supply (typically weeks on the daily, multiple sessions on the 4H) do you start watching for the SOS trigger.

2

Wait for the LPS — the SOS bar itself is not the entry

The SOS bar's close is typically 3–8% above the Creek on a clean crypto accumulation — chasing it means entering at the first resistance test above the range. The LPS pullback on declining volume is the structural confirmation entry: supply has been exhausted at the Creek level, the range is genuinely behind price, and the next upside test is likely to achieve follow-through. If you miss the LPS, wait for the next BU (Back-Up) test — the structure gives multiple entry windows.

3

Calibrate position size to the range width (P&F cause-and-effect)

Wyckoff's Point-and-Figure cause-and-effect method estimates markup targets from the width of the accumulation base: more time spent absorbing supply = larger potential move up (more cause = more effect). Count the columns across the accumulation range on a P&F chart and project the target. On crypto, the practical approach is: measure the SC-to-AR range in percentage, multiply by 2–3 for a conservative target, and size the LPS entry so that risk-to-reward is at least 2R to the first target.

4

Use the microstructure read on the SOS and LPS

The SOS bar on a footprint should show stacked bid imbalances at every price level — every offer lifted, aggressively. The LPS bar should show declining volume and a neutral-to-positive delta that cannot push price below the Creek. A footprint with ask imbalances on the LPS (sellers pressing into the retest) means supply has returned — the setup is invalidated. Builds on Absorption (Issue #59), Effort vs Result (Issue #58), and OFI (Issue #63).

“The composite operator accumulates stock over a long period of time without revealing his intentions. He then causes a markup, distributing as prices rise.”

“Volume is the effort; price spread is the result. When large effort finally produces a large upward result, supply has been overcome.”

§07
Common Traps
How traders get faked out before the real SOS
The biggest mistake: treating any strong green candle on volume as an SOS when there has been no prior accumulation phase to give it context — structure must come first.
1
Calling every strong candle an SOS without prior accumulation context

An SOS is a specific structural event — the resolution of a defined accumulation phase. A random impulse bar on volume in the middle of a downtrend is not an SOS; it is a dead-cat bounce, a bear-flag rip, or distribution into late longs. Before you identify an SOS, you must identify the Selling Climax (SC) and the Trading Range (TR) that followed it. No TR, no SOS — just a green candle with volume.

2
Entering on the SOS bar instead of waiting for the LPS

The SOS is the signal; the Last Point of Support is the entry. Chasing the SOS bar means buying at or above the Creek, typically 3–8% above the range top in crypto, with no structural confirmation that supply has been absorbed there. The LPS gives you: a lower price, a smaller stop (Creek as support beneath you), and the confirmation of low-volume supply absence. Buying the SOS is buying the announcement; buying the LPS is buying the proof.

3
Mistaking an Upthrust (UT) for an SOS

An Upthrust pierces the Creek on moderate-to-high volume but closes back below it — supply absorbed the demand at that level and pushed price back into the range. The key difference is the close: an SOS closes above the Creek convincingly; a UT closes back below it. In real time this can look identical during the bar; the read comes at the close. If price surges through the Creek intra-bar but closes back below it on the 4H, treat it as an Upthrust (bearish), not an SOS — and expect a re-test of range lows.

4
Ignoring the Spring as a prerequisite

Many of the highest-quality SOS moves are preceded by a Spring — a shakeout below the range's Selling Climax support that triggers retail stops and allows the Composite Operator to acquire the last supply cheaply before the markup. An SOS off a prior Spring typically has stronger follow-through because the exhaustion of supply is more complete. An SOS without a prior Spring is valid but warrants a tighter stop — the range may not be fully absorbed, and a late Spring can still occur below the Creek even after an apparent SOS.

5
Honest callout: Wyckoff is a framework, not a formula — and the SOS is the most abused label in it

No trading approach is more frequently retrofitted onto price action than Wyckoff. After every accumulation-looking range, someone on social media draws the schematic and labels it — the SC, AR, Spring, SOS. The SOS label in particular gets attached to any strong candle because it makes the analyst look like they called it. In practice, the Wyckoff method requires contemporaneous identification of the phase, not post-hoc label-fitting. If you can only see the SOS clearly after the fact, you are describing history, not trading a signal. The discipline is to identify the TR structure in real time, draw the Creek in advance, and only declare an SOS when the closing volume condition is met at that pre-defined level — not when the chart happens to have gone up.
