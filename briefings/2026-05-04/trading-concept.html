Alpha — Speed of Tape

Monday · 04 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #12 11-min read
TL;DR

Speed of tape — prints per second on the time-and-sales feed — measures the urgency of participants, not the direction. When the tape accelerates from a baseline of 20–40 prints/sec to 200+ in seconds, someone has decided to get filled now, regardless of price. The practical edge: pace acceleration into a structural level is information that price hasn't shown yet — a fast, one-sided burst into a known liquidity pool tells you a sweep is happening before the candle closes, and a sudden deceleration after a thrust tells you the move has run out of buyers. Read pace first, price second.

§01
What It Is
Urgency on the time-and-sales feed · prints per second as a state variable
Speed of tape is the rate at which trades are printing — transactions per second on the time-and-sales window — and it is the cleanest single read of how urgent participants are right now.

Every executed trade stamps a print on the time-and-sales (T&S) feed: price, size, side, timestamp. The number of prints per unit of time is the tempo of the market. Calm tape: a few prints per second, mostly small, mixed bid and ask. Active tape: dozens per second, larger sizes, a clear bias to one side. Hot tape: the feed becomes a blur — hundreds of prints per second, all stacked at the ask or bid, the order book visibly shedding levels as it goes.

Tony, Wyckoff would call this the activity of the tape. Bookmap calls it tempo. Footprint platforms call it speed of tape. They all measure the same primitive: how many decisions are crossing the spread per unit of time. That metric, more than any single candle or indicator, tells you whether participants are willing to wait (passive, low pace) or need to be filled now (aggressive, high pace).

Why it matters mechanically: every print on the tape is a market order eating a passive resting limit order. A burst of pace is a burst of forced liquidity-taking. When that burst is one-sided and lands into a known structural pocket — prior swing high, equal lows, a liquidation cluster, a thin patch of book — it is the first observable evidence of a sweep, breakout, or liquidation cascade. Price will catch up; the tape already knows. As the Bookmap practitioner guide puts it: "the speed of the tape helps you stay one step ahead of momentum before it becomes obvious on the chart."

Speed of tape is a sibling concept to CVD, footprint, and absorption — but it lives one rung lower in the stack. CVD asks who is winning; speed of tape asks how hard they're trying right now. The signal is intentionally crude. It does not tell you direction in isolation; combined with the bid/ask split of those prints, it does.

If you only remember one thing Pace is urgency. A fast, one-sided tape is participants telling you they cannot wait. A slow, two-sided tape is the market telling you no one cares.
§02
Mechanics
Calculation · baseline establishment · pace + bias decoder
Pace is computed by counting prints (or summing volume) over a rolling N-second window, then comparing to a recent rolling average — burst signals fire when current pace exceeds a multiple of baseline.

The raw inputs are exchange trade messages. Every Binance, Bybit, or Coinbase websocket trade event carries timestamp, price, size, and an aggressor flag (or implied side). Speed-of-tape implementations differ on what they count, but the core formula is the same.

Pace (prints): P(t) = count of trades in window [t−N, t] Pace (volume): V(t) = Σ(trade_size) over window [t−N, t] Baseline (rolling average): P̄(t) = SMA of P over a longer window [t−M, t] (e.g. M = 30 × N) Burst signal: P(t) > k · P̄(t) → pace acceleration typical k = 3 to 5 for "noticeable", 8+ for "violent" Directional pace: ΔP(t) = (asks at offer − bids at bid) over window N sign(ΔP) gives the bias of the burst
N is typically 1–5 seconds for scalping, 30–60s for swing context. Tools like Bookmap, MotiveWave's Speed of Tape study, and Volumetrica all expose configurable N and threshold k. ATAS and Sierra Chart expose this as "Cluster Speed" or "Tape Speed Filter."

The two operational modes worth distinguishing:

ModeWhat it showsWhen to use
Print countNumber of executions per second — sensitive to retail clustersSpotting algo bursts, panic prints, headline-driven flow
Volume per secondNotional throughput — large prints weighted heavierDistinguishing institutional aggression from retail noise
Side-split paceAsks/sec vs bids/sec — directional componentConfirming whether a burst is one-sided or churn
Tape × levelsPace overlaid on absorption/liquidity zonesCatching sweeps, false breakouts, and stop runs in real time

The most common practical decoder pairs pace and side into four states. Fast + one-sided = aggression is in control; trade with it. Fast + two-sided = volatility burst, no clear hand; stand aside. Slow + drifting = passive control, mean-reversion zone. Slow + then sudden burst at a level = ambush — the highest-information setup the tape produces, because the silence before the burst means the move was not foreshadowed by ongoing pressure.

Critical nuance: pace decay is as informative as pace acceleration. A thrust into a level on 200 prints/sec that immediately collapses to 30 prints/sec while price holds the high is absorption — passive size on the other side soaked the aggression and the aggressor ran out of fuel. Bookmap's framing: "Aggressive moves into high liquidity with sudden stalls signal potential exhaustion." Read the deceleration; it is where reversals are born.

Speed of tape only generates trades from one quadrant of this 2×2 — fast, one-sided pace. Quiet drift is for swing context, chop is for sitting on hands, and a two-sided volatility burst is a coin flip you do not need to take. The full alpha is in pace + bias agreement, ideally landing into a structural level.
The reversal template: pace grinds at baseline (~30 prints/sec), then bursts to 200+/sec on a single thrust candle into resistance. The very next bar, pace collapses back to baseline — aggression has run out — and price reverses. The burst alone is a momentum signal; the burst-followed-by-stall is the reversal signal. Without the stall, you do not have absorption; you have a continuation.
§04
In Practice
Establish baseline · pair with structure · trade the deceleration
The one rule: never trade pace in a vacuum. Pace at a level is signal; pace in the middle of a range is noise.
1

Calibrate the Baseline Before You Calibrate the Trade

Every market and every session has its own normal pace. BTC perp on Binance during US session might run 60–100 prints/sec baseline; the same pair in Asia dead hours might run 15–25/sec. Watch the tape for 5–10 minutes before you act. Without a calibrated baseline, you cannot see a burst — every cluster looks fast.

2

Pair Pace With a Pre-Defined Level

Pace bursts in the middle of a range are usually algos rebalancing or news flickers — not tradeable. Pace bursts at a level — prior swing high, equal lows, a liquidation cluster from Coinglass, an HVN/LVN — are tradeable. Mark your levels first; then watch for pace to confirm.

3

Trade the Stall, Not the Burst

The burst is the trap most traders fall into — chasing a fast tape into a level that was the entire reason institutions were waiting there. The cleaner trade is the deceleration: pace collapses while price holds the high, body of the next candle closes back inside the level. That's the fade. Burst alone is FOMO bait; burst plus stall is information.

4

Cross-Reference With Size, Not Just Count

Two hundred prints/sec of 0.01 BTC is retail panic. Twenty prints/sec at 5 BTC each is one institutional desk working a sell. Print count and notional volume tell you different things — always glance at trade size on the T&S window. Bookmap and footprint platforms make this trivial; on raw exchange feeds, sort by size.

Worked Example — BTC Fade After a Headline Pop
SetupBTC ranging 77,800–78,400 for 6 hours. Baseline pace ~40 prints/sec. Pre-defined resistance at $79,000 (prior swing + liquidation cluster).
BurstHeadline hits at 14:08 Sydney. Pace explodes to ~280 prints/sec, all ask-side. BTC sweeps from $78,150 → $79,050 in under 4 minutes.
StallPace collapses back to ~50/sec within 2 minutes of the high. Bid prints start outnumbering asks. Price holds just below $79,000 — no continuation.
Trigger5m candle closes back below $78,800 — failed sweep. Enter short at market ~$78,750.
StopAbove the headline high: $79,150. Risk = $400/BTC (~0.51%).
TargetRange mid at $78,100. R:R = ~1.6:1. Trail stop to entry below $78,400.
InvalidationPace re-accelerates ask-side and prints close above $79,150 — sweep was real, exit and reverse.

Timeframe fit: 1m–5m for entry; pace itself is a sub-second metric. 1h+ for level identification. Speed of tape is execution-grade information — it does not tell you what to trade, only when to fire on what you've already decided to trade.

BTC's Iran-Headline Sweep — A Pace Lesson in Two Acts

Through Saturday and Sunday (3–4 May 2026), BTC consolidated tightly around $78,000–78,400 on subdued weekend pace. Sometime during the US/Asia handoff, a headline crossed reporting that Iran had sent a fresh diplomatic proposal to the United States. The tape immediately accelerated — ask-side prints stacked, depth on the offer side of the order book was eaten, and BTC pushed to a session high above $79,000 before the move had been priced into the cash equities open.

The classic acceleration phase. Then the second act: Trump publicly expressed skepticism about the Iranian proposal within the hour. The tape did not panic-sell — it simply stopped buying. Pace collapsed back to baseline, ask-side prints dried up, and price drifted back to the prior consolidation zone near $78,200 over the following candles. No catalyst for a flush; just an aggressor that ran out of reason to keep paying up. By Sunday's close BTC was right back at $78,190 — exactly where it had started, with intraday range cleared and stops on both sides of $78K refreshed.

Two things this teaches. First, fast tape into a clear structural pocket ($79K had been prior resistance for the entire month) is not by itself a continuation signal — most of the time it is a sweep that gets faded the moment the catalyst weakens. Second, the absence of pace on the way back down is itself information: nobody chased the reversal because nobody was urgent. Just a controlled fade as the speculative ask dried up. This is also why the morning's Morning Edge flagged BTC as grinding $78K with crowded shorts — the market is in a state where headline-driven bursts move price two ways without committing to either.

§07
Common Traps
Five ways traders wreck themselves with speed of tape
The single biggest mistake: chasing the burst. The whole edge of speed of tape is reading the deceleration that comes after — not the acceleration itself.
1
Chasing the Burst

The burst is the part everyone sees — pace explodes, price thrusts, you market in. Most of the time you are buying the high tick from a desk that has been waiting at that level. The burst is the bait; the stall is the fade. If you can only watch one thing, watch what happens to pace after the thrust candle.

2
No Baseline, No Burst

A "fast tape" is meaningless without a reference. 100 prints/sec is fast for ETH at 03:00 Sydney; it's slow for BTC at the US cash open. Sit with the tape for 5–10 minutes before you trade it. If you cannot tell me current pace as a multiple of baseline, you are reading noise.

3
Treating Pace as Direction

Speed of tape is not a directional indicator. A two-sided burst — both bid and ask prints stacking — is volatility, not momentum. Always cross-reference pace with side: only a fast and one-sided tape carries directional information. Bookmap's coloured dots, ATAS's cluster filter, or simply the bid/ask split on the T&S column do this for you.

4
Ignoring HFT Print-Spam

Modern algos generate enormous print counts at trivial sizes — a single HFT can flood the tape with 500 prints/sec of 0.001 BTC each, signalling nothing. Always sanity-check pace against notional volume. If the per-second count is 5× baseline but per-second volume is flat, you are watching algorithmic noise, not aggression.

5
Treating Speed of Tape as a Standalone Strategy

Speed of tape is not a system. It is execution information that sharpens trades you have already decided to take from structure, levels, and bias. The traders who got rich "reading the tape" in 2010 had no other tools — modern crypto has CVD, footprint, heatmaps, OI, funding, and on-chain flow. Pace is now one input among many. Anyone selling you a "tape reading course" as standalone alpha in 2026 is selling you nostalgia. Use it as a confluence layer; do not build a strategy around it alone.
