The senior author, endocrinologist Bu Yeap of the University of Western Australia, argues the low reading may signal other health issues or risk factors, not disease of the testes, so the number is a prompt to investigate rather than a target to treat. Longevity & Performance Science

Two studies this cycle push in the same direction: a single biomarker rarely carries its own meaning. Low testosterone in a large cohort tracked with higher cancer mortality, yet the authors describe it as a flag for other health problems, so the mechanism is still open. Partial reprogramming protected aged brain tissue in mice, but only when the reprogramming dose came before the injury, which means the timing window matters as much as the headline effect. The practical lesson for a biomarker-tracking practitioner is to read any one value against age, body composition, and a second marker before changing anything.

Low testosterone and cancer death: 18% higher relative risk in 26,000 men
Obs

A cohort of about 26,000 men, led by endocrinologist Bu Yeap at the University of Western Australia and published in The Lancet Healthy Longevity, compared the lowest and highest fifths of testosterone. Men in the bottom fifth showed an 18% higher relative risk of dying from any cancer, and the association held after adjustment for age and other health-related factors. The study is observational, so it cannot show that low testosterone causes cancer death. Yeap's own reading is that a low result may reflect other health issues or risk factors that deserve attention in their own right.

Practical takeaway: If a total testosterone result falls in the bottom fifth of your reference range, retest on a morning draw, add SHBG and free testosterone, and review the metabolic and inflammatory markers you already track before drawing any conclusion from one number.

Source: ScienceAlert report on The Lancet Healthy Longevity paper, 14 Nov 2026
Partial reprogramming shields the aged brain from stroke damage in mice
Mech

In a study published in Aging Cell in October 2026, aged mice received four weeks of cyclic OSKM expression before a permanent cerebral artery occlusion. At 48 hours the ischaemic area was smaller in treated animals (24.9% of the hemisphere versus 31.5% in controls), with neurons and myelin better preserved. The mechanism proposed is that brief, cyclic expression of reprogramming factors resets cell state without full dedifferentiation. The dosing ran before the injury and the readout was acute, so the result says nothing yet about treating an established stroke.

Practical takeaway: No protocol applies to people yet. Track it as a signal that the timing of any future intervention may matter more than its dose.

Source: Arclight Longevity Survey, November 2026 (summarising Aging Cell, October 2026)
⚡ Implication: Treat any single longevity biomarker as one input. The cohort result argues for a repeat measurement with a second marker before action, and the reprogramming result argues that intervention timing is the variable to test once human studies appear.
Section 02
Training & Performance
ACSM 2026 position stand: the first full update in 17 years
Guide

The 2026 position stand from the American College of Sports Medicine synthesised 137 systematic reviews covering more than 30,000 participants. Hypertrophy occurred across loads from about 30% to 100% of one-rep max, provided effort was sufficient, so the classic 8–12 rep rule is no longer treated as a requirement. Taking every set to absolute failure is not necessary; training within roughly 2–3 reps in reserve was judged enough. The stand also points to weekly volume as the main driver, with about 10 hard sets per muscle group as a guide for hypertrophy.

Practical protocol: Use loads you can move for 8–20 reps, stop each set 2–3 reps short of failure, and keep weekly hard sets for each muscle group in the 10–12 range to start. Progress by adding volume only when recovery and joint tolerance hold.

Source: ACSM, Med Sci Sports Exerc 58(4), 2026
⚡ Update: Weekly training volume, now with a 12–20 set range
Meta

A 2026 Sports Medicine meta-regression (volume 56, pages 481–505) found about 12 sets per muscle per week optimised growth for most trained individuals, as summarised in a 2026 Journal of Applied Physiology conference report. A 2026 narrative review in the Journal of Functional Morphology and Kinesiology pooled the literature and gave 12 to 20 weekly sets per muscle group as a defensible starting range, with a non-linear dose-response. This is an update to the June protocol on strength volume, because the new evidence refines the target range rather than repeating the earlier message.

Practical protocol: Start a lagging muscle at 12 weekly sets and add two sets per week for three weeks while tracking performance and soreness. Stop when progress stalls or recovery is poor.

Sources: J Appl Physiol 2026 conference summary · JFMK 11(4), 2026
Abrupt +120% volume jump did not blunt hypertrophy in trained adults
RCT

A 2026 study in the Journal of Applied Physiology compared a sudden +120% increase in weekly resistance training volume with a modest +20% increase in trained individuals. The larger jump did not reduce hypertrophy, and most acute and chronic molecular markers did not differ between protocols. Neither protocol significantly increased muscle fibre cross-sectional area, which is an important caveat: the study shows the higher load was not harmful, not that it produced additional growth. Full design details beyond the abstract were not verified for this issue.

Source: J Appl Physiol 2026, VOL120 vs VOL20 protocols
Integrate now
  • Target 10–12 hard sets per muscle per week before adding more.
  • Train 2–3 reps short of failure rather than to failure on most sets.
  • Use any load from roughly 30% to 100% of one-rep max when effort is sufficient.
Caveats
  • Volume ranges come from pooled and population-level data, not individual responders.
  • The abrupt-volume trial did not show added fibre growth in either arm.
  • Sets per muscle are an estimate; count only hard sets that reach the target effort.
⚡ Implication: For a PPL split, distribute the weekly set target across the three days and keep the effort stop consistent. Log weekly sets per muscle for six weeks before judging whether to add volume.
Section 03
Supplements & Nutrition
⚡ Update: Nicotinamide riboside raised NAD but did not help early Parkinson's
RCT

The Phase 3 NOPARK trial, reported in JAMA in 2026, gave one year of nicotinamide riboside to 410 people with early Parkinson's disease. NAD metabolism rose as intended, but the clinical scores were slightly worse than placebo. The NAD precursor story was covered in late June, so this result is a new angle: a biomarker lift did not translate into clinical benefit. Mechanism: the precursor feeds NAD salvage, which is measurable, while the disease pathway it was meant to affect was not moved. Evidence rating: strong design, single disease population.

Source: NOPARK trial, JAMA 2026 (summarised by Arclight, November 2026)
⚡ Update: Fish oil lifted brain omega-3 but not memory over two years
RCT

A two-year trial reported in October 2026 found that fish oil supplements delivered omega-3s to the brain, yet did not improve memory or slow Alzheimer's-related brain shrinkage. Mechanism: the omega-3 fatty acids reach brain tissue, so the lack of cognitive effect suggests delivery is not the bottleneck. Practical takeaway: The stronger case for fish oil remains triglyceride lowering and cardiovascular risk, where the evidence is more mature.

Source: ScienceDaily report, October 2026
Vitamin D with calcium: fracture benefit in older adults, mixed elsewhere
Meta

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements summarised a meta-analysis of six trials in 49,282 older adults, where vitamin D plus calcium was linked to a 6% lower risk of any fracture and a 16% lower risk of hip fracture. Other reviews found no benefit, so the evidence is strongest for fracture prevention and weak for general longevity. Practical takeaway: Measure 25(OH)D before supplementing, and treat supplementation as a correction for a measured deficit rather than a default.

Source: InCare review citing NIH ODS, November 2026
⚡ Update: A review of 350+ studies argues for less protein, not more
Expert

A major review of more than 350 studies, reported on 10 November 2026, suggests that eating less protein could slow aging, improve metabolic health, and possibly extend lifespan. This runs against the protein-first message from June. The primary design of the included studies was not verified for this issue, so the strength of the claim cannot be confirmed. Mechanism: the review's proposed pathway is not confirmed in our sources; reduced mTOR and IGF-1 signalling is the usual hypothesis and should be treated as untested here.

Practical takeaway: Do not cut protein below the current target during a hypertrophy block. Hold intake steady and track body composition before changing anything.

Source: ScienceDaily report, 10 November 2026
Choline and metabolic health: a missing link in a critical chain
Expert

A November 2026 review in Current Opinion in Clinical Nutrition and Metabolic Care argues that choline shapes metabolic disease beyond its well-known role in hepatic triglyceride handling. It synthesises the last 18 months of evidence and calls for biomarker-guided precision nutrition. Practical takeaway: No dose is set by this review, so use dietary choline sources as a baseline and look for a clinical reason before supplementing.

Source: Current Opinion in Clinical Nutrition & Metabolic Care 29(6), 2026
