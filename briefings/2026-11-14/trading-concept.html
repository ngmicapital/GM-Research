Alpha — Sweep Orders

Saturday, 14 November 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue TBC 11 min read
TL;DR

A sweep order is a market order large enough to consume every resting level it reaches, so price jumps several ticks in one burst. It matters because the candle only shows the outcome, while the book shows whether the aggressor used up size or met a refill. Practical takeaway: compare the sweep's size with the resting size at the first level, and wait for the refill or the failed refill before you trade the reaction.

§01
What It Is
One order, several price levels, one burst of urgency
A sweep is a marketable order that clears several price levels in one push, so it shows urgency rather than direction.

A sweep is a marketable order that will not wait. It takes every resting share at the best price, steps to the next price level, takes that too, and keeps going until it is filled or the book runs out of size you are willing to pay for. The defining feature is not direction. The order consumes liquidity faster than the book can refill it, so the printed price jumps several ticks in one burst.

Position first: most of what traders call a breakout or a flush on crypto perps is a sweep in disguise. Read it as a statement about urgency. Someone with a deadline crossed the spread through size, and the market had to find a new clearing price to absorb it. The sweep tells you the aggressor accepted slippage, which is more informative than the candle that results.

In crypto the mechanic shows up in two forms. One is a discretionary trader hitting market orders into thin size. The other is forced liquidation orders from exchanges closing levered longs or shorts. Both print as one-sided aggressive volume that walks the book. Telling them apart matters, because a stop run and a liquidation cascade look identical on the candle but say different things about who is still left to sell. This builds on Footprint Charts (issue dated 13 Nov 2026), which showed aggressive volume by price; this issue covers the event that produces it.

§02
Mechanics
From the best ask to the next level, and the average fill
It walks the ask or bid ladder level by level, and the weighted average of those fills sets your slippage.

The mechanic runs in three steps. First, a market buy or sell arrives with no price limit, so it accepts the best posted offer. Second, that offer is smaller than the order, so the matching engine walks to the next level and takes size there, then the next. Third, the last fill becomes the new reference and the book refreshes around it. Your read comes from comparing the order's size with the size resting at the first two or three levels.

Two properties decide whether a sweep moves price or only prints. The first is how much size sits at each level. The second is whether that size gets replenished. A thick level that is taken and refilled within seconds absorbs the sweep. A thin level that is taken and left empty lets the next print run several ticks.

Average fill = Σ(qtyi × pricei) / Σ qtyi
Sum across every level the sweep consumed. qtyi is the size taken at pricei.
Sweep slippage = Average fill − best quoted price
For a buy, positive slippage means the aggressor paid up the ladder. Measure it in dollars or ticks on the same pair, not in percent.

Worked logic: if 40 BTC sits at the best ask and the order wants 90 BTC, the first 40 fill at the top price and the rest fill at higher levels. The gap between the weighted average and the top price is your slippage. The larger that gap relative to the spread, the more the sweep consumed real liquidity instead of nibbling at the edge.

§04
In Practice
Read the refill, not the wick
Size the sweep against the first level's resting depth, then wait for the refill before you act.
1

Size the sweep against the first level

Compare the aggressive size with the resting size at the top two or three levels. A sweep that is a small fraction of the first level is usually noise, while one several times larger than that level will move the book.

2

Watch what refills

A level that gets cleared and then refilled within seconds has absorbed the sweep. A level that stays empty while the next print runs several ticks has genuinely lost liquidity. Confirm the difference on the tape before you act on it.

3

Separate stop runs from liquidation cascades

A discretionary stop run often reverses once the stops are filled. A forced liquidation cascade keeps going until the levered positions are cleared. Check open interest and the size of the flush after the print to see which one you are looking at.

4

Trade the aftermath, not the print

Entering on the sweep candle means paying the slippage you were trying to measure. Wait for the first retest of the swept level and let the reaction define the stop.

§07
Common Traps
Calling every fast candle a sweep
Treating every fast wick as a sweep, and entering on the print, which pays the slippage you were trying to measure.
1
Calling every fast candle a sweep

A quick wick is not a sweep until the book shows size consumed across levels. Fix it by checking the depth ladder or footprint for the aggressive volume before you label the move.

2
Ignoring the size of the first level

Without the resting size at the top levels you cannot tell whether a sweep is absorbed or breaks the level. Fix it by logging the first two levels before the print, not after.

3
Merging stop runs with liquidation cascades

They look the same on the candle but unwind differently. Fix it by checking open interest and whether the flush is still producing size after the first hour.

4
Chasing the print

Entering on the sweep candle pays the slippage that defined the move. Fix it by waiting for the retest of the swept level, and size the stop beyond the sweep extreme.

!
Overhyped: the 'smart money sweep' label

Options-flow sites sell 'sweep' tags as proof of directional conviction. In practice a sweep tag mostly describes how an order was routed across exchanges to fill fast, not what the buyer intended. Treat it as a fill-method label and check the price reaction before you read anything into it.
