Alpha — Stop Clusters & Magnet Zones

Monday · 22 June 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #57 11 MIN READ
TL;DR

Stop clusters are pools of resting stop-loss orders that build up at obvious levels — equal highs, equal lows, round numbers, prior-session extremes — and they act as magnets because triggering them converts dormant stops into forced market orders that accelerate price into the level. Mark where the crowd's stops must sit, expect price to be pulled there and swept, and trade the reaction after the pool is taken — never the breakout into it.

§01
What It Is
Resting stops as stored fuel
A stop cluster is a pool of resting stop orders parked at an obvious level; price is drawn to it because hitting the pool dumps a burst of forced flow into the book.

Price doesn't drift to round numbers and old highs by accident. It's pulled there. Underneath an obvious swing low sits a wall of stop-loss orders from every long who bought the range — and every one of those stops is a market sell waiting to fire. A dense band of them is not "support". It is stored fuel, and price burns toward fuel.

A stop cluster forms wherever the crowd is taught to hide its risk: just beyond equal highs and equal lows, prior-day high/low (PDH/PDL), session opens, and psychological round numbers. The herd places stops in the same handful of spots, so the orders stack into a pool. To a desk that needs to move size, that pool is the only place deep enough to fill into without paying brutal slippage — which is exactly why it gets targeted. Run the stops, harvest the liquidity, then go the other way.

The language to keep straight: buy-side liquidity sits above the market (the stops of shorts, plus breakout buy-stops) and sell-side liquidity sits below (the stops of longs, plus breakdown sell-stops). When price reaches a pool and the resting stops fire, that's a liquidity grab or sweep. This is the same event Wyckoff named a spring (below support) or upthrust (above resistance) ninety years ago — it builds directly on the Wyckoff Spring (Issue #37) and is the resting-stop cousin of the Liquidity Heatmaps (Issue #49) you already know: a heatmap maps resting limit orders; a stop cluster maps resting stop orders, which behave very differently when touched.

§02
Mechanics
Stops → market orders → magnet
A stop is latent aggressive flow, so a cluster is a battery of it; price gets pulled in, the pool discharges, and what happens next tells you sweep versus breakout.

The mechanic runs in four beats:

1. Accumulation. Longs buy a range and tuck stops below the lows; shorts sell it and tuck stops above the highs. The more times a level is touched without breaking, the more obvious it looks — and the heavier the pool that piles up just beyond it. Equal highs and equal lows are the densest because every touch adds a fresh layer of stops.

2. Attraction. Price is drawn toward the larger untapped pool — it's the path of least resistance to real liquidity. This is the "magnet". Nothing mystical: large participants would rather source fills where orders are stacked than chase a thin book.

3. Trigger cascade. Price tags the level and the resting stops convert to market orders in the direction of the move. Sell-stops below a low fire as market sells, accelerating the drop; that self-reinforcing burst is what makes a sweep look violent and fast — it shares its engine with the Liquidation Cascade (Issue #48).

4. The tell. Now the only question that matters: did the cluster firing meet fresh passive liquidity on the other side? If absorbing bids step in and price reclaims the level — it was a sweep (reversal). If new resting liquidity stacks in the breakout direction and price holds beyond the level — it was a genuine breakout (continuation).

Pool fuel ≈ Σ ( resting stop size beyond the level )
  → bigger, more obvious level = deeper pool = stronger magnet

SWEEP (fade it)  = spike into pool · no new passive liq in spike direction · reclaim ≤ a few bars
BREAKOUT (follow it) = spike into pool · fresh passive liq stacks beyond level · price holds
~80% of retail stops cluster within 1–2% beyond obvious highs/lows, which is what makes the pools targetable in the first place (Mind Math Money).

The same wick is read two opposite ways depending on what follows it. That is why the discipline is structural, not predictive:

§04
In Practice
Mark the pools, let price come, fade the sweep
The one rule: mark the pools first, let price come to them, and trade the reaction after the sweep — never chase the move into the cluster.
1

Map the pools before the session

Mark equal highs/lows, PDH/PDL, the weekly open, and round numbers. Those are the only places deep stop liquidity can sit. If you can see the obvious level, so can every desk that needs to fill into it.

2

Wait for the sweep, not the approach

The signal is the failed-breakout wick — a sharp thrust through the pool with no follow-through. The spike into the level is the bait; you want the reaction out of it. "Trade the reaction, not the initial spike."

3

Confirm with flow, not the wick alone

Demand a reclaim of the level plus evidence the spike was absorbed — stalling delta, a CVD divergence, no fresh passive liquidity in the spike direction. A wick with continuation is a breakout, not a sweep.

4

Target the opposite pool

Liquidity runs to liquidity. Once the sell-side pool is taken and price reclaims, the next magnet is the buy-side pool above — that's your objective, not an arbitrary R multiple.

Worked example · BTC spring long off the sell-side pool
SetupBTC ranges 63.6K–64.6K; equal lows park sell-side stops just under 63,650, equal highs / a 330M short-squeeze pool sit at 64,550.
TriggerWick to 62,900 sweeps the sell-side pool, no follow-through, price reclaims 63,650 within two 15m bars.
EntryLong 63,720 on the reclaim close.
Stop62,800 — below the sweep wick (where you'd be wrong, not where the crowd hides).
Target64,550 buy-side pool (the opposite magnet).
R mathRisk 920 pts, reward 830 pts ≈ 0.9R to first pool; trail beyond 64,550 if the squeeze pool ignites for the 65K+ extension.
Timeframe fit: 5m–1h execution; mark the pools off 4h / 1D swing structure. The sweep itself usually prints on a single lower-timeframe wick — be at the screen, or work a reclaim limit.

§07
Common Traps
How the pool eats you instead
The single biggest mistake: buying the breakout into the cluster — at that price, you are the liquidity being harvested.
1
Chasing the break into the pool

Buying the thrust through equal highs (or shorting the break of equal lows) puts you in exactly where smart money needs counterparties. If there's no fresh passive liquidity backing the break, you bought the bait. Wait for the reaction.

2
Stops on the obvious level

A stop sitting one tick under the swing low is the cluster. You are not hiding — you are donating. Place risk beyond where the sweep would invalidate the idea (past the wick), or size down and use a structural stop, not a herd stop.

3
Treating every wick as a sweep

A long wick only counts once price reclaims the level and the move shows no follow-through. A wick with continuation and stacking liquidity is a genuine breakout. Confirm the reclaim before you fade — otherwise you're top/bottom-ticking a trend.

4
Assuming the pool always reverses

Pools also fuel continuation. When the sweep meets fresh passive liquidity in the breakout direction and price holds, the cluster was the ignition for a trend, not a reversal. The tell is the order flow after the trigger — not your bias going in.

5
Overhyped: "the market is fully manipulated"

The SMC repackaging of this — equal highs and lows as "liquidity", the implication that every move is engineered against you — is mostly rebranding. The mechanic is real and old: Wyckoff's spring/upthrust, Raschke's Turtle Soup, the failed breakout. The mystique and the conspiracy framing are not. Stops cluster at obvious levels because traders are predictable, not because a cabal is hunting you specifically. Trade the structure; skip the lore.
