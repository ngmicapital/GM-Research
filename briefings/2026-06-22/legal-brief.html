World Liberty Nears an OCC National Trust-Bank Charter — and the Crypto Charter War Turns to Litigation

“I'm confident that we have full legal authority to do the things that we're doing. There's nothing wrong with being on the receiving side of a lawsuit.” Jonathan Gould, Comptroller of the Currency — public remarks, 20 May 2026

World Liberty Financial — the Trump-family stablecoin venture — is on the cusp of a federal national trust-bank charter. Former OCC officials told The Block a rejection of WLTC Holdings' application is “inconceivable,” with Comptroller Jonathan Gould expected to rule imminently. The charter would let World Liberty Trust Company, N.A. issue and redeem its USD1 stablecoin, hold reserves, custody assets and run settlement under a single federal regulator — functions BitGo currently performs for USD1.

It would be the most politically charged entry in a charter wave the OCC opened in December 2025, when it conditionally approved trust banks for Circle (First National Digital Currency Bank), Ripple, BitGo, Paxos and Fidelity Digital Assets in a single batch, then added Coinbase National Trust Company on 2 April. At least nine crypto firms now hold or await national trust charters, with Crypto.com (Foris DAX), Stripe's Bridge and others in the queue.

The wave has drawn a two-front assault. The American Bankers Association and Bank Policy Institute are preparing litigation and have pressed the OCC to pause all crypto charter reviews until the GENIUS Act stablecoin rules are finalised (due 18 July). And Senator Elizabeth Warren, in an 18 May letter, accused the OCC of illegally granting charters to nine firms under the National Bank Act, demanding records — including Trump-family communications — by 1 June, a deadline now lapsed without full production.

World Liberty crystallises the conflict-of-interest objection: disclosures show roughly 75% of WLFI token-sale proceeds flow to DT Marks DEFI LLC, a Trump-controlled entity, and the Trump family has reportedly booked more than US$2.3 billion from crypto ventures since January. A June House Financial Services hearing aired those conflicts — and the same fight is now the last obstacle to the CLARITY Act (Story 2).

📎 Historical Precedent: The OCC's 2018–2020 fintech “special-purpose” charter was litigated to a standstill by the NYDFS and the Conference of State Bank Supervisors before the agency retreated — the template for how states fight a federal charter expansion.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Gould's defence is statutory and deliberate: he argues the National Bank Act's “reasonable chance of success” test — not a zero-risk standard — governs, and that trust powers can encompass custody and stablecoin reserve management. The litigation risk is asymmetric: a court loss would void charters mid-stream and strand issuers, while a win entrenches a federal pathway that pre-empts state money-transmitter regimes. The Brief's read: the banks' strongest argument is timing, not merits — forcing the OCC to wait for GENIUS final rules (18 July) would deny crypto firms a first-mover charter advantage; expect a suit filed in a business-friendly circuit within weeks of any World Liberty grant.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Cody Carbone, CEO of the Chamber of Digital Commerce, argues the charters simply execute Congress's own direction — the GENIUS Act expressly contemplates federally-regulated stablecoin issuers, so the OCC is filling a gap rather than inventing authority. The Brief's read: true on stablecoins, but Warren's narrower point bites — staking and lending are not trust activities, and a charter that blesses them stretches the National Bank Act past where any court has taken it.

🏗️ Industry Implications

A clean World Liberty approval validates the trust-charter template for every applicant in the queue — Crypto.com, Stripe's Bridge, Protego, Zerohash — and pressures state-chartered rivals (Anchorage; Paxos's NYDFS trust) toward federal conversion. Circle (CRCL) and Coinbase (COIN) gain a regulatory moat; traditional banks lose the deposit-and-payments perimeter they have defended since Glass-Steagall. The Brief's read: the ABA's lobbying spend signals it views federal crypto charters as an existential channel threat, not merely a prudential one.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish COIN, CRCL and USD1/USDC rails — a federal charter compresses compliance cost and pre-empts 50-state licensing.

🔴 Bearish regional-bank payment franchises and money-transmitter incumbents; a live ABA/BPI suit caps near-term issuance.

⚪ Neutral XRP / Ripple — charter helps RLUSD long-term, but the Trump-crypto optics invite the backlash that could stall the whole wave.

UpdateCLARITY Act's Last Fight Shifts From Stablecoin Yield to Conflict-of-Interest Rules

“This will be something more a matter of focus after the 4th of July recess period, but I certainly hope to see it done before.” Sen. Bill Hagerty (R-TN) — on the CLARITY Act floor timetable, June 2026

The flashpoint blocking the CLARITY Act has moved: with the 1 May deal banning passive stablecoin yield now holding, the last live dispute is a package of conflict-of-interest and ethics provisions that would bar government officials — pointedly, the President's family — from crypto business while in office. The bill has sat on the Senate Legislative Calendar (No. 423) since 1 June with no floor date set.

Nothing else has changed on the count: the bill cleared Senate Banking 15–9 on 14 May with only two Democrats (Gallego, Alsobrooks) aboard, leaving a roughly seven-vote gap to the 60 needed to break a filibuster. The White House has floated a de facto 4 July target; the August recess is the real cliff, beyond which the midterm calendar threatens a 2027 restart before a less favourable Congress.

The yield fight that dominated spring — and cost Circle a fifth of its market cap when the draft leaked — is effectively settled; the ABA's 8,000-letter blitz failed to reopen it. The ethics clause is now the leverage point, and it collides directly with the World Liberty charter (Story 1).

📎 Historical Precedent: Market-structure bills that miss their pre-recess window rarely survive — FIT21 passed the House in 2024 and died in the Senate when no floor time materialised before the calendar ran out.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The ethics provision is doing double duty — it gives wavering Democrats political cover to vote yes (they can claim they curbed Trump-crypto self-dealing) while giving some Republicans a reason to resist (it constrains the President). The Brief's read: per CoinDesk's whip reporting, leadership will likely soften or strip the clause to clear the floor; a bill that dies on ethics optics would be the worst outcome for issuers who have priced in 2026 passage.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Some lobbyists, per Yahoo Finance reporting, argue the calendar math is fatal regardless of the ethics fight — only about eight session weeks remain and crypto competes with appropriations for floor time. The Brief's read: plausible, but a President pushing a 4 July signing has tools to find floor time; handicap passage as a coin-flip, not a long shot.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Passage hands the CFTC primary spot-market jurisdiction over digital commodities and unlocks the “mature blockchain” decentralisation exemption coveted by Coinbase, Kraken and a16z portfolio tokens; failure leaves the March SEC/CFTC joint interpretation as the only rulebook. The Brief's read: US-spot exchanges are the swing beneficiaries; stablecoin issuers are largely covered already by GENIUS.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish US-spot exchanges (COIN, Kraken) and large-cap “commodity” tokens (BTC, ETH) that gain CFTC clarity on passage.

🔴 Bearish altcoins reliant on the SEC's narrower exemptions if the bill dies and regulation-by-interpretation persists.

⚪ Neutral stablecoins — GENIUS already governs them and CLARITY's yield ban is priced in.

UpdateAustralia's AFSL Cliff Is 8 Days Out — OSL Becomes a Flagship Regulated Grant Before the 30 June Lodge-or-Breach Deadline

“At OSL, we're building the regulated rails to unlock a unified, borderless financial flow… Securing our AFSL demonstrates our commitment to Australia and our many enterprise partners who rely on us to move value safely across borders.” Kevin Cui, CEO, OSL Group — 19 June 2026

ASIC granted OSL Group's Australian entity an Australian Financial Services Licence on 19 June, authorising payment and custody services and OTC dealing for wholesale clients — a rare full-fat regulated approval landing days before the country's 30 June AFSL “cliff.” The licence builds on OSL's January acquisition of Melbourne-founded payments firm Banxa and its USDGO enterprise stablecoin.

The cliff itself is unchanged and now eight days away: ASIC's INFO 225 class no-action position expires 30 June, and any crypto business operating in Australia on or before 31 December 2025 must lodge a complete AFSL application by that date or risk operating in breach of the Corporations Act from 1 July. This is the end of forbearance under existing law — separate from, and 18 months ahead of, the Digital Assets Framework Act (Royal Assent 8 April; commences 9 April 2027).

📎 Historical Precedent: Australia's 2018 AUSTRAC digital-currency-exchange registration sweep set the pattern — a hard date forced a filing rush, then a long tail of enforcement against the laggards who missed it.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The distinction to hold clients to: lodging a complete application by 30 June preserves no-action protection; an incomplete or late lodgement does not, exposing directors to personal liability for unlicensed financial-services conduct. The Brief's read: ASIC will likely extend forbearance to firms with a genuine pending application and come down hard on those who ignored the date — the OSL grant is the carrot, enforcement is the stick.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Some argue the cliff is overstated because ASIC retains discretion and is unlikely to act on day one. The Brief's read: discretion is not a defence — the no-action position is the only shield, and it evaporates 30 June; relying on regulatory mercy is not a compliance strategy.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Institutional custodians and payments players — OSL plus AFSL-holding incumbents — gain a moat as unlicensed retail platforms face exit or wind-down; expect consolidation as smaller exchanges sell client books to licence-holders. The Brief's read: the USDGO/Banxa stack positions OSL as an APAC stablecoin-payments consolidator, mirroring the MiCA-driven consolidation underway in Europe (Story 4).

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish regulated APAC custodians and payments (OSL Group / 0863.HK) and AFSL-holding exchanges that absorb displaced clients.

🔴 Bearish unlicensed Australian retail platforms facing 1 July breach exposure and forced wind-down.

⚪ Neutral global majors (Coinbase, Kraken AU) already lodged — this is clarity, not a fresh catalyst.

UpdateMiCA T-Minus 9: France Threatens Criminal Penalties as 60% of EU Users Stay on Unlicensed Venues

“After [1 July 2026], any entity providing crypto-asset services to EU clients without a MiCA licence will be in breach of EU law and must cease offering such services.” ESMA — statement on the end of MiCA transitional periods

With the 1 July transitional cutoff nine days away, France's AMF has sharpened the stakes, warning that unauthorised crypto activity from 1 July could constitute a criminal offence carrying up to two years' imprisonment and €30,000 in fines — on top of MiCA's administrative penalties of up to €5 million or 5% of turnover. The escalation lands as roughly 204 CASPs hold full authorisation against more than 3,000 pre-MiCA providers.

The demand side is the real story: an OKX Europe analysis found around 60% of European users still transact on unlicensed exchanges, with 7.6 million of 18.5 million app downloads over the past year going to non-compliant platforms — meaning the cutoff will strand a majority of users mid-stream unless their venues exit or migrate them in time.

📎 Historical Precedent: The pattern echoes the 2020 5AMLD VASP registration deadline, when a hard date produced a filing rush, a wave of EU market exits, and a long tail of national-regulator enforcement.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The criminal overlay matters because it shifts exposure from the entity to named executives and removes the “wind-down quietly” option — ESMA expects credible, immediately-executable exit or client-offboarding plans in place by 1 July. The Brief's read: expect a cluster of national-regulator cease-and-desist orders in the first fortnight of July, with France and the smaller markets most aggressive.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Licensed majors (Coinbase, Kraken, Bitstamp, OKX Europe) absorb displaced users while unlicensed venues either exit the EU or fire-sale client books — the same consolidation Australia faces 24 hours earlier (Story 3). The Brief's read: the user-migration friction is a one-time land-grab for CASP-licensed platforms.

UpdateEU Parliament Adopts the Digital Omnibus 423–57, Locking In a 16-Month Deferral of the AI Act's High-Risk Regime

“[paraphrased] Parliament voted to postpone application of parts of the AI Act to ensure the necessary standards and support measures are in place before high-risk obligations bite.” European Parliament — Digital Omnibus on AI, adopted 16 June 2026 [paraphrased]

The European Parliament on 16 June adopted the Digital Omnibus on AI by 423 votes to 57 (174 abstentions), formally postponing the AI Act's high-risk obligations — Annex III standalone systems to 2 December 2027, and Annex I product-embedded systems to 2 August 2028. The deferral guts the 2 August 2026 high-risk cliff practitioners had been racing toward, though that date stays legally live until the Council formally signs off.

The Omnibus is not pure deregulation: it adds an EU-wide ban on “nudifier” deepfake apps (non-consensual intimate-image generators), with a 2 December 2026 compliance date, and preserves the requirement that every member state stand up an AI regulatory sandbox by 2 August 2026.

📎 Historical Precedent: It mirrors GDPR's rollout, where headline obligations were softened and staggered under industry pressure once the real compliance cost became clear.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The practical effect for providers is breathing room — high-risk conformity assessments, logging and human-oversight build-outs can stretch into 2027–28 — but the legal trap is the gap: 2 August 2026 remains binding until the Council adopts, so compliance teams cannot stand down yet. The Brief's read: build to the original August date with a deferral contingency; civil-society groups (liberties.eu) warn the Omnibus weakens fundamental-rights protections, which could invite a legal challenge.

🏗️ Industry Implications

US and EU AI developers — the foundation-model labs, plus high-risk deployers in HR, biometrics and credit — get a reprieve that lowers near-term compliance spend; the nudifier ban forces a discrete app category off the EU market by December. The Brief's read: the deferral is a competitiveness signal — Brussels blinking on its flagship AI law to keep pace with a lighter-touch US and UK.
