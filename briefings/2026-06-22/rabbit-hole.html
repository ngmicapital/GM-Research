Every physics textbook teaches the Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse wrong

November 7, 1940 — Tacoma, Washington — a $6.4M bridge, a cocker spaniel, and an 80-year textbook error

Every physics textbook teaches the Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse wrong. The Federal Works Administration investigators explicitly rejected resonance as the cause — the real mechanism, aeroelastic flutter, is a self-amplifying feedback loop where the bridge’s own twisting recruited energy from the wind, and it’s the same phenomenon that kills airplanes.

TL;DR

On November 7, 1940, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge — nicknamed “Galloping Gertie” for its wind-induced undulations — twisted itself apart in a 40 mph breeze just four months after opening. The footage became the most-watched engineering failure in history and lodged itself in physics classrooms worldwide as the definitive example of resonance. The problem: the engineers who actually investigated the collapse explicitly said resonance had nothing to do with it. The real cause, aeroelastic flutter, is a fundamentally different phenomenon — one that also destroys aircraft, and one that a generation of engineers had to learn to fear from scratch.

Section 01

The Story

By any measure, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge was a triumph. When it opened on July 1, 1940, its 2,800-foot main span made it the third-longest suspension bridge in the world. Its designer, Leon Moisseiff, was one of the most celebrated engineers in America — the man who had refined the mathematics of the Golden Gate Bridge and convinced the engineering establishment that slender, flexible suspension bridges were not just economical but beautiful. The Tacoma Narrows was the apotheosis of his theory: a deck so thin and graceful it looked like a ribbon across the water.

Almost immediately, it started moving. From the first week, the deck undulated in any stiff breeze, rising and falling in slow vertical waves that could lift a car several feet off the road. Workers on the bridge reported nausea. Drivers loved it. By summer, the locals had given it an affectionate nickname — Galloping Gertie — and people were driving out from Tacoma specifically for the experience of bouncing along a living bridge. The state toll authority quietly hired engineers to investigate, but the consensus was that the oscillations, while unsettling, were within acceptable limits. The bridge had survived the worst winds of its first season. It seemed like it would be fine.

The Last Drive

On the morning of November 7, 1940, Leonard Coatsworth — an editor at the Tacoma News Tribune — drove onto the bridge at around 10:00 a.m. When the deck began twisting violently, he abandoned his car and crawled on his hands and knees to safety across a heaving road surface that tilted 45 degrees. Left behind in the car was his daughter’s cocker spaniel, Tubby. Multiple rescuers attempted to retrieve the dog during the two hours the bridge remained standing. Tubby bit every one of them and went down with the car. He is the only confirmed casualty of the collapse. Coatsworth was later reimbursed $814.40 for the car and its contents.

The bridge collapsed at 11:02 a.m. in front of a small crowd and a single 16mm camera operated by a local amateur, Barney Elliott. The footage — grainy, slightly over-exposed, absolutely riveting — captured the deck twisting in full torsional oscillation, one side rising as the other fell, before chunks of concrete began shedding into the Narrows and the center span tore loose and plunged into the water 190 feet below. The Federal Works Agency commissioned an investigation. Three engineers were appointed: Othmar Ammann, who had built the George Washington Bridge; Glenn Woodruff; and Theodore von Kármán, director of the Guggenheim Aeronautical Laboratory and the foremost aerodynamicist alive. Their conclusions would be unambiguous — and would be largely ignored by physics educators for the next fifty years.

It is very improbable that resonance with alternating vortices played an important role in the oscillations observed. — Federal Works Administration Final Report, 1941
Section 02

The Mechanism

The resonance story is clean, and that is why it survived. In the textbook version, wind blowing past the bridge created periodic vortices — little whirlpools shed alternately from each side of the deck — at a frequency that happened to match the bridge’s natural oscillation frequency. Like pushing a child on a swing at exactly the right moment, the wind kept pumping energy into the bridge at just the right rate, and the amplitude grew until the structure failed. It is an elegant, satisfying explanation, and it is essentially wrong.

The problem is this: vortex shedding frequency depends on wind speed. For resonance to work, the wind would need to maintain a precise and constant speed that matches the bridge’s natural frequency exactly — and maintain it for hours while the bridge amplitude grew. Real wind does not do this. More importantly, the investigators found that the vortex shedding in the Tacoma collapse was not independent of the bridge’s motion. It was controlled by it. As the bridge twisted, the motion itself shaped the airflow around the deck, causing vortices to be shed in perfect synchrony with the bridge’s oscillation — not because the wind happened to match, but because the bridge was generating the pattern that fed it.

Aeroelastic Flutter: The Core Mechanism

The Tacoma Narrows deck was a solid plate girder — 8 feet deep, flat-sided, acting aerodynamically like an airplane wing. When the deck twisted slightly, one edge rose and the other fell. A twisted surface generates lift. But the lift acted to amplify the twist rather than correct it, because the aerodynamic center and the elastic center of the deck were misaligned. Each twist pulled more energy from the wind, increasing the twist further. This is negative damping: instead of the oscillation dying out over time, it grew with every cycle. Above a critical wind speed of roughly 35 mph, there was no stable equilibrium. The oscillations would grow without bound until the structure failed or the wind dropped.

The key distinction is one of direction. In forced resonance, energy is supplied by an external periodic force at a fixed frequency. In aeroelastic flutter, the structure itself controls the aerodynamic forces — its own motion determines when and how energy transfers from the wind. This means flutter is not sensitive to a specific wind frequency. It is sensitive to wind speed: once the threshold is crossed, any sustained wind above that speed will drive the oscillation to destruction. The bridge did not need a peculiarly tuned wind. It needed a moderately windy day.

This is exactly what happened. The 40 mph winds on November 7, 1940 were not extreme by Pacific Northwest standards. They were barely above the flutter threshold. But once the torsional mode was triggered, the bridge had no mechanism to dissipate the energy. Moisseiff’s design — so admired for its graceful slenderness — had removed the open deck trusses that would have let wind pass through the structure. The solid plate girders acted as an airfoil. The bridge was, in aerodynamic terms, a wing. And like a wing that has exceeded its flutter boundary, it destroyed itself.

Section 03

The Connections

The same phenomenon kills airplanes. Aeroelastic flutter is one of the most feared failure modes in aviation. Wings flex. When a wing flexes upward, the aerodynamic forces change; if those changes amplify the flex rather than dampen it, the wing enters flutter and can tear off within seconds. Every commercial aircraft undergoes rigorous flutter testing before certification, often involving test pilots deliberately pushing planes toward their flutter boundary. The Tacoma Narrows collapse, read correctly, is not a story about bridges and wind — it is an early lesson in the same aerodynamic instability that aerospace engineers spend careers designing around.

The textbook error has been formally documented — and still persists. In 1991, K. Yusuf Billah and Robert H. Scanlan published a paper in the American Journal of Physics titled “Resonance, Tacoma Narrows bridge failure, and undergraduate physics textbooks.” They surveyed the major physics texts of the era and found the resonance explanation in almost every one. They walked through the technical errors carefully and concluded that the story being taught was not just incomplete but mechanistically false. The paper was well-received in engineering circles. Physics textbooks largely continued using the resonance story anyway, because it fits so neatly into chapters on oscillation and wave mechanics. As of the early 2020s, versions of the myth still appear in widely adopted curricula.

Moisseiff’s tragedy is in the details. The investigation board technically exonerated Moisseiff — his deflection theory was mathematically sound, and other engineers had approved the design. But the implicit verdict was clear: his philosophy of extreme slenderness had deleted the margin of safety that would have prevented flutter. After November 7, 1940, commissions stopped arriving. He died in 1943, three years after the collapse, largely professionally isolated. The man who contributed to the Golden Gate Bridge ended his career as the cautionary footnote to a 16mm film screened in first-year physics courses.

The Narrows changed how every subsequent large bridge was designed. Wind tunnel testing of bridge deck sections became standard practice almost immediately after the collapse. Engineers stopped designing solid plate decks and returned to open trusses, or developed new aerodynamic fairings that would allow air to pass through or around the deck cleanly without generating lift. The Severn Bridge in England (1966), the Humber Bridge (1981), and ultimately the Millau Viaduct (2004) were all shaped in wind tunnels before a single piece of steel was ordered. The aerodynamics of the deck is now as fundamental a design input as the tensile strength of the cables.

Von Kármán’s Contribution

Theodore von Kármán identified the correct flutter mechanism in the investigation’s aftermath, drawing on his expertise in aerodynamics to explain what the bridge’s own motion had done to the airflow around it. His name is already attached to “Kármán vortex streets” — the periodic vortex shedding behind blunt objects in flow, which was initially suspected as the cause of the Tacoma collapse. Von Kármán himself argued that the vortex street mechanism, while real, was not the primary driver, and that the self-exciting torsional flutter was the actual failure mode. His analysis laid the groundwork for bridge aerodynamics as a formal engineering discipline.

The film is doing something insidious. Because the collapse is so spectacular and so well-documented, it has become pedagogically irresistible. The footage puts a concrete image to an abstract concept — resonance — that is otherwise hard to visualize. The fact that the image is attached to the wrong concept has not diminished its utility as a teaching tool; if anything, the vividness of the footage makes it harder to dislodge the misconception it creates. This is a general problem with memorable examples in science education: the story sticks, and if the story is wrong, the error sticks too.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

✈
Aircraft Flutter Testing

Before any commercial aircraft is certified, it must be pushed to its flutter boundary — sometimes with test pilots aboard. Engineers add flutter suppression systems and stiffen control surfaces precisely because the same feedback loop that destroyed the Tacoma bridge also destroys wings.

🋔
Kármán Vortex Streets

The periodic shedding of vortices behind a blunt object — named for Theodore von Kármán — is real and visible in everything from chimney stacks to satellite images of clouds around islands. It is simply not what destroyed the Tacoma Narrows Bridge.

📑
Billah & Scanlan, 1991

The paper that formally debunked the textbook story: “Resonance, Tacoma Narrows bridge failure, and undergraduate physics textbooks,” American Journal of Physics 59(2), 1991. A rare case of scientists formally auditing what their discipline was teaching — and finding it wrong.

🎉
The Deflection Theory

Moisseiff’s innovation was allowing bridge decks to flex with wind loads rather than rigidly resisting them. Sound in principle — it reduced material costs significantly — but the Tacoma design applied it past the point where aerodynamic stability had been adequately checked.

💨
Millennium Bridge, London

In 2000, London’s Millennium pedestrian bridge was closed two days after opening because crowds walking across it caused lateral oscillations — a different aeroelastic-style feedback loop between human gait and bridge sway. It was fixed with dampers. Galloping Gertie’s lesson, relearned.
