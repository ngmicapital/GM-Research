In 1859 the Sun Fired a Storm So Powerful That Telegraph Operators Unplugged Their Batteries — and the Messages Still Went Through

On 1 September 1859, a coronal mass ejection reached Earth in 17.6 hours — a journey that normally takes several days. It sparked fires in telegraph offices, gave operators electric shocks, and produced auroras visible from Cuba to Santiago. Then a pair of operators in Boston and Maine discovered they could disconnect their batteries entirely and hold a two-hour conversation using only the current induced by the sky. It remains the most powerful solar storm in 500 years of recorded history. If it happened today, it would be the largest economic disaster in human history.

In 1859, a solar storm so powerful that telegraph operators ran their lines without batteries for two hours struck Earth — and ice core evidence shows it was twice as large as any solar storm in the past 500 years. If a Carrington-scale event hit today, it could knock out power to 40 million people for up to two years and cause up to $2.6 trillion in US economic damage alone, and the high-voltage transformers it would destroy take four years to manufacture.

TL;DR

On the morning of 1 September 1859, amateur astronomer Richard Carrington was sketching sunspots in his private observatory when two patches of intensely bright light erupted from the surface of the Sun — the first solar flare ever observed and recorded. Seventeen hours and thirty-six minutes later, a wall of magnetised plasma struck Earth's magnetic field. For the next two days, telegraph systems across North America and Europe failed, sparked, and caught fire. Operators received electric shocks. Gold miners in the Rocky Mountains woke at 1 a.m. convinced it was dawn. People in the northeastern United States read newspapers by the light of the aurora. And in Boston, two telegraph operators discovered they could unplug their batteries entirely and still exchange messages — using only the electric current flowing from the sky. The event, now called the Carrington Event, remains the most powerful geomagnetic storm in recorded history. Ice core analysis places it at roughly twice the magnitude of any other storm in 500 years. The technology it disrupted in 1859 was simple enough to recover in days. The technology it would disrupt today is not.

Section 01

The Story

Richard Carrington was a wealthy English amateur astronomer — the son of a brewer, financially independent, serious enough about science to build a private observatory at his estate in Redhill, Surrey. In the 1850s, he was compiling a systematic catalogue of sunspots, projecting the Sun's image through his brass telescope onto a sheet of paper and tracing what he saw each day. It was painstaking work, but it produced the kind of long-run data that professional observatories rarely bothered to collect.

Just before noon on 1 September 1859, something he had never seen before interrupted his routine. As he later described it: two patches of "intensely bright and white light" burst from a cluster of sunspots he had been watching. They were brighter than the photosphere around them, moving rapidly. He ran to fetch a witness, but by the time he returned the lights had already begun to fade. The whole display lasted perhaps five minutes. He had seen a solar flare — the first one ever recorded. His colleague Richard Hodgson, observing independently at his own home, had seen the same thing.

Carrington suspected the event was connected to disturbances he had been hearing about from magnetic observatories — there had been unusual readings at Kew in London. But he was cautious. He wrote that "one swallow does not make a summer," refusing to assert a causal link between a solar event and disturbances on Earth without more evidence. That caution turned out to be scientifically correct — the connection between solar activity and geomagnetic disturbance was not firmly established for decades — but the data he was accumulating would ultimately prove it.

Seventeen hours and thirty-six minutes after the flare, a wall of magnetised plasma travelling at roughly 2,400 kilometres per second struck Earth's magnetosphere. A typical coronal mass ejection takes three to four days to make the same journey. This one had been accelerated by a prior CME days earlier that had already swept the path clear.

What followed was the most extreme geomagnetic storm in recorded history. Telegraph systems across North America and Europe collapsed simultaneously. Current surged through the lines unprompted, overloading equipment. Operators received electric shocks from their instruments. In some stations, the current was powerful enough to ignite the paper tape used to record incoming messages. At telegraph offices in Washington, D.C., operators reported flames leaping from their switchboards. Some stations were simply abandoned.

The auroras were unlike anything documented before or since. The northern lights — normally a high-latitude phenomenon — were seen as far south as Cuba, Honolulu, and the Bahamas. The southern lights reached as far north as Santiago, Chile, and Queensland, Australia. Across the Rocky Mountains, the display was so brilliant that gold prospectors woke at one in the morning, mistook the aurora for sunrise, and began making breakfast. In the northeastern United States, newspapers reported people reading by the light of the sky.

Then came the strangest part. When American Telegraph Company employees arrived at their Boston office that morning, they found it impossible to transmit or receive anything in the normal way. The storm's induced current was overwhelming their batteries. But as they attempted to work around the problem, they discovered something extraordinary: if they disconnected the batteries entirely, there was still current in the wire. The sky itself was supplying enough electricity to run the line. Two operators — one in Boston, one in Portland, Maine — held a conversation for approximately two hours using no battery power whatsoever, driven entirely by the auroral current flowing through the Earth. It was the first time more than a word or two had been transmitted this way.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The Sun is not a stable object. Its outer layer — the corona — is a plasma of charged particles at temperatures reaching several million degrees, and its magnetic field is constantly twisting, snapping, and reconfiguring. When magnetic field lines in the corona cross and reconnect, they release enormous amounts of energy suddenly: a solar flare. If the reconnection is large enough, it can also eject a mass of magnetised plasma into space — a coronal mass ejection, or CME. A large CME can contain up to ten billion tonnes of material travelling at speeds between 500 and 3,000 kilometres per second.

When a CME reaches Earth, it collides with the magnetosphere — the invisible magnetic bubble that normally shields the planet from the solar wind. The interaction compresses the sunward side of the magnetosphere and stretches the night-side into a long tail. If the CME's magnetic field is oriented southward (opposite to Earth's northward field), the two fields partially cancel and reconnect at the boundary, flooding the magnetosphere with energetic particles. This is a geomagnetic storm.

The critical physics for infrastructure is Faraday's law: a changing magnetic field induces an electric current in any conducting loop. The Earth's surface and crust form one half of such a loop; the long metal lines of telegraph wires (and, today, power transmission lines, pipelines, and submarine cables) form the other. When the magnetic field changes rapidly during a severe storm, geomagnetically induced currents — GICs — flow through every conductive structure on the surface. In 1859, those structures were simple enough that the induced current was merely a nuisance: it disrupted signals, shocked operators, and started some fires. In the modern electrical grid, the same current would flow through transformers.

Why Transformers Are the Critical Vulnerability

High-voltage transformers are the backbone of long-distance power transmission. They are also uniquely vulnerable to GICs. Unlike consumer electronics — which have their own circuit protection and fail gracefully — large grid transformers are designed to handle alternating current at 50–60 cycles per second. A GIC is effectively direct current, which can saturate the transformer core, cause extreme overheating, and lead to permanent failure of equipment that cannot be rapidly replaced. Large high-voltage transformers are custom-manufactured, weigh hundreds of tonnes, and the current global supply chain for them has lead times of roughly four years per unit. A Carrington-scale storm that damaged dozens or hundreds of these transformers simultaneously would create a power restoration problem with no precedent.

The Carrington Event of 1859 is the benchmark because it remains the largest solar storm in the modern observational record — and ice core data from Greenland and Antarctica, which preserve nitrate deposits from historical geomagnetic events, confirm that it was roughly twice as energetic as any other storm in the past five centuries. A 2013 study by Lloyd's of London and Atmospheric and Environmental Research estimated that a repeat event would leave between 20 and 40 million Americans without power for between 16 days and two years, with US economic losses ranging from $600 billion to $2.6 trillion in 2013 dollars. Global losses would be substantially higher.

There is one complicating factor that makes the 1859 event especially instructive. It was not a single CME but two, in quick succession. A large CME had erupted on 28 August and preceded the main event by approximately two days. The first CME partially cleared the interplanetary medium of solar wind particles, reducing the drag on the second. This is why the 1 September CME made the 150-million-kilometre journey in 17.6 hours instead of the usual three to four days. The faster it travels, the harder it hits. The double-CME structure is not unusual — and it is exactly the kind of sequence that would give the least warning time of arrival.

Section 03

The Connections

The 1989 Quebec storm — a rehearsal nobody studied hard enough. On 13 March 1989, a geomagnetic storm far less powerful than Carrington-level collapsed the entire Hydro-Québec power grid in ninety seconds. Six million Canadians lost power for up to nine hours. The northeastern United States came close to a cascade failure. Transformers were damaged as far away as New Jersey. The storm also permanently damaged a transformer at a nuclear plant in New Hampshire. This was a moderate geomagnetic storm — Dst index around -589 nT compared to estimates of -850 nT or lower for 1859. The infrastructure of 1989 was already modern enough to be deeply vulnerable. The infrastructure of today, with far more high-voltage direct-current interconnections and longer transmission lines, is more so.

The near-miss of July 2012. On 23 July 2012, a CME of approximately Carrington-scale magnitude erupted from the Sun and travelled through space at roughly 2,500 kilometres per second. It missed Earth by nine days — the planet had been in that region of its orbit nine days earlier. A 2014 paper by physicist Daniel Baker and colleagues estimated that if the CME had launched one week earlier, it would have hit Earth with full force and almost certainly caused damage comparable to the 1859 event. The near-miss attracted brief press attention and then largely disappeared from public discussion. No significant new infrastructure protection measures were mandated in its wake.

The warning window and what it buys. Between detecting a CME departing the Sun and its arrival at Earth, the warning time is between 17 hours and four days, depending on speed. The L1 Lagrange point — roughly 1.5 million kilometres from Earth, where the Deep Space Climate Observatory satellite sits — provides approximately 15–60 minutes of final warning once the CME arrives there. In theory, this window is long enough to take protective action: power grid operators can reduce load, disconnect vulnerable transformers, ground aircraft at risk from radiation exposure, and put satellites into safe mode. In practice, the economic cost of acting on a geomagnetic storm warning that turns out to be less severe than predicted — taking a grid offline costs money, airline groundings cost money — means that operators are reluctant to act without very high confidence in the forecast. Solar storm forecasting is not yet good enough to justify that confidence consistently.

Solar Cycle 25 and the current risk window. The Sun follows an approximately eleven-year cycle of magnetic activity, from solar minimum (few sunspots, fewer CMEs) to solar maximum (intense activity). Solar Cycle 25 reached its peak in 2025 and is now in the early declining phase — historically, large geomagnetic events can occur both at and after the maximum, as the Sun's magnetic field begins to reorganise. The May 2024 storm — the strongest in two decades — produced auroras visible across central Europe and the continental United States but caused no major infrastructure damage. It served as a reminder that the current cycle is active. It was not Carrington-scale. It was, in geomagnetic terms, a tap on the shoulder.

Why the Boston-Portland conversation matters philosophically. The telegraph operators who disconnected their batteries and kept talking were not conducting an experiment. They were doing their jobs under unusual conditions and adapting practically to what they found. But the moment encapsulates something important about the Carrington Event's relationship to technology: the storm did not simply break the existing system. It revealed what the system was actually made of, and showed that the boundary between the infrastructure and the natural world it was embedded in was thinner than assumed. Every major electrical system since — the power grid, satellite communications, GPS, the internet's submarine cable network — rests on infrastructure that makes the same implicit assumption: the electromagnetic environment will stay within normal parameters. The Carrington Event is the record of the one time it decisively did not, and it predates by sixty years the systems that would be most affected by a repeat.

Further Down the Hole

🔭
Richard Carrington — The Amateur Who Made the Discovery
Carrington's 1863 book Observations of the Spots of the Sun documents his sunspot cataloguing work and the events of 1 September 1859. He is one of the relatively rare cases of an amateur scientist making a fundamental discovery — his systematic, long-run observations were exactly the kind of data that professional observatories of his era were too busy (and too funded for specific projects) to collect. He died in 1875 at 49; the solar-terrestrial connection he hesitated to claim was not firmly established until the twentieth century.
⚡
The 1989 Quebec Blackout — The Modern Preview
The North American Aerospace Defense Command's incident report on the March 1989 storm is publicly available. The collapse of the Hydro-Québec grid in 90 seconds from a storm roughly half the estimated intensity of 1859 gives a baseline for what modern infrastructure looks like under geomagnetic stress. The NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center has archived geomagnetic index data from 1989 onward; the Dst index for March 1989 reached approximately -589 nT. The estimated Dst for 1859 ranges from -850 nT to -1,760 nT in different reconstructions.
🌌
The July 2012 Near-Miss
Baker et al. (2013), "A major solar eruptive event in July 2012: Defining extreme space weather scenarios," in Space Weather, is the primary paper. It reconstructs the July 23, 2012 CME using data from the STEREO-A spacecraft, which was in the CME's direct path and survived. The storm's speed (roughly 2,500 km/s), its magnetic field orientation (southward — the worst case for Earth), and its duration would, the authors conclude, have produced a storm comparable to 1859 had it struck Earth. The paper estimated the probability of a Carrington-scale event hitting Earth within a decade at approximately 12 percent.
🏭
The Transformer Supply Chain Problem
The Electric Power Research Institute and the Department of Energy have both published analyses of high-voltage transformer vulnerability. The key constraint is that large extra-high-voltage transformers (345 kV and above) are manufactured by a small number of companies worldwide, weigh up to 400 tonnes, require specialist shipping, and have lead times of 12 to 18 months under normal conditions — rising to years in a scenario with simultaneous demand from multiple utilities. A 2019 EIS Council report identified approximately 2,000 such transformers in the US grid, of which 300–500 could be at serious risk in a Carrington-scale event.
🧊
Ice Core Evidence and the 775 AD Miyake Event
The JSTOR Daily piece on "Miyake Events" is a useful entry point. In 2012, physicist Fusa Miyake identified a massive spike in radiocarbon (C-14) in tree ring records from 775 AD — roughly ten times larger than any solar event in the modern record. The mechanism is not fully understood but is thought to involve an extraordinarily powerful solar particle event. A Miyake-scale event would dwarf the Carrington Event and has no modern analogue for comparison. Ice core nitrate records and cosmogenic isotope studies have become the primary tools for reconstructing pre-instrumental solar history.
