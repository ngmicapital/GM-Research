Driving Thesis

Three high-quality findings reshape the longevity and supplementation model this cycle. UCL's analysis of 3,556 adults across seven epigenetic clocks shows that arts engagement — concerts, theatre, museums, or making art — slows biological aging at the same 4% rate as physical activity, with a clear dose-response from three engagements per year up to weekly participation as measured via the DunedinPACE clock. This is not a soft wellness signal: for those running serious training protocols, arts participation appears additive to exercise, operating through overlapping but distinct mechanisms both centered on reducing allostatic load and inflammaging. The COSMOS trial (Nature Medicine, 958 adults, 2-year RCT) delivers the first large-scale placebo-controlled evidence that a standard daily multivitamin slows epigenetic aging by approximately four months over two years — with the effect concentrated in people already aging faster than their chronological age, and with cocoa extract showing no effect on any clock, making the multivitamin signal more credible by contrast. In nutrition, a 2,192-person Australian cross-sectional study formalizes the ultra-processed food attention and dementia risk link: every 10% increase in UPF intake drops attention performance and raises CAIDE dementia risk scores independently of overall diet quality, which means food processing itself — not just nutrient displacement — is driving the harm signal.

Longevity & Performance Science

The UCL arts engagement finding is genuinely surprising — not because the direction is unexpected, but because the effect size matches physical activity and is dose-dependent across seven epigenetic clocks. The DunedinPACE clock is the most relevant: it measures the pace of aging rather than biological age at a point in time, making it more sensitive to behavioral interventions. Three arts engagements per year is a very low bar for a measurable signal. The mechanism is inferred (arts engagement → stress reduction → cortisol normalisation → reduced inflammatory load → epigenetic deceleration), but is consistent with what is known about allostatic load, HRV, and chronic inflammation as aging accelerants. Both "making" and "attending" show the effect, which argues against this being just an exercise proxy for dance.

The Australian ultra-processed food cognitive study is the other side of the same mechanistic coin. The critical methodological step is the adjustment for overall diet quality: the UPF harm signal survives. This means it's not simply that UPFs displace good nutrients — the industrial processing itself (emulsifiers, additives, advanced glycation end products, modified starches) appears to drive a separate harm pathway on cognitive function and dementia risk, likely through gut microbiome disruption and neuroinflammatory signaling at the blood-brain barrier.

Arts Engagement Slows Biological Aging at the Same Rate as Physical Activity — UCL Analysis of 3,556 Adults Across Seven Epigenetic Clocks
[Observational]

Researchers at University College London analyzed biological age data from 3,556 UK adults whose blood samples were used to measure aging across seven epigenetic clocks — including DunedinPACE, the clock most sensitive to the current pace of aging rather than cumulative biological age at a point in time. Participants reported frequency of arts and cultural engagement across two dimensions: attending (concerts, theatre, galleries, museums) and making (dancing, singing, visual art, crafts). The study was published in Innovation in Ageing (2026).

The headline result: regular arts engagement was associated with 4% slower aging on the DunedinPACE clock — approximately one biological year of difference — matching the magnitude seen for physical activity in the same dataset. The relationship is dose-dependent: three or more engagements per year was linked to 2% slower aging, monthly engagement to 3% slower aging, and weekly engagement to 4% slower aging. Both attending and making showed the effect — this is not an exercise proxy via dancing.

The proposed mechanism centers on chronic stress reduction and its downstream effects on inflammation. Arts engagement is documented to lower cortisol, improve heart rate variability, and activate parasympathetic nervous system states. These are the same pathways through which social connection is a robust longevity predictor — and arts participation is a reliably parasympathetic, socially embedded activity. The study authors are explicit that the design is observational and causality isn't established; higher SES and healthier baseline lifestyles in arts participants were partially controlled but not fully eliminated.

For dc's context: the arts effect appears genuinely additive to training rather than a proxy for it. Physical activity → mitochondrial biogenesis, aerobic capacity, and mechanical stress-mediated adaptation. Arts engagement → allostatic load reduction and cortisol management. These are complementary, not competing, pathways. Live music, gallery visits, or active creative practice now have a quantifiable longevity case at a number comparable to the PPL program itself.

Source: UCL News · May 2026 · Innovation in Ageing, 2026 · University College London
One Daily Ultra-Processed Food Serving Measurably Drops Attention and Raises Dementia Risk Score in Mid-Life Australians — Processing Harm Survives Diet-Quality Adjustment
[Observational]

A cross-sectional study published in Alzheimer's & Dementia: Diagnosis, Assessment & Disease Monitoring (Cardoso et al., 2026, DOI: 10.1002/dad2.70335) assessed 2,192 dementia-free Australian adults aged 40–70, using dietary recall data against two validated instruments: the Cogstate Brief Battery (cognitive function across attention, processing speed, learning, and working memory) and the CAIDE dementia risk score (a validated 10-year dementia risk estimator for midlife populations).

The key finding: every 10% increase in the proportion of daily diet from ultra-processed foods — roughly equivalent to adding one packet of chips — was associated with measurable drops in attention performance and higher CAIDE dementia risk scores. The researchers classified foods using the NOVA system (ultra-processed = industrially formulated products containing additives not used in home cooking: emulsifiers, preservatives, artificial flavors, modified starches, colorings). The dose-response was consistent across the range of intake observed in the cohort.

The methodologically critical result: the association persisted after adjusting for overall diet quality. This means food processing itself — not just the displacement of whole foods and their nutrients — appears to drive the cognitive risk signal. The candidate mechanisms include gut microbiome disruption via emulsifiers, neuroinflammatory effects of advanced glycation end products formed during high-temperature industrial processing, and additives affecting blood-brain barrier permeability. The cross-sectional design cannot establish causation, and effect sizes at the individual level are modest — but the adjustment for diet quality is the step that makes this finding more actionable than previous correlational UPF research.

Source: Alzheimer's & Dementia: DADM, 2026 · Cardoso et al. · Australian cohort · 2,192 adults aged 40–70
⚡ Implication: Arts engagement is now a legitimate longevity protocol with the same epigenetic magnitude as exercise — additive to training, not a replacement. Even three concerts or gallery visits per year produce a measurable signal; weekly engagement doubles it. For UPF: the processing harm survives diet-quality adjustment, which means hitting your protein and micronutrient targets via processed food isn't the same as hitting them via whole food. UPF exposure is an independent lever, not compensable by macros.
Section 02
Training & Performance

Edith Cowan University's eccentric exercise research program has been building a consistent body of evidence for years — this 2026 Journal of Sport and Health Science study is the clearest articulation yet. The finding isn't that eccentric work is universally superior to concentric for hypertrophy (the evidence on that is more mixed), but that eccentric-dominant training delivers meaningful strength and muscle gains at substantially lower cardiovascular load, lower effort perception, and lower time and equipment requirements than traditional volume-based training. For PPL practitioners, this is most relevant as a complement to compound bilateral loading: tacking a deliberate 3–4 second eccentric phase onto existing lifts, or using eccentric-dominant finishers on travel and recovery days. The five-minutes-per-day claim is legitimate for maintenance and untrained populations — it's not a rationalization for skipping full sessions in a trained athlete context.

Five Minutes of Daily Eccentric Exercise Produces Meaningful Strength Gains — ECU Formalises the Minimal-Dose Training Evidence
[Research Review]

A study published in the Journal of Sport and Health Science (Volume 15, Article 101126, 2026) from Edith Cowan University by Professor Ken Nosaka — one of the leading researchers in eccentric exercise physiology globally — consolidates the evidence that improvements in muscle size, strength, and performance do not require high-volume or high-effort training, provided the training stimulus emphasises the eccentric (muscle-lengthening) phase.

The mechanistic basis: muscles generate greater force during lengthening movements than during shortening, while consuming less energy. This means eccentric-focused training accumulates more mechanical tension — the primary driver of hypertrophy and strength adaptation — with less metabolic cost per unit of stimulus. Even five minutes per day of simple eccentric movements (chair squats with a 4-second lowering phase, wall push-ups with slow descent, Nordic hamstring curls) produce measurable strength gains in untrained and recreationally active adults. The effect is real and dose-responsive; the gains are not equivalent to a full PPL session, but they are meaningful for maintenance and progressive entry into loading.

Two practical framings for trained athletes. First: deliberately slowing the eccentric phase of PPL compound lifts to 3–5 seconds increases mechanical tension per rep without proportionally increasing training fatigue, session duration, or cardiovascular demand. A 3-second eccentric on squats, bench press, and rows is one of the highest-yield, zero-cost training upgrades available. Second: for travel days, recovery days, or high-stress periods when a full session isn't viable, 5–10 minutes of eccentric-focused bodyweight work now has a legitimate evidence base for maintaining the hypertrophy and strength stimulus between sessions.

One caveat requiring planning: eccentric-emphasis produces more DOMS than concentric or mixed-rep training, particularly when first introduced. The "repeated bout effect" means the first 2–3 sessions produce more soreness than subsequent ones. Starting volume conservatively (2–3 sets, 6–8 reps, 3-second eccentric) and avoiding heavy eccentric emphasis the day before leg days prevents recovery conflicts.

Source: ScienceDaily · May 2026 · Journal of Sport and Health Science, Vol. 15, Article 101126 (2026) · Edith Cowan University · Prof. Ken Nosaka
What to Integrate
  • Slow the eccentric phase of all PPL compound lifts to 3–4 seconds — zero added time, meaningfully higher mechanical tension per rep
  • Use 5–10 min eccentric-dominant work (Nordic curls, slow-descent squats, slow negatives) as a travel-day or recovery-day maintenance protocol
  • During deload weeks, keep eccentric-emphasis to maintain the neural and mechanical stimulus while reducing metabolic volume
What to Watch
  • DOMS is disproportionately high with eccentric emphasis — don't add new eccentric-focus work directly before leg day
  • The 5-min finding is for maintenance and untrained individuals — don't use it to rationalize skipping full PPL sessions in a progressive overload context
  • Eccentric-only protocols don't replace bilateral compound volume for maximal hypertrophy; they are a complement, not a substitute
⚡ Implication: Slowing the eccentric phase of PPL lifts to 3–4 seconds is the simplest high-leverage training upgrade available this cycle — no equipment, no added time, measurably higher mechanical tension per rep. For travel and recovery days, 5 minutes of eccentric-dominant bodyweight work now has a solid evidence base as a minimum viable session.

Supplements & Nutrition

The COSMOS multivitamin RCT is the most actionable supplementation finding in months, possibly this year. It fills a gap that the supplement industry has long papered over: a large, well-designed, placebo-controlled trial showing that a standard daily multivitamin slows biological aging on mortality-linked epigenetic clocks. The four-month effect over two years is modest, but it's real, it's in the right direction on PCGrimAge and PCPhenoAge (the two clocks most strongly associated with all-cause mortality risk in prospective studies), and the benefit concentrates exactly where you'd want it — in people already aging faster than their chronological age. The negative cocoa extract result is methodologically important: it argues against a non-specific supplement response and makes the multivitamin finding more credible as a specific nutritional signal.

Creatine's cognitive story is now formalized. The May 2026 CRC Press handbook and corresponding Frontiers commentary clarify what was previously scattered across the literature: creatine's phosphocreatine-ATP recycling mechanism operates in neurons as well as muscle cells, the cognitive benefits are strongest in populations with depleted baseline creatine (older adults, vegetarians, vegans), and the EFSA's methodology concerns about inflated meta-analytic effect sizes are worth understanding before making dosing decisions. The sleep deprivation data is the most practically useful subset: a single creatine dose maintained cerebral high-energy phosphates and cognitive performance under acute sleep deprivation in a clean mechanistic study.

COSMOS Trial: Daily Multivitamin Slows Biological Aging by ~4 Months Over 2 Years — First Large-Scale RCT Evidence, Strongest Effect in Those Already Aging Fast
[RCT]

The COcoa Supplement and Multivitamin Outcomes Study (COSMOS), led by Mass General Brigham in collaboration with the Medical College of Georgia at Augusta University, enrolled 958 healthy older adults (average age 70) and randomized them to daily multivitamin (Centrum Silver), daily cocoa extract, both, or placebo for two years. DNA methylation was analyzed at baseline, year 1, and year 2 across five epigenetic clocks: PCGrimAge, PCPhenoAge, PCHorvath, PCHannum, and DunedinPACE. Results were published in Nature Medicine (Li et al., 2026, DOI: 10.1038/s41591-026-04239-3).

Results: multivitamin supplementation showed slower biological aging across all five clocks compared to placebo. The aggregate effect equated to approximately four months of reduced biological aging over the two-year period. PCGrimAge and PCPhenoAge — the two clocks with the strongest prospective associations with mortality risk and age-related disease — reached statistical significance: between-group difference in yearly change of −0.113 years for PCGrimAge and −0.214 years for PCPhenoAge (roughly 2.7 and 5.1 months of slowing over two years respectively).

The benefit was heterogeneous by baseline aging status: participants whose biological age exceeded their chronological age at baseline showed a significantly stronger response. This is mechanistically plausible — a multivitamin's longevity benefit may be substantially attributable to correcting micronutrient deficiencies that accumulate with aging: suboptimal B12, D, K2, and folate are all documented in older adults and all have known roles in epigenetic maintenance processes.

Cocoa extract showed no significant effect on any of the five clocks. The contrast is methodologically valuable: it argues against a general non-specific supplement response and makes the multivitamin finding more credible as a real nutritional signal rather than an artifact of supplementation behavior. Caveats: long-term disease outcome data (cancer, cardiovascular, cognitive incidence) are not yet available; two years may not be sufficient to observe downstream disease effects. The COSMOS-Mind cognitive substudy found separate positive cognitive effects of multivitamin supplementation, providing modest convergent evidence from a different outcome domain.

Source: ScienceDaily · May 2026 · Nature Medicine 32(3):1012, DOI: 10.1038/s41591-026-04239-3 · Mass General Brigham / COSMOS Trial
Creatine's Brain Energy Role Formalized: PCr-ATP Recycling Operates in Neurons — Cognitive Benefits Concentrated in Depleted Populations
[Review]

A comprehensive synthesis of creatine's mechanisms, published as the Handbook of Creatine and Creatinine In Vivo Kinetics (CRC Press, May 11, 2026, DOI: 10.1201/9781003604662) by Dr. Mehdi Boroujerdi, formalises what several smaller RCTs have been indicating: creatine's phosphocreatine (PCr) / ATP recycling mechanism is not muscle-specific. Brain tissue uses PCr for the same rapid energy regeneration function — particularly during high-demand cognitive tasks, sleep deprivation, and metabolic stress states. A 2024 systematic review of 16 RCTs found significant positive effects on memory and attention with creatine supplementation. A Scientific Reports study demonstrated that a single creatine dose maintained cerebral high-energy phosphates and improved cognitive performance during acute sleep deprivation — one of the cleaner mechanistic demonstrations that creatine's brain effect operates via the same ATP pathway as its muscle effect.

A Frontiers in Nutrition commentary (2026) raises a legitimate methodological concern: the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) noted that many cognitive meta-analyses pooled non-independent cognitive tests (multiple measures from the same participant counted as independent data points), inflating effective sample sizes and potentially overstating effect sizes. The EFSA concluded no firm conclusions could be drawn from those meta-analytic estimates alone.

The population-specific gradient resolves much of the apparent inconsistency: cognitive benefits are strongest in older adults (brain creatine naturally declines with age), vegetarians and vegans (who have 20–30% lower baseline muscle and brain creatine than omnivores), and individuals under acute cognitive stress such as sleep deprivation. In well-rested, younger, omnivorous adults already near creatine saturation, effects are modest and inconsistent. Standard dosing: creatine monohydrate 3–5g/day is equivalent to premium formulations; brain saturation takes 4–6 weeks. Kidney condition patients should consult a physician before use — the evidence for harm in healthy individuals with normal kidney function is absent.

Source: ScienceDaily · May 2026 · CRC Press, DOI: 10.1201/9781003604662 · Frontiers in Nutrition commentary 2026 · Scientific Reports, 2024 (sleep deprivation study)
New Data — Consider Acting
  • Daily multivitamin (standard formulation — Centrum Silver equivalent) — COSMOS provides the strongest RCT evidence for a widely accessible longevity intervention; low cost, minimal downside, strongest benefit if biological age > chronological age
  • Creatine monohydrate 3–5g/day if vegetarian, vegan, older adult, or regularly under sleep pressure — cognitive benefits are best supported in these depleted-baseline populations
  • Reduce UPF to <20% of daily diet — processing harm is independent of macro targets; this is a separate risk signal, not compensable by hitting protein numbers
Stable Positions — No Change
  • Total protein ≥1.6g/kg/day remains the primary hypertrophy lever — multivitamin is an adjunct, not a replacement for nutritional fundamentals
  • DHA-dominant omega-3 formulations preferred over standard EPA+DHA if any head impact history (BIO-050: MUSC Cell Reports 2026 finding)
  • Fiber and fermented foods remain the primary dietary lever for microbiome health while CAG-170 targeted probiotics are in development
⚠ Hype Watch

"Creatine is a cognitive enhancer for everyone": The cognitive benefit is real but population-specific. In well-rested, younger omnivorous adults already near baseline creatine saturation, effects are inconsistent and often non-significant. The energy return on creatine for brain function is best where there's a genuine PCr deficit — older adults, vegans, sleep-deprived. Don't extrapolate the depleted-population finding to "creatine makes everyone smarter."

Multivitamin as a standalone longevity strategy: The COSMOS effect is real but modest — four months over two years, strongest in those already aging faster than their chronological age. A multivitamin doesn't substitute for training, diet quality, sleep, or stress management. It's a low-cost adjunct that corrects micronutrient deficiency states. Don't let a positive RCT finding become a rationalization for neglecting the fundamentals that the UCL arts study and HIIT research confirm are doing the heavier lifting.

⚡ Implication: Add a standard daily multivitamin — COSMOS is the most credible large-scale RCT evidence the supplement category has produced for a broadly accessible aging intervention. Add creatine monohydrate 3–5g/day if vegetarian, vegan, or regularly under sleep pressure. For UPF: processing harm is independent of macros — hitting your protein targets via processed food is not equivalent to hitting them from whole food.

Watchlist
1
New
COSMOS Multivitamin — Long-Term Disease Outcome Follow-Up
The COSMOS trial shows ~4 months of slowed epigenetic aging over 2 years. The outstanding question is whether this translates to reduced incidence of age-related disease (cardiovascular, cancer, cognitive decline) over longer follow-up, and whether the benefit requires continuous supplementation or persists after cessation. The COSMOS-Mind cognitive substudy is ongoing. Watching for: disease incidence endpoints from extended follow-up and post-trial cessation data.
2
New
HMW-HA / Naked Mole Rat Longevity — Pre-Clinical Human Translation
University of Rochester researchers (Gorbunova/Seluanov lab) transferred the Has2 gene from naked mole rats into mice, producing 4.4% lifespan extension, reduced cancer rates, lower multi-tissue inflammation, and improved gut health (Nature, 2023). The team is now testing molecules that slow hyaluronan degradation in pre-clinical trials — the human translation pathway. Watching for: IND filing for first human trial of an HMW-HA modulator; Phase 1 safety data on hyaluronan-preserving compounds.
3
Carried Forward
CAG-170 Targeted Probiotics — Development Pipeline
Cambridge researchers are working to culture CAG-170 ex vivo — the primary barrier before clinical formulations can begin. The cross-disease health signature (13 conditions, 39 countries) makes this the highest-potential next-generation probiotic target in the literature. Watching for: first successful culture announcement, Phase 1 probiotic trial registration. In the interim: dietary fiber and fermented foods remain the best-available proxy for supporting the conditions in which CAG-170 thrives.
4
Carried Forward
Rapamycin / PEARL Trial — Phase 2 Efficacy Data
The PEARL trial (rapamycin for longevity in healthy adults, Phase 2) results remain in the pipeline. No new data this cycle. mTOR inhibition remains the most clinically advanced pharmacological longevity approach, but the human efficacy question in healthy (non-diseased) populations is still open. Watching for: top-line biomarker outcome data, particularly on immune function and metabolic markers in the healthy adult population.
5
Carried Forward
Testosterone & Coronary Artery Disease — Mendelian Randomization Replication
The 2026 Mendelian randomization analysis finding ~17% higher CAD risk in genetically high-testosterone men needs independent replication. The finding is mechanistically plausible (blood pressure-mediated pathways), but the TRAVERSE trial's non-inferiority on MACE for hypogonadal men on TRT limits how broadly it applies. Watching for: independent MR replication in separate cohorts and mechanistic studies separating BP-mediated from direct testosterone effects on coronary vasculature.
