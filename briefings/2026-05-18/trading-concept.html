Alpha — Stop Hunts & Liquidity Grabs

Monday · 18 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #24 11-min read
TL;DR

A stop hunt is a deliberate price probe through an obvious level — a swing low, a round number, the high of yesterday — to detonate clustered stop orders and harvest the resulting liquidity, after which price reverses because the move had no fundamental fuel behind it. The trade is never to chase the wick; it is to wait for the wick to print, to fail, and for price to recover back inside the range — then enter against the trapped retail flow with stops beyond the spike high or low.

§01
What It Is
Predatory probe · Wyckoff spring/upthrust · liquidity harvest
A stop hunt is the act of pushing price just far enough beyond an obvious level to trigger clustered stops, after which price reverses because the probe had no real trend behind it.

Markets are predators. They are not benevolent venues that politely deliver fills at clean levels — they are auctions where the side with more capital and faster execution systematically extracts capital from the side with neither. Stop hunts are the most reliable predatory pattern in crypto because retail behaviour is the most reliable input. Every breakout trader puts a stop in the same place. Every long below recent support. Every short above recent resistance. Round numbers, prior swings, range highs and lows, session opens. Liquidity pools are not random — they are the residue of consensus.

A stop hunt is a tactical price move designed to trigger that residue. A liquidity grab is the broader concept: the strategic act of running price through a pool of resting orders — stops, take-profits, liquidation triggers, breakout pending orders — to source the order flow needed to fill institutional size with minimal slippage. The stop hunt is the weapon; the liquidity grab is the war aim. Sources distinguish the two by character: stop hunts wick and reverse, liquidity sweeps wick and continue. In practice the same candle can be either depending on what happens after.

Wyckoff named this a century ago. The spring is a false break below the support of a trading range — the Composite Operator drives price low enough to shake out longs, then absorbs the resulting forced selling to complete his accumulation at a discount. The upthrust is the mirror image: a false break above resistance during distribution, designed to trap breakout buyers and supply the offer the operator needs to finish dumping. Trading Wyckoff frames it exactly as you would frame a stop hunt today — price briefly breaches a level, reverses, and the trap is sprung. Same mechanic, different vocabulary.

What changed in crypto is the visibility of the trap. On a forex pair you infer the stop hunt from price action alone. On BTC you can pull up liquidation heatmaps, watch open interest drop in real time, see the aggressor side on tape, and overlay the liquidity heatmap showing exactly where size is resting. The pools are mapped. The hunt is televised. The setup is still tradeable because the people getting hunted either don't look at the maps or don't care.

If you only remember one thing Liquidity is the destination, not the wall. Price doesn't stop at the support — it stops where the support stops paying, which is the level just beyond it where stops sit. Trade the recovery, not the probe.
§02
Mechanics
Stop placement → liquidity map → probe → reversal
The mechanic chains four steps: stops cluster at obvious levels → operators read the cluster → a market order or sweep drives price into it → cascading triggers supply the liquidity, then exhaust.

The arithmetic of a stop hunt is straightforward. Retail stops cluster predictably — they live just below visible support (longs protecting downside), just above visible resistance (shorts protecting upside), and just outside round numbers (because human cognition rounds). Crypto adds a fourth pool: the liquidation price of the consensus leveraged position, which the exchange's own engine will trigger as a forced market order. Stack those four pools at the same price and you have a magnet.

Liquidity pool location (stylised): StopPool(support) ≈ Support − k · ATR where k ≈ 0.1 to 0.4 StopPool(resistance) ≈ Resistance + k · ATR Cluster strength (rough proxy): ClusterMagnetism ∝ OpenInterest(level) × LeverageRatio × Distance⁻¹ Probe completion test: Wick low < Support AND Close > Support → probe-and-reclaim = stop hunt completed (long bias) Wick high > Resistance AND Close < Resistance → probe-and-reclaim = stop hunt completed (short bias)
Note: the equations are heuristics, not precise formulas — k varies by asset and timeframe. The "probe-and-reclaim" close test is the operationally useful piece: a wick that closes back inside the prior range is the stop-hunt signature; a wick that closes outside is a real break and you are on the wrong side of the move.

The execution side is where stop hunts and pure liquidity sweeps diverge. A tactical stop hunt is small and surgical — a single aggressive market order or short sweep that pokes the level, harvests the immediate stops, then disappears as the operator scoops the resulting fills passively on the recovery. A strategic liquidity sweep is larger and continues — same probe, but the operator is building a real directional position and follows through with displacement after the sweep. The Zeiierman split is the cleanest framing: stop hunts wick and reverse; sweeps wick and continue. Same opening move, different second act.

The piece traders miss is the open interest signature. A genuine stop hunt is accompanied by an OI drop — the candle that wicked through support didn't just trigger stops, it forcibly closed leveraged positions whose liquidation price sat there. Coinglass and Velo both show this clearly on their liquidation-flow panels. If you see a sharp wick with no OI change, you are looking at a sweep with positions still open — far more dangerous than a clean shakeout, because the trapped flow has not yet capitulated. Wait. The candle isn't done telling the story.

§04
In Practice
Pick the level · wait for the wick · trade the reclaim
The one rule: never trade the probe — trade the reclaim. The wick is bait; the close back inside the range is the signal.
1

Map the Liquidity Before the Session

Mark the prior swing high, prior swing low, range high, range low, daily and weekly high/low, the nearest round number, and the consensus liquidation level (pull from Coinglass liquidation heatmap or Hyblock). These are your candidate magnets. If price is far from any of them, you are not in a stop-hunt regime; sit out.

2

Wait for the Probe — Don't Predict It

The mistake is shorting into a magnet because it "should" get hit. Wait for the wick to actually print. The probe must do three things: pierce the level, fail to close beyond it, and recover at least 50% of the wick within one to three candles. Without the recovery you are looking at a real break, not a hunt.

3

Enter on the Reclaim Candle, Stop Beyond the Wick

Enter when price closes back inside the prior range on the candle immediately after the probe (or at most one candle later). Stop goes just beyond the wick extreme — not at the wick, beyond it, accounting for the next stop hunt. Target the opposite side of the range as first take; trail with structure thereafter.

4

Confirm with Open Interest Drop

The cleanest stop hunts are accompanied by a visible OI drop on the same candle — proof that leveraged positions were liquidated rather than just stops triggered. If OI is flat on the probe, the trapped flow has not capitulated; the setup is weaker and the recovery may be incomplete. Use Velo or Coinglass to verify in real time.

Worked example — BTC range-low stop hunt (template)
Range76,300 – 82,900 (5-day consolidation, last week of trading)
Magnet76,313 prior swing low + 76,000 round number → consensus stop pool ≈ 75,900–76,100
Probe1h candle wicks to 75,950; close 76,420 back inside range; OI drops $180M on the candle
EntryLong 76,450 on the reclaim close (next candle open)
Stop75,800 (200 below the wick low → −0.85% risk on entry)
TP178,500 mid-range (+2.7% → R 3.2)
TP282,800 range high (+8.3% → R 9.8); trail stop to entry after TP1
Invalidation1h close below 75,800 → no reclaim, real break, exit and reassess
Timeframe fit: 5m–1h for execution and reclaim trigger; 4h+ for identifying the range and the liquidity map. Stop hunts on the daily are rare but enormous — they map to Wyckoff phase C springs at the major range lows and are the highest-RR trades in the playbook.

Historical reference: the cleanest crypto stop hunt of the cycle remains 19 May 2021 — BTC crashed from ~$42,000 to wick $29,000 before rebounding above $32,000 inside a single trading session, with $8.6 billion in leveraged positions liquidated in 24 hours per Coinglass. Every condition above was present: an obvious range low, a stacked magnet at $30,000, a stop pool beneath, and a sharp recovery back inside the range. That candle defined the trough of the May correction.

Read The magnet is loaded; the trigger isn't. Don't short to the level — wait for the probe and read the recovery. If the wick comes and closes back, you have asymmetry of 3R+ on a long; if it doesn't, the range is breaking and the next magnet is 73,314.

Wyckoff on shakeouts · pros on stop placement
A century of traders converging on the same point: the market is hunting where everyone agrees, and the trade is on the recovery, not the probe.

— Trader Dante · X · 2021 · widely-cited maxim on stop-placement consensus

"What stops price from moving? Liquidity — large limit orders at key levels. When price moves through a support or resistance and eats the available liquidity, there's little left to slow it down."

— Pentoshi · X thread on liquidity dynamics · 2021

§07
Common Traps
Chasing the probe · pattern as inevitability · sweep vs hunt confusion
The single biggest mistake is treating every wick as a stop hunt. Most wicks are not — most are part of a real break with continuation, and the cost of trading every wick as a fade is ruin.
1
Chasing the Probe Instead of the Reclaim

Trying to catch the wick by buying mid-flush is how accounts blow up. The probe is fast, asymmetric, and tells you nothing about reversal. The information is in the close — does price come back inside the range or not? Until the reclaim candle closes, you are guessing. Wait.

2
Treating Every Wick as a Stop Hunt

Most wicks through levels are real breakouts with brief retracement, not predatory probes. Fading every wick is the fastest way to short the bottom and long the top. Confirm with: (a) does price close back inside the range, (b) does OI drop on the candle, (c) is the recovery accompanied by volume. All three missing = it's not a hunt.

3
Stop Placement at the Wick Extreme

"My stop is below the wick low" — and so is everyone else's, which is the next stop pool. Put stops beyond the wick by a meaningful buffer (≥ 0.3 × ATR or below the next minor structural level), accepting the larger risk in exchange for not being the hunted in the next round.

4
Confusing a Sweep for a Hunt

A liquidity sweep that continues in the direction of the probe is not a stop hunt — it's the start of a real move. The difference: OI behaviour and follow-through volume. If OI rises on the probe candle and volume sustains afterwards, you are watching the start of trend, not the end. Get on the right side.

5
Overhyped: "Smart Money Concepts" mysticism

Half the modern SMC vocabulary — liquidity grabs, inducement, mitigation blocks, breaker blocks — is rebadged Wyckoff and order-flow with a 2020s rebrand. The underlying mechanics are real and worth knowing. The premium courses selling them as proprietary discoveries are not. Read Wyckoff, read Dalton, learn the orderbook. Skip the $999 cohort.
