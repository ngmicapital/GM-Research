Alpha — Spoofing & Iceberg Orders

Monday, 23 November 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue TBC 11 min read
TL;DR

Spoofing is displayed size placed to move other traders and pulled before it fills; an iceberg is real size hidden behind a small visible clip. Both mean the order book on your screen is not a reliable map of committed liquidity. Practical takeaway: judge a level by what actually trades there and how it behaves on repeat tests, not by how large it looks on the DOM. With BTC near 61,100 and the Fear and Greed index at 11, displayed walls deserve the least trust when fear is this high.

§01
What It Is
Fake size and hidden size
Spoofing is fake displayed size you never mean to fill; an iceberg is real size with most of it hidden.

Spoofing is the practice of showing displayed size you never intend to fill. A trader stacks large bids or offers away from the touch so the book looks like real demand or supply, then cancels before the order executes. The aim is to move other participants’ prices and queue decisions, and then trade the opposite side. US law names this directly: the Commodity Exchange Act, as amended in 2010, targets bidding or offering with the intent to cancel before execution.

An iceberg is the mirror image. It is real intent with most of the size hidden. A large limit order exposes only a small clip, and the reserve behind it refills the visible portion as it trades. Because reserve is not shown on the public depth view, the displayed book understates the liquidity sitting at that price.

Both concepts break one assumption: that displayed depth equals committed liquidity. Spoofing overstates it on purpose, and icebergs understate it on purpose. A trader who reads the DOM at face value is reading a picture someone chose to draw.

§02
Mechanics
How layers get placed and pulled
A spoofer layers visible size to push other participants, trades the other side, then cancels; an iceberg refills a small visible clip from reserve.

The spoof sequence has four steps. First, the trader places several large orders at prices away from the touch, building a visible wall on one side. Second, algorithms and discretionary traders read that wall as supply or demand and adjust their quotes. Third, the trader fills on the opposite side, where the book has been pushed in their favour. Fourth, the layers are cancelled before they can execute. The cancellation pattern is what separates manipulation from a legitimate order that was simply pulled, which is why regulators focus on intent to cancel rather than on the cancel itself.

The iceberg sequence is simpler. The trader sets a total size and a display size. Each time the visible clip fills, the exchange or the broker’s software exposes the next clip from reserve, so the price keeps printing at one level while the displayed size stays roughly constant.

Spoof tell = large size added away from touch, then cancelled, with opposite-side fills in the same window.
A proxy only. Exchanges with order-level data can see who cancelled; most crypto traders see aggregated depth, so this read is weaker.
Iceberg tell = repeated prints at one price while displayed size stays near the clip.
Hidden reserve is inferred from behaviour, not observed directly.

§04
In Practice
Reading depth without trusting it
Judge a level by repeated trades and cancellation behaviour, never by displayed size alone.
1

Treat walls as claims, not levels

A large displayed bid is a claim that someone will defend the price. Mark it as a level only after price trades into it and the size is still there at the touch. Until then it is a hypothesis.

2

Watch the size as price approaches

Real size tends to hold or get absorbed. Spoofed size tends to shrink or vanish within a few ticks of the touch. The shrinkage is the observation; the intent behind it is the inference.

3

Count repeated prints at one price

If a level keeps trading while the visible size stays small, reserve is likely refilling. That is a sign someone wants size done at that price, which matters more than the number on the book.

§07
Common Traps
Where the DOM misleads
Treating every cancelled order as proof of manipulation, or every big wall as real support.
1
Reading every cancellation as spoofing

Orders get pulled for many legitimate reasons, including risk limits and quote updates. Look for a pattern across repeated placements and opposite-side fills before calling it manipulation.

2
Treating a big bid as real support

Displayed size is a claim, not a commitment. Wait for price to trade into the level and for the size to hold at the touch before you lean on it.

3
Assuming hidden size is always there

An iceberg is inferred from repeated prints, not seen. If the prints stop and the level breaks, treat the reserve as gone and re-price the level from scratch.

4
Overhyped: spoof-hunting as a standalone edge

Retail-speed traders see aggregated depth on a few venues, not order-level data, so “spoof detected” calls are usually guesses from cancellations. Use the concept as a reason to distrust walls, not as a signal in its own right.
