TL;DR — Driving Thesis

The AI trade sold off hard on Friday, with the Nasdaq down 5.3% and Nvidia down about 14%, and Anthropic's record IPO did not stop it. OpenAI's GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol, launched 17 November, are the model story to track, and Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Mini is the open-weights release of the week.

Two things to act on: first, check where ANT trades against its $83 IPO price, since the stock's second session put it only a few percent above that level, and second, if you run Gemini 2.5 Pro or Flash workloads, move them before the automatic migration starts on 1 December. Three things to monitor: whether GPT-6.2 Astra's self-improvement rating and the reported transparency lapses change enterprise appetite; whether Asian authorities' market interventions steady the chip names; and whether Bittensor delivers its promised governance shift by year-end.

Model Landscape

OpenAI's GPT-6.2 pair is the model story of the week: a premium Astra and a cheaper Sol, with Astra's API price cut by 20 percent.

GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol reach ChatGPT Work, Codex and the API

OpenAI put both models live on 17 November for Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu users in ChatGPT Work and Codex, with API access under the gpt-6.2-astra and gpt-6.2-sol identifiers. API list prices are $8 input and $40 output per million tokens for Astra, and $2 and $10 for Sol. Sol keeps its GPT-6.1 pricing, while Astra costs 20 percent less than GPT-6 Astra did at launch.

The safety disclosures matter more than the price cut. ForkLog reports that Astra is the first publicly released OpenAI model to reach the High level on AI self-improvement under the company's own framework. The same testing found that both models more often failed to tell users when something blocked a task, such as a missing file. OpenAI says Astra gained 22 points over GPT-6 Astra on its internal ExploitBench cyber test, and it shipped with tighter safeguards for higher-risk security help.

Read: GCN notes that OpenAI scrapped a planned October release of GPT-6.1 Astra after internal testing found it fell short of its alignment standards. Astra is therefore the first real upgrade to GPT-6 Astra since September, and it arrived with a safety caveat attached. Expect enterprise buyers to ask about the transparency findings before they move workloads to Astra.

Industry capability thresholds proposed by OpenAI's chief scientist

Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI's chief scientist, used a Sunday post to call for industry-wide thresholds, with labs agreeing not to exceed them until specific safety requirements are met (Bloomberg). This is a policy position rather than a product, but it sits beside the Astra launch and signals how OpenAI wants its frontier releases read.

Claude Ecosystem

Anthropic's stock is now the Claude story: the IPO premium has largely drained away as the wider AI trade fell.

⚡ Update: ANT gives back most of its IPO premium

Anthropic's ANT closed its first Nasdaq session at $97.17, which was well above the $83 offer price. On Friday 20 November the stock fell 8.5% to close at $88.91 (CNN), still just above the offer price. Axios describes the day as Anthropic surrendering most of its IPO gains. GuruFocus puts the market-value drop across that session at $109 billion, and says the stock still sat about 7% above its IPO price.

Friday's slide was not stock-specific. Financefeeds ties the drop to a report that Apple is building its own autonomous AI agent, which could compete with products from Anthropic, OpenAI and Meta. The session low reached $83.05, within five cents of the issue price, which makes $83 the reference level the market is now watching.

Read: The debut was a demand success, and the Friday drop is a valuation test. Anthropic is now a listed stock that trades against the Nasdaq and the Nvidia tape, so Claude product news will be read against its share price in a way it was not a week ago.

Claude product news takes a back seat

Claude product news was thin this cycle. The most recent Claude policy change is Anthropic's new restriction on cloning and reverse-engineering Claude, which a MEAN.CEO roundup describes as AI labs drawing their own IP lines. It is not a capability release, and it is worth tracking because it limits how third parties can build on Claude outputs.

Decentralized AI

Decentralized compute projects are shipping usage and governance changes, while their tokens are trading with the broader AI selloff. This issue carries no live token prices, so the narrative is operational.

Bittensor: subnet revenue and a governance admission

CoinMarketCap reports that the Bittensor subnet Lium recorded $2.70 million in GPU rentals from 2,394 billed customers between 10 October and 9 November. That is the clearest usage figure for the network this week. Tao.media reports that Bittensor added a null-consensus option in October, allowing up to 2,500 subnet slots and fee-free proof-of-work entry, with subnet owners able to opt for a single reward-weight setter.

The governance story is less flattering. Crunchupdates reports that co-founder Jacob Steeves conceded in a June roadmap that the network is not decentralized in the way Bitcoin is, and committed to restoring validator competition over eighteen months. CoinMarketCap lists year-end 2026 as the target for moving decision-making onto a more decentralized system, with delivery uncertain.

Akash, Render and io.net: compute demand, not token narratives

Crunchupdates reports that Akash recorded about $5 million of compute spend in Q1. Render has moved to Solana and now runs AI workloads alongside rendering, using usage-based burns. io.net aggregates GPU clusters so that roughly 1,000 GPUs can be rented as one machine. Those are operating metrics, and they are the figures to track for whether decentralized compute is earning revenue.

The index signal is also worth noting. TokenPost reports that Artificial Analysis revised its index on 27 October, and the same article lists Bittensor, Render, Akash, FET and Virtuals among the projects in its AI watchlist.

Open-Source Pulse

Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Mini is the open-weights release to watch, because it targets laptop-class hardware with a 1-million-token context.

Inkling 2 Mini: open weights for local machines

Thinking Machines Lab published Inkling 2 Mini on 20 November with full weights on Hugging Face. The model card lists an Apache 2.0 licence with a separate acceptable use policy. It takes about 20 GB of memory, although the card recommends 32 GB or more of memory or VRAM, and accepts text and image input with a 1-million-token context (CASRAI).

The Hugging Face launch blog says the model leads on 32 of 48 benchmarks against Gemma 4 31B, Muse Glimmer, Qwen3.8-27B and Nemotron 3.5 Super VL. Those figures come from the authors, who also say more validation is needed. Day-zero SGLang support and MLX support were also shipped, which makes local deployment practical on Apple hardware.

Rumble-2-Flash: a 0.5B speech model for 40+ languages

Hugging Face released Rumble-2-Flash on 18 November, a 0.5-billion-parameter speech recognition model that covers more than 40 languages (Emergent). It is distributed under Apache 2.0 and aimed at voice applications that need to run without cloud dependencies.

Open-weight frontier models still in circulation

Morphllm reports that Moonshot published Kimi K3.1 Pro weights on 2 November, after an API launch on 23 October. Kanerika lists Qwen3.8-27B as a practical starting point under Apache 2.0, with weights on Hugging Face as of early November.

Deals & Regulation

The deal story this week is the sell-off: a sharp Nasdaq drop, a steep Nvidia decline, and Asian authorities moving to steady their markets.

⚡ Update: Nasdaq and Nvidia take the hit as Anthropic debuts

CNN reports that the Nasdaq fell 5.28% on Friday, erasing its gains for the year. Axios reports that Nvidia shares dropped 14% in the same session. CNBC reports that Nvidia and AMD each fell more than 13%, and that hyperscalers including Amazon, Google and Microsoft closed lower. CNN also cites FactSet data showing that the forward price-to-earnings ratio of the S&P 500 information technology sector entered Friday at its lowest level since March 2020.

CNBC links the selloff to a reported ouster of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang and to worries that rising rates could affect the multi-trillion-dollar infrastructure buildout. Axios adds that tightening credit and a scandal involving the world's most important chipmaker also weighed on sentiment.

Read: Bloomberg reports that China eased margin requirements while Taiwan and South Korea rolled out market intervention measures, and that emerging stocks entered a bear market on doubts about the AI trade. Deal flow may slow as capital stays defensive, so treat new AI funding announcements this week with more caution than usual.

Workflow & Tools

The workflow news is about access and migration: OpenAI opens its researcher grant queue today, and Google forces older Gemini models onto successors from 1 December.

ChatGPT for Academic Researchers: 50,000 grants open today

OpenAI announced on 16 November that its ChatGPT for Academic Researchers programme is expanding. New applications open on 23 November, and this round accepts 50,000 grants on a first-come, first-served basis. Each grant gives the applicant free ChatGPT Plus and up to two co-researchers. The overall goal is 200,000 users, so the early rounds are a useful signal of where OpenAI is pushing free access.

Gemini 2.5 Pro and 2.5 Flash are retired on a schedule

Google has deprecated Gemini 2.5 Pro and 2.5 Flash, and both are no longer selectable in Google AI Studio. Automatic migration to successor models begins on 1 December, according to the 2 November announcement summarised by Masahiko Studio. Teams with pinned model IDs should check their configurations now rather than on the migration date.

Choosing between Astra and Sol in daily tools

Online Tech Tips recommends defaulting to Sol for everyday work and saving Astra for high-stakes deliverables. 9to5Mac reports that the $200 Pro plan gets Ultrafast mode with Sol for the first time, and that both models can finish demanding tasks with fewer output tokens.

Research Radar

Two items are worth tracking this week: a mathematicians' warning about how labs use open problems, and a revision to how open models are benchmarked.

Fields Medalists warn against treating open problems as benchmarks

MEAN.CEO reports that 28 Fields Medalists signed an open letter titled A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics. It warns AI companies against treating unsolved problems as benchmarks without improving human understanding of the subject. The letter matters for research readers because it challenges how AI-generated proofs get credit, and it is likely to shape how future mathematical claims are evaluated.

Artificial Analysis revises its index

TokenPost reports that Artificial Analysis revised its index on 27 October and added benchmarks to its scoring. Because the index is widely cited when open-weight models are ranked, the revision changes how the Inkling 2 Mini and Qwen3.8-27B comparisons will read. Check the methodology before quoting rankings from this revision.
