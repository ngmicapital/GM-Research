Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

This week's convergence: the things that matter most in a financial life are the ones you cannot see, whether that is the purchase you didn't make or the older version of you who pays for today's decisions. Morgan Housel's notebook argues that wealth is hidden and that progress is too slow to notice while setbacks are too quick to ignore. Hal Hershfield and Peggy Yin's October 2026 research brief on adolescent future selves shows that a structured conversation with an older self reduced anxiety and strengthened connection after a single session. Housel's inversion essay adds the practical half: protect what you have by naming what would destroy it. The practice is to make the invisible visible on purpose, with a ledger of avoided spending, a log of small progress, and a dialogue with your future self.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Notebook · Morgan Housel · “Wealth Is What You Don’t See” · Dec 7, 2025

The wealth that matters is the spending you never made, so it never appears on a statement

Housel’s notebook starts from a blunt observation: wealth is hidden. He lists the cars not purchased, the clothes not bought and the jewelry not worn as the evidence. The point is that wealth accumulates in the gap between what you could have spent and what you actually did, and that gap produces no visible signal on any given day.

His second note explains why people feel stuck. Progress, he writes, often happens too slowly to notice, but setbacks happen too quickly to ignore. Those two speeds together produce chronic pessimism even when a person is quietly getting ahead. The same asymmetry that hides your savings also hides your improvement.

“Progress often happens too slowly to notice, but setbacks happen too quickly to ignore.”

Morgan Housel, Collaborative Fund notebook, 7 Dec 2025

If the invisible half of your finances is where the real gains sit, then the discipline is not to chase visible returns but to make the invisible half legible on purpose.

Research Brief · Peggy Yin & Hal Hershfield · “The Development of Future-Self Concepts in Adolescence” · Oct 28, 2026

A conversation with an older version of yourself is a cheap lever that changes how you feel about the future

This issue extends last week’s thread on the future self, which argued that people treat their older selves as near strangers. Yin and Hershfield’s new brief asks what happens next. Their finding is that the future-self sense is shaped less by biology or calendar time than by social expectations, which means it can be deliberately built.

In two related studies, participants aged 18 to 30 talked with an AI-generated older version of themselves. After a single session they reported less anxiety and a stronger connection to their future selves. A separate randomised study with 92 participants found that text, voice and avatar versions all strengthened future-self continuity compared with a control, with no significant difference between formats. The stranger problem has a tested and inexpensive intervention.

Yin and Hershfield suggest adolescents’ sense of their future selves is “limited less by biology or time than by social expectations.”

Peggy Yin & Hal Hershfield, UCLA Anderson Review research brief, 28 Oct 2026

The invisible person who pays for your decisions is not fixed. Give them a voice and a face, and the saving, spending and patience decisions made today start to look like they belong to someone you know.

Essay · Morgan Housel · “Very Bad Advice” · Jul 24, 2025

Build wealth by working out what would knock it down, because that is easier to see than what builds it

Housel’s inversion starts with an analogy: building a house is complex, while destroying one is simple. He suggests that when trying to get ahead it helps to flip the question and focus on how not to fall back. This is the same asymmetry as the notebook. The threats are loud and specific, and the sources of growth are diffuse and slow.

He also warns that competitors can probably innovate and execute as well as you can, so any talent you feel proud of is probably shared by others who want to win just as badly. For a reader, the practical version is to list the two or three things that would genuinely damage your position, then protect those first. Growth follows from a position that is not being quietly eroded.

Hidden wealth is protected more reliably by defence against the visible destroyers than by pursuing the invisible builders, so the work this week is defensive before it is ambitious.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · drawn from Morgan Housel

Make the wealth you are building visible with a ledger of avoided spending and a log of small progress

Housel’s notebook and inversion essay point to the same fix. Since the gains are hidden and the setbacks are loud, you need a deliberate instrument that records the quiet side of your finances.

  • Keep an avoided-spending ledger for seven days. Each time you decide not to buy something, write down the amount and the item. The total is wealth you would otherwise have never seen.
  • Log one piece of progress every evening. Record one concrete improvement, however small, so slow progress gets noticed before a setback distorts the picture.
  • Write down your two or three real threats. Name the specific events that would damage your position, such as a job loss, a leveraged bet or a fixed cost you cannot cut, and rank them by how permanent the damage would be.
  • Protect the top threat before adding a new goal. Pick the first threat on the list and take one step that reduces it this week, rather than starting another investment.

A ledger does not make your wealth bigger on its own, but it stops the invisible half from being ignored, which is where most of the damage and most of the growth quietly happens.

Practice · drawn from Yin & Hershfield

Hold a short dialogue with your future self before the next large money decision

The adolescent study used an AI-generated older self, but the mechanism is available without any software. A written or spoken exchange with a specific older version of you is enough to start building continuity.

  • Describe your future self in concrete terms. Write their age, location, health and financial situation in three or four sentences, so the figure has details rather than being a stranger.
  • Ask that future self one money question. Write the question you would want answered before making the decision, and write the reply in their voice.
  • Check your mood before and after. Note your anxiety level before and after the exchange, since the brief reported lower anxiety after one session.

A short Collaborative Fund notebook with the hidden-wealth idea and the asymmetry between slow progress and fast setbacks. It is worth reading in full because each line is a portable mental model you can apply to a personal ledger this week. Available at collabfund.substack.com.

Housel, “Very Bad Advice”

The inversion essay that frames financial progress as a problem of avoiding falls. The house-building analogy is the cleanest statement of why defence should come before ambition, and the piece is brief enough to reread before a major decision.

Yin & Hershfield, “The Development of Future-Self Concepts in Adolescence”

The UCLA Anderson research brief summarising two studies in which a conversation with an AI-generated older self reduced anxiety after one session. It is the most direct evidence in this issue that the future self can be built rather than merely imagined.

Book

Hal Hershfield, Your Future Self

The book-length treatment of why we regard our older selves almost as strangers and why that makes present sacrifices harder. Read it as the theory behind the research brief, since it explains the mechanism that the new studies are testing.

Hershfield et al., “Increasing saving behavior through age-progressed renderings of the future self”

The Journal of Marketing Research study behind the aged-photo experiments often cited in this area. It is the original evidence that making the future self vivid changes saving, and it is the right place to check any claim about the size of the effect before repeating it.

Mollick’s roughly weekly newsletter tests each new model on real tasks and draws a practical rule from the result. His November 9 essay on “Fable 5.2 and Jagged AGI” is a useful companion for anyone deciding how much to trust AI tools that simulate a future self.

04 On the Horizon
Finance

The housing gap is the largest hidden wealth transfer, and it is invisible until you are locked out

Housel argues that the median age of first-time homebuyers rose from 29 in 1981 to 40 today, and that being shut out of owning a home reshapes everything downstream. If the wealth that matters is the wealth that does not show up, then ownership is the clearest case of an asset that compounds unseen, and its absence is a delayed cost many people only notice late.

Mind

Future-self tools will move from research prototypes into everyday money apps

The randomised study of text, voice and avatar future selves found that all personalised formats beat a control on future-self continuity and motivation, with no significant difference between formats. Expect products that simulate your older self to appear in budgeting and retirement tools. The thesis of this issue predicts the test that matters: whether they make the invisible decision visible or simply entertain.

Culture

Tribe will keep setting the default for what counts as enough

Housel notes that everyone belongs to a tribe and underestimates how much that tribe shapes their thinking. The same tribe effect sits behind hidden spending, since the comparison group is what makes unbought things feel like a loss. Making the ledger of avoided spending is one cheap way to see the comparison group operating.
