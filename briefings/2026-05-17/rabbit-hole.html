TL;DR

Thomas Midgley Jr. was a celebrated American industrial chemist who solved two of the most pressing engineering problems of the early twentieth century. The first was engine knock — the destructive premature detonation in car engines that limited fuel efficiency and power. His solution, tetraethyl lead added to gasoline, worked perfectly and was known to be poisonous from the beginning: workers died building the production plants, Midgley himself contracted lead poisoning twice, and the occupational toxicologist Alice Hamilton warned in writing before the product launched that it would cause a public health catastrophe. It did. The second was refrigerant safety — existing chemicals were toxic or explosive. His solution, dichlorodifluoromethane (Freon), was genuinely safe at ground level and transformed food storage and air conditioning globally. It also, when it reached the stratosphere, released chlorine atoms that destroyed ozone in catalytic chain reactions. That mechanism was not discovered until 1974, thirty years after Midgley's death. He won every award chemistry had. Then polio left him bedridden, and the rope-and-pulley system he built to help himself rise was found wrapped around his neck.

Section 01

The Story

Thomas Midgley Jr. was born in 1889 into an inventive family — his father held patents for improved bicycle tires and early automobile components. He studied mechanical engineering at Cornell, graduated in 1911, and went to work at Dayton Research Laboratories, the research arm linked to General Motors and headed by the prolific inventor Charles F. Kettering. If there was a centre of applied industrial chemistry in 1920s America, this was it. The problems were important, the funding was real, and the culture rewarded results.

The problem Midgley took on was engine knock: the sharp metallic clanging of premature detonation in internal combustion engines. It wasted fuel, damaged pistons, limited power, and set a ceiling on how far the automobile industry could develop its engines. Kettering's team had been working on it for years. Midgley joined and attacked it systematically, testing hundreds of compounds. By December 1921 he had his answer. Tetraethyl lead — a compound of lead and organic carbon — added to gasoline in small quantities eliminated knock almost completely. It was a clean, commercially scalable solution to a genuine problem.

It was also, from the first day, known to be toxic. Lead's effects on human health had been documented for over two thousand years; Roman physicians wrote about it; nineteenth-century industrial medicine had catalogued its neurological damage in detail. Tetraethyl lead itself had been synthesised by a German chemist in 1853 and described as "highly poisonous." Within two years of commercial production starting in 1923, workers at the manufacturing plants were dying. At the Bayway Refinery in New Jersey in October 1924, five workers died and more than thirty were hospitalised with hallucinations and psychosis. The press called it "looney gas."

On October 30, 1924, Midgley poured tetraethyl lead over his hands, held a vial under his nose, and inhaled the vapour for sixty seconds. He told the assembled journalists he could do this every day without risk. Within days he was on medical leave, diagnosed with lead poisoning for the second time.

The surgeon general convened a conference in 1925. Experts including Alice Hamilton — the foremost occupational toxicologist of her era — testified that putting lead into gasoline was a decision that would spread a neurotoxin throughout the air, soil, and food chain for generations. The industry contested this, secured a finding of "no grounds for prohibition," and continued production. Midgley, who had recovered, was reassigned. He moved on to the next problem.

That problem was refrigerants. The chemicals in use — ammonia, methyl chloride, sulfur dioxide — were effective but dangerous: toxic, corrosive, or flammable. A methyl chloride refrigerant leak at a hospital in Cleveland in 1929 killed over a hundred people. General Motors' Frigidaire division needed something chemically inert and safe for domestic use. Midgley spent roughly three days thinking about the periodic table, identified the intersection of fluorine and carbon as the most likely region to yield a stable, non-reactive compound, and synthesised dichlorodifluoromethane. He called it Freon.

At the 1930 American Chemical Society meeting, he demonstrated it: poured a bowl of Freon vapour over a lit candle — the flame went out, showing it was non-flammable — and then breathed deeply from the bowl to show it was non-toxic. It was a showman's trick that worked exactly as intended. Freon transformed the refrigeration and air conditioning industry over the following decades. It was safe, stable, and genuinely useful. Midgley received the Perkin Medal for it in 1937 and the Priestley Medal — the American Chemical Society's highest honour — in 1941. He held over a hundred patents. He was elected president of the ACS in 1944.

In 1940, he contracted polio. The disease left him severely disabled; he could not rise from bed without mechanical assistance. He designed a system of ropes and pulleys to help himself up. On November 2, 1944, he was found dead, entangled in it. He was fifty-five. The coroner ruled his death a suicide.

Section 02

The Mechanism

Two separate mechanisms of atmospheric damage, decades apart in their discovery, stemming from the same man's two most celebrated inventions.

Lead from gasoline. When tetraethyl lead burns in an engine, it does not combust cleanly — lead particles are released in the exhaust. Those particles disperse into the air, settle in soil and dust, enter water systems, and accumulate in food chains. Lead is a neurotoxin with no safe level of exposure in children; it causes irreversible cognitive impairment, reduced IQ, impulsivity, and neurological damage even at concentrations once considered acceptable. Between the start of commercial production in 1923 and the US phase-out that began in 1975 and completed in 1996, leaded gasoline was used in virtually every car on earth. Blood lead levels in American children were approximately four times higher in 1978 than in the early 1990s after the phase-out reduced atmospheric lead concentrations. The Centers for Disease Control tracked average blood lead levels in US children falling roughly 75 percent between 1978 and 1991 as lead was progressively removed from the fuel supply.

The Lead-Crime Hypothesis

Researchers including Rick Nevin (2007) and economist Jessica Reyes (2007) identified a striking pattern: in countries across the world, the rise and fall of childhood blood lead levels — tracking leaded gasoline adoption and phase-out timelines — preceded corresponding rises and falls in violent crime rates by approximately twenty years, the time it takes for an exposed child cohort to reach peak crime-committing ages. The correlation holds across the United States, Britain, France, Australia, and several other countries, each with different phase-out timelines, each showing the same lagged pattern. The hypothesis remains contested and has not displaced other explanations for the 1990s crime decline, but the consistency of the lag structure across independent national datasets is difficult to dismiss.

CFCs and the ozone layer. Freon and its successors — collectively chlorofluorocarbons, or CFCs — are genuinely stable at ground level, which was the whole point of inventing them. In the troposphere, they do not react with anything. In the stratosphere, at altitudes above roughly 15 kilometres, ultraviolet radiation is intense enough to break the carbon-chlorine bond. This releases a chlorine atom. That chlorine atom reacts with ozone (O₃) to form chlorine monoxide and oxygen. The chlorine monoxide then reacts with another oxygen atom to regenerate the chlorine atom, which attacks another ozone molecule. The chlorine is not consumed — it is a catalyst. A single chlorine atom can destroy tens of thousands of ozone molecules before being captured by a competing reaction.

This mechanism was published by Mario Molina and Sherwood Rowland in Nature in June 1974 — thirty years after Midgley's death. When Midgley invented Freon and demonstrated its safety, CFC-ozone chemistry was not a known field of science; there was no theoretical framework that would have suggested CFCs were stratospheric hazards. This is a meaningful distinction from the lead story, where the toxicity was documented and the warnings were explicit. With CFCs, the damage was genuinely unforeseen by everyone. Molina and Rowland received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1995 for the discovery. The Montreal Protocol, adopted in 1987, became the most universally ratified treaty in UN history — 198 parties — and mandated the global phase-out of CFCs. The Antarctic ozone hole, which was measured annually from the late 1970s, has been slowly recovering since the mid-1990s and is not expected to return to pre-1980 levels until mid-century.

Section 03

The Connections

The warnings that were heard and dismissed. Alice Hamilton is not well remembered outside occupational medicine, but she was one of the most significant public health figures of the twentieth century — the first woman appointed to the Harvard faculty, a pioneer of industrial toxicology who documented lead poisoning in factory workers decades before the TEL controversy. In 1922, before commercial production of leaded gasoline had begun, she wrote that the industry was about to release "the most dangerous occupational disease that we have ever encountered" into the general environment. Her 1925 testimony at the surgeon general's conference predicted with precision what would unfold over the following fifty years. The institutional response was to commission further study while production continued. The lesson Hamilton drew — that industry-funded safety reviews of industry products require independent verification — became a recurring theme in twentieth-century public health, from tobacco to asbestos to pharmaceutical trials.

The "elegant solution to the wrong problem" pattern. Midgley was not careless or incompetent. He was solving real engineering problems with genuine sophistication and his solutions worked. The structural failure was in how those problems were defined. "What additive eliminates engine knock cheaply enough to produce at scale?" is a tractable engineering question. "What additive eliminates engine knock at acceptable total cost to human health and the environment?" is a different question, and it was not the one being asked — or not by anyone whose answer could affect the outcome. The same pattern recurs in the CFC story: "What refrigerant is safe enough for domestic appliances?" is answerable; "safe enough" implicitly meant "safe for humans in normal use," not "safe for the stratosphere." The externalities were not counted because they were not visible to the people counting costs.

The Montreal Protocol as a template — and its limits. The comparison between the CFC phase-out and the climate negotiations that have defined international environmental politics since the 1990s is instructive. The Montreal Protocol succeeded for several identifiable reasons: the mechanism was clearly established and accepted by a scientific community with no competing financial interest in the conclusion; the affected industry (chemical manufacturers) eventually identified profitable substitutes and stopped fighting; the timeline for visible harm was short enough that political will was sustained. Climate negotiations have encountered each of those conditions in a harder version — a more complex mechanism contested by better-funded opposition, an affected industry with trillions in existing assets, and a timeline for the worst consequences that consistently exceeds political cycles. The Montreal Protocol is evidence that international coordination on atmospheric chemistry is possible. It is also a demonstration of how unusual those conditions need to be.

J.R. McNeill's verdict, and what it means. The claim that Midgley was "the single organism that has had more adverse impact on the atmosphere than any other in Earth's history" — from McNeill's environmental history Something New Under the Sun (2001) — is sometimes misattributed to Bill Bryson, who made a related but softer observation about Midgley's "instinct for the regrettable." The McNeill framing is the more intellectually serious one. It is not a moral judgement about Midgley's intentions but a quantitative claim about atmospheric impact: the cumulative lead deposited globally by leaded gasoline, and the cumulative ozone depletion caused by CFC production, add up to a measurable change in the composition of the atmosphere that can be traced to decisions made by one person in Ohio in the 1920s and 1930s. The claim is about scale, not culpability. It is, in its way, more disturbing than a moral accusation: it describes an ordinary man, celebrated by his peers, solving real problems, whose aggregate effect on the physical environment exceeded that of any war, any industrial revolution, any natural disaster of the modern era.

Further Down the Hole

👩‍⚕️
Alice Hamilton — The Woman Who Was Right About Everything
Hamilton spent decades documenting lead poisoning in industrial workers before the TEL controversy. Her 1943 memoir Exploring the Dangerous Trades covers her career from factory floor investigations to her testimony against leaded gasoline. The Harvard appointment that made her the "first woman" at the university came with the condition that she not march in commencement or claim alumni tickets — Harvard was unsure what to do with her. She was unsure what to do with Harvard too.
🌡️
The Lead-Crime Hypothesis — Read the Papers
Rick Nevin's 2007 paper "Understanding International Crime Trends: The Legacy of Preschool Lead Exposure" in Environmental Research is the clearest presentation of the cross-national case. Jessica Reyes' 2007 paper in the B.E. Journal of Economic Analysis & Policy uses variation in US state-level phase-out timelines as a natural experiment. Neither paper claims lead is the only factor — both are careful about confounders. The convergence of their findings from different methodologies is what makes the hypothesis worth taking seriously.
🧪
Molina & Rowland — The Discovery That Changed Atmospheric Chemistry
Mario Molina's 2021 memoir, published posthumously (he died in 2020), describes the moment in 1973 when he and Rowland began calculating what happened to CFCs in the stratosphere and realised they had a problem. The chemical industry's initial response — accusing them of being "anti-American" and motivated by a desire to destroy capitalism — followed a playbook already familiar from the tobacco and lead industries. The Nobel citation in 1995 was partly a vindication of the principle that atmospheric chemistry belongs to science, not to the companies whose products it implicates.
🌍
The Montreal Protocol — How International Environmental Law Actually Worked Once
The negotiating history of the Montreal Protocol is studied in international law schools as the rare case of a working international environmental regime. Benedick's Ozone Diplomacy (1991) is the insider account — Benedick led the US negotiating team under Reagan, who was initially sceptical but ultimately signed. The protocol's success depended on a treaty structure that allowed graduated obligations for developing countries, and on DuPont's 1986 announcement that it had developed commercially viable HFC substitutes — removing the industry's incentive to fight.
⚗️
J.R. McNeill's Something New Under the Sun
McNeill's 2001 environmental history of the twentieth century is one of the best books about how industrial civilisation changed the planet. Midgley appears as a case study in a broader argument about the structure of modern production — that the feedback loops between industrial activity and environmental consequence are long enough, and diffuse enough, that they fall outside the decision-making horizon of both individuals and corporations. The book is a useful corrective to both techno-optimism and apocalypticism: it is not hopeful, but it is precise about what actually happened and why.
