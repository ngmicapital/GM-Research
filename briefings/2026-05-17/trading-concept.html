Alpha — Fair Value Gaps (FVG)

Sunday · 17 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #23 11-min read
TL;DR

A Fair Value Gap (FVG) is a three-candle price pattern where the wicks of candle 1 and candle 3 fail to overlap — a visual proxy for a one-sided, high-displacement move that left a thin-volume pocket the market often revisits to "rebalance". Treat it as a clean way to mark imbalance zones on a price chart, not as institutional X-ray vision; pair every FVG with a structural level, an orderflow read, and a price-action trigger, or it's just another rectangle in a sea of rectangles.

§01
What It Is
Three candles · one displacement · a chartist's imbalance proxy
An FVG is a three-candle window where candle 1's wick and candle 3's wick don't touch — leaving a visible price gap that price often returns to "fill".

Strip away the ICT vocabulary and a Fair Value Gap is a simple geometric pattern: three consecutive candles where the middle one moved so violently in one direction that the wick of the prior candle and the wick of the next candle never reach into the body of the displacement candle. The empty zone between them — the gap — is the FVG. Flux Charts puts the bullish rule cleanly: "the left candle's high does not overlap with the right candle's low". Flip it for bearish.

The conceptual claim — much stronger than the geometric one — is that those untraded prices represent an imbalance: aggressive demand or supply pushed price through a level so fast the opposite side never got a fair shot. The mean-reverting tendency of markets means price will often (not always) return to that pocket later to "rebalance" before continuing — which gives traders a defined zone to set entries against.

This is the central conceit and the place to be careful. As crypto orderflow shop Buildix argues bluntly: "Drawing a rectangle on a chart where price reversed and calling it an 'order block' is not the same as seeing actual institutional orders in the order book. One is a guess based on past price action. The other is data." The same critique applies to FVG. The pattern is real; the institutional-footprint narrative draped over it is a vibe, not a measurement.

That said, the geometry still has utility. A non-overlapping three-candle structure is a clean visual marker of high single-direction displacement — and the orderflow translation is real: in Volume Profile terms an FVG corresponds roughly to a Low Volume Node (LVN), a thin-traded price area that price tends to cross quickly in either direction. FVG, used honestly, is the chartist's version of "watch this LVN".

§02
Mechanics
Identification rules · mitigation · inversion · the orderflow equivalents
FVGs are identified mechanically from three candle highs/lows; mitigation, invalidation, and inversion are all defined by whether and how price re-enters the gap.

The identification is fully mechanical, which is part of why FVGs scale so well as an indicator. Flux Charts formalises it cleanly: zone boundaries are simply the inner wicks of the outer candles, and invalidation triggers on a close beyond the far side of the zone.

Bullish FVG identification: candle1.high < candle3.low zone = [candle1.high, candle3.low] invalidation = price closes below candle1.high Bearish FVG identification: candle1.low > candle3.high zone = [candle3.high, candle1.low] invalidation = price closes above candle1.low Mitigation: price re-enters the zone (often partial fill at 50% level) Inversion FVG (IFVG): price closes beyond the FVG in the opposite direction → broken bullish FVG becomes resistance → broken bearish FVG becomes support
Note: many implementations also require that the middle candle is meaningfully larger than the two outer candles (e.g. body ≥ 1.5× the 20-bar average true range) to filter out cosmetic gaps inside chop. Without a displacement filter, every choppy 1m chart prints dozens of FVGs an hour.

The mitigation rule is where the strategy gets contested. The standard ICT framing — codified across Writofinance, LiteFinance, and others — says price will return to the FVG to rebalance liquidity, often to the 50% midpoint, and continue in the original direction. The honest version, from EarnForex: "The theory of Fair Value Gaps states that any FVG will be filled sooner or later. But it does not say how late the 'later' can be." An FVG can sit unfilled for weeks; some are never filled. "Will be filled eventually" is an unfalsifiable claim dressed as a setup.

The Inversion FVG (IFVG) is the more useful evolution. When price closes through an FVG in the opposite direction, the gap "flips role": a broken bullish FVG (formed during an up-move) becomes resistance on the retest, a broken bearish FVG becomes support. The Trinity Trading deep-dive frames it as: "price violates the FVG, but instead of invalidation, it respects it from the other side." Mechanically this is just a classic role-reversal pattern — but pinned to a defined imbalance zone rather than a manually drawn line, which makes it cleaner to backtest.

Where this matters for an orderflow-native trader: every SMC pattern has a measurable orderflow equivalent. The Buildix translation table is the most useful single reference for converting between schools:

Visual
Two diagrams — the canonical bullish FVG, and an Inversion FVG flip
First diagram shows a textbook bullish FVG identified, retested, and held; second shows a bullish FVG broken and re-tested as resistance — an IFVG.
A textbook bullish FVG: the displacement candle (C2) leaves a gap between C1's high and C3's low. Price extends, pulls back to retest the zone — the test holds, buyers defend at the 50% midpoint, and the trend resumes. The retest into the FVG is the entry window; a clean close below C1's high invalidates.
Inversion FVG (IFVG): an FVG created during an up-move is broken to the downside (a strong close beyond the gap). On the retest from below, what used to be support flips role and acts as resistance — the rejection is the entry trigger. This is just classical role-reversal pinned to a mechanically defined zone.
§04
In Practice
Higher-TF first · confluence is non-negotiable · the trigger is price action
The one rule: never trade an isolated FVG. Use it as a level inside a higher-timeframe directional bias, with at least one orderflow confluence and a price-action trigger.
1

Top-Down: Bias on 4H/Daily, Execute on 15m/1H

Mark FVGs only on the 1H and 4H — those carry structural weight. Use them as zones in the direction of the daily trend. LiteFinance calls out M15–H1 as the sweet spot for execution; treat 5m FVGs as noise unless they sit inside a higher-TF zone. A 1h FVG aligned with daily structure is signal; a 1m FVG inside chop is decoration.

2

Apply a Displacement Filter

Require the middle candle's body to be at least 1.5× the 20-bar ATR. Without this filter every choppy session prints a dozen "FVGs" and the indicator becomes wallpaper. The whole point of an FVG is that the move was aggressive enough that the other side couldn't respond — a half-ATR move doesn't qualify.

3

Confluence or Pass

An FVG alone is a rectangle. An FVG that sits at a prior swing high/low, on a volume-profile LVN, at the daily VWAP, at a session high/low, or at a high-density liquidation cluster — that's a level with multiple reasons to react. Pair the FVG with at least one orderflow/structural input. Without confluence, fade or ignore.

4

The Trigger is Always Price Action, Never the Gap Itself

The FVG is the where. The entry is a price-action signal at the where: a rejection wick into the zone, a 50%-fill bounce, a failed breakdown that closes back inside, or an IFVG-style failed retest. Entering on the tap alone runs you straight into the "FVGs that get filled and keep going" problem that breaks newcomers.

Timeframe fit: 4H and daily FVGs for swing zones, 1H for tactical pullbacks, 15m for execution triggers. Anything below 15m is noise unless it nests inside a 1H+ structure. A daily FVG that's been mitigated cleanly is the most reliable variant; a fresh, untested 4H FVG is second tier.

§07
Common Traps
Five ways FVG turns into a losing edge
The single biggest mistake: treating FVG as an institutional-footprint X-ray rather than what it is — a geometric pattern marking high-displacement zones.
1
Trading Every FVG You See

On a 1m chart in a volatile session a chart prints dozens of FVGs an hour. Without a displacement filter (e.g. middle body ≥ 1.5× ATR) and a higher-timeframe bias, you are trading random rectangles. Restrict to 1H and 4H for zones, 15m and below only for triggers inside an established zone.

2
Believing "All Gaps Get Filled"

This is the most-repeated and most-misleading FVG claim. As EarnForex puts it: the theory says gaps get filled "sooner or later" but never says how late later is. Some FVGs stay unfilled for months — sometimes forever. "Eventually" is not a tradable timeframe; the unfalsifiability of the claim is exactly what makes it dangerous as a thesis.

3
Confusing the FVG With the Setup

The FVG is a level, not a trade. Tapping into the zone is necessary, never sufficient. Entering on the touch alone runs you straight into the "FVG that gets fully filled and keeps going" — the most common stop-out pattern. The entry is always a price-action signal at the level: rejection wick, failed flush, IFVG retest. Without that trigger, sit out.

4
Treating FVGs as Institutional X-Ray

An FVG is a chart pattern, not a view of the order book. Real institutional flow is measurable through Volume Profile, CVD, and exchange-side OI data — none of which require interpretation of three candle shapes. The "smart money left a footprint here" narrative is interpretation. When the FVG corresponds to an LVN on volume profile, the institutional claim has data behind it; otherwise it's storytelling.

5
Buying the ICT/SMC Hype Wholesale

FVG, Order Blocks, BOS, CHoCH, liquidity sweeps — the SMC vocabulary has rebranded several classical price-action concepts (role reversal, supply/demand zones, false breakouts, stop-hunts) in new language and sold them as institutional insider knowledge. A valid academic critique notes "SMC's terminology hides simple mechanics under sophisticated jargon". Backtests of strict SMC rules typically deliver 50–65% win rates — better than random, no better than disciplined classical price action. FVG works when it works as one more zone-identification tool. Treat it as such. Anyone selling it as standalone institutional alpha in 2026 is selling a course, not a system.
