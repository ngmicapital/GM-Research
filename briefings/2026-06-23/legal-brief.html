CFTC Moves to Bless Most Sports Event Contracts — Just as Kentucky Sues Kalshi and Polymarket

“Kalshi and Polymarket are operating illegal sportsbooks in Kentucky and breaking our laws.” Russell Coleman, Kentucky Attorney General

Kentucky Attorney General Russell Coleman filed suits in mid-June against Kalshi and Polymarket (and sweepstakes-casino operator VGW), alleging they run unlicensed sportsbooks in the state — landing roughly two weeks after the CFTC published a 267-page notice of proposed rulemaking (10 June) that would formally permit most sports event contracts on registered exchanges. The two events crystallise the central question for the asset class: who governs prediction markets — Washington or the states?

The CFTC proposal would allow contracts on final scores, point differentials, win/loss outcomes, tournament advancement, and individual or season-long statistical performance, while barring as “contrary to the public interest” contracts tied to player injuries, officiating decisions, physical altercations and pre-collegiate sports — alongside non-sports bets on assassinations, terrorism, war and the violent overthrow of governments. The 45-day comment window runs to roughly 25 July. The release leans on the Commodity Exchange Act’s public-interest review (§5c(c)(5)(C)) and the swap definition (§1a(47)), and Chairman Michael Selig’s CFTC argues in the text that sports contracts “may serve price discovery functions and provide meaningful information.”

The courts are split. The Third Circuit held in early April that the CEA preempts state law for sports event contracts offered on CFTC-licensed venues (the Kalshi–New Jersey appeal; Judge Roth dissented, calling the products “virtually indistinguishable” from DraftKings and FanDuel), while a Massachusetts judge in January found state gaming law still applies. The CFTC and DOJ went on offence in April, suing the governors of Illinois, Connecticut and Arizona. The stakes are no longer theoretical: Pew put combined Kalshi/Polymarket monthly turnover at roughly $24bn in April, up from under $5bn in September 2025, with the bulk of Kalshi’s volume in sports.

📎 Historical Precedent: The CFTC’s 2024 ban on election contracts — vacated after Kalshi v. CFTC (D.C. Cir., 2024) — is the cautionary tale Selig’s release explicitly disavows as “merit regulation.”
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The NPRM converts a litigation question into a rulemaking: once a final rule is in place, federally-registered designated contract markets gain a far stronger field- and conflict-preemption argument, while state gaming regulators retain their hook only over offshore and unlicensed venues. Norton Rose Fulbright (“Prediction markets at a crossroads”) and Epstein Becker Green (“the Kalshi litigation gamble”) both flag that the public-interest carve-outs are doing the heavy lifting. The Brief's read: the injury and officiating exclusions are the real battleground — they sit precisely where manipulation risk overlaps with state gambling-consumer law, and that is where the comment fight (and the next round of suits) will concentrate.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Congressional Democrats pressed the CFTC on 30 April (CNBC) to rein in sports betting and insider-trading risk, and state AGs argue that a “merit-neutral” federal framing launders sports wagering past state consumer protections and tax regimes — a revenue grab dressed as derivatives. The Brief's read: the counter is real on tax and consumer-protection grounds, but the CEA’s exclusive-jurisdiction text is hard to litigate around once a venue is CFTC-registered; expect states to win delay, not the war.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Winners are the CFTC-registered venues — Kalshi, Polymarket, and distribution partners such as Coinbase and Robinhood — plus CME, which gains optionality to list event contracts. Threatened incumbents are the state-licensed sportsbooks (DraftKings, Flutter/FanDuel), which increasingly want their own CFTC licenses rather than 50 state regimes; state lotteries and gaming-tax receipts are the structural losers. The Brief's read: the regulatory moat is shifting from a state sportsbook license to a federal DCM/DCO registration.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🔴 Bearish DKNG, FLUT — CFTC-regulated event contracts undercut state-licensed sportsbooks on tax drag and product breadth.

⚪ Neutral CME — long-run optionality to list event contracts, but no near-term revenue line.

Hong Kong Lines Up Dealer and Custodian Licensing — and Opens a New Front on Crypto Advisers

“The new dealing and custody regimes will sit under the Anti-Money Laundering Ordinance and broadly mirror the requirements that already apply to securities dealing.” [paraphrased] FSTB / SFC consultation conclusions

Hong Kong’s Financial Services and the Treasury Bureau (FSTB) and the Securities and Futures Commission (SFC) are preparing to introduce, to the Legislative Council in 2026, bills creating licensing regimes for virtual-asset dealers and custodians — and have separately launched a one-month consultation on regulating VA advisory and asset-management services, extending the SFC’s five-pillar “ASPIRe” roadmap down the full intermediary chain. The dealer/custody proposals followed consultations that drew more than 190 responses (101 on dealing, 93 on custody) before closing in August 2025.

The sequencing is the story: Hong Kong is licensing crypto by function — trading platforms first (2023), then dealers, custodians and now advisers — rather than imposing a single all-at-once cliff. That stands in sharp contrast to the EU’s hard MiCA deadline next week and the US’s legislate-by-deadline scramble, and it positions Hong Kong (alongside Singapore) as the natural booking centre for firms that cannot make Europe’s cut.

📎 Historical Precedent: Hong Kong’s 2023 VASP trading-platform regime (OSL, HashKey) is the template now being extended to the rest of the value chain.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Gibson Dunn (“Hong Kong concludes consultations… with yet more to come”) and CoinDesk note the deliberate, function-by-function pace — dealing and custody under the AMLO, mirroring securities-dealing standards, with advisory next. The Brief's read: the measured cadence is a feature, not a bug: by avoiding a single enforcement cliff, Hong Kong reduces the wind-down chaos the EU is about to experience, and firms shut out of MiCA by 1 July have a credible Plan B.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Skeptics note Hong Kong has announced more than it has enacted, and the 2026 LegCo timetable is crowded; a slipped bill leaves dealers and custodians in the same grey zone they occupy today. The Brief's read: execution risk is real, but the live adviser consultation shows momentum, and the AMLO hook means enforcement does not have to wait for new primary legislation.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Beneficiaries include licensed platforms and custodians (OSL, HashKey, Animoca-adjacent ventures) and global custodians (BitGo, Anchorage) eyeing Hong Kong charters; HKEX deepens its regulated-liquidity story. The competitive pressure falls squarely on Singapore’s MAS, which is racing on tokenisation pilots. The Brief's read: the APAC hub contest is now a licensing-speed race, and Hong Kong just added two more lanes.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish OSL (863.HK), HashKey ecosystem, HKEX (0388.HK) — a regulated intermediary chain deepens Hong Kong crypto liquidity.

🔴 Bearish Unlicensed offshore custodians serving Hong Kong clients — license-or-exit pressure arrives with the bills.

⚪ Neutral Mainland-China exposure — Hong Kong’s hub status does not move the PRC’s onshore prohibition.

SEC’s Draft 2026–2030 Plan Makes Digital Assets Its First Stated Regulatory Objective

“Establishing a fit-for-purpose framework for digital assets and distributed-ledger technology is the Commission’s first objective.” [paraphrased] SEC Draft Strategic Plan, FY2026–2030 (2 June 2026)

On 2 June the SEC published its draft strategic plan for FY2026–2030, elevating digital assets and distributed-ledger technology to the first objective under Goal 1. The significance is institutional: it signals that the Atkins-era pivot — the March SEC–CFTC joint interpretation and five-category token taxonomy, the dismissal of the Coinbase, Binance and Ripple actions, and the proposed tokenisation “innovation exemption” — is now embedded in the agency’s multi-year mandate rather than a reversible enforcement posture.

Practically, it reorients the Commission from enforcement-first to rulemaking-first and makes the token taxonomy (digital commodities, collectibles, tools, stablecoins, securities) the operating model, with only “digital securities” squarely under full SEC jurisdiction. The headline open item — the on-chain tokenisation “innovation exemption” floated by Chair Atkins — remains under White House review and is still a political signal, not a final rule.

📎 Historical Precedent: The 2022–2024 “regulation by enforcement” era under former Chair Gensler is the explicit foil the new plan is written against.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Latham’s US Crypto Policy Tracker and the Sidley/Norton Rose readouts of the March joint interpretation, a strategic plan is a priority-setting and budgeting document, not law. The Brief's read: precisely because it drives headcount and supervisory focus, expect the Division of Trading & Markets and the Crypto Task Force to be resourced — and expect the next 18 months of crypto rulemakings (custody, exchange registration, the innovation exemption) to be where the real fights happen.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Registered exchanges (Coinbase), would-be DCMs, and RIAs adding spot-crypto exposure get a clearer multi-year runway; tokenisation issuers (BlackRock, Franklin Templeton) read the plan as a green light to keep building on-chain product. The Brief's read: the durable winner is anyone whose business model needs the SEC to write rules rather than litigate — the plan tells them the door is open.

Regulators Lock In “Technology-Neutral” Capital Treatment as Tokenized Treasuries Near $15bn

“An eligible tokenized security receives the same capital treatment as the non-tokenized form of the same security.” [paraphrased] Federal Reserve / OCC / FDIC joint FAQ (5 March 2026)

The prudential question that gated bank participation in tokenisation has been answered: a 5 March joint FAQ from the Federal Reserve, OCC and FDIC confirmed the capital rule is technology-neutral — an eligible tokenised security carries the same capital charge as its conventional twin — and the IMF followed on 2 April with a note calling tokenisation a fundamental reconfiguration of financial architecture. Together they remove the “is it riskier because it’s on a blockchain?” objection that banks had used to stay on the sidelines.

The market has run with it. rwa.xyz put tokenised US Treasuries at roughly $14.8bn across 82 assets and 65,000+ holders as of 10 June, with BlackRock’s BUIDL fund alone above $2.5bn by late May. The remaining US gating item is the SEC’s proposed on-chain trading “innovation exemption” (see Story 3), still under White House review.

📎 Historical Precedent: The 2023 SEC staff position (SAB 121) that forced banks to balance-sheet custodied crypto — since rescinded — was the prudential roadblock this FAQ finally clears in the opposite direction.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Technology-neutrality is the unlock: it lets bank treasuries and broker-dealers treat tokenised Treasuries and money-market funds as ordinary collateral, not exotic crypto exposure. The Brief's read: the next contested frontier is not capital but secondary-market structure — who can operate the on-chain venue and under what exemption — which is exactly what the SEC’s pending innovation exemption decides.

🏗️ Industry Implications

BlackRock (BUIDL), Franklin Templeton, Ondo and Securitize are the issuers compounding fastest; JPMorgan and Goldman extend tokenised-collateral rails; the CFTC’s December tokenised-collateral pilot lets FCMs post BTC, ETH and stablecoins as margin. The Brief's read: tokenised Treasuries are quietly becoming the on-chain risk-free rate, and whoever owns distribution of that collateral owns the plumbing.

The open question is foreign-issuer comparability — Treasury must decide which overseas stablecoin regimes are “comparable,” and FinCEN has asked whether AML obligations should extend to foreign issuers. Source: OCC/FDIC/Treasury Federal Register notices; Sullivan & Cromwell.
CLARITY Act: on the calendar, off the floor The market-structure bill sits on the Senate Legislative Calendar (No. 423) but Majority Leader Thune has not scheduled floor time; it needs roughly seven Democratic votes to clear cloture. The White House’s 4 July target is slipping toward the August-recess wall, with midterm politics hardening behind it. Source: CoinDesk; The Defiant; Senate Banking Committee.
California DFAL goes live 1 July — the “complete application” trap Firms serving California residents must hold a DFAL license, qualify for an exemption, or have a complete application on file by 1 July to keep operating while DFPI reviews; a placeholder filing will not do. Initial expectations: $7,500 fee, ~$100,000 tangible net worth and a $500,000 surety bond. Source: California DFPI; Jones Day; Elliptic.
MiCA transitional cliff: ~210 of 1,200+ legacy VASPs converted With eight days to the 1 July hard stop, only around 210 of the 1,200-plus pre-MiCA registrants hold full CASP authorisation; ESMA has confirmed no grace period and unlicensed service after the date breaches EU law. Tether still declines to seek MiCA authorisation, sustaining USDT delistings across EEA venues. Source: ESMA; Elvinger Hoss; news.bitcoin.com.
Australia’s AFSL relief expires 30 June ASIC’s no-action relief ends in seven days: firms dealing in digital-asset financial products must have lodged an AFS licence application by 30 June or face civil/criminal exposure. The Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Bill still awaits a resumed second reading. Source: ASIC 25-250MR; Treasury; Piper Alderman.
Tornado Cash: developer-liability bellwether still pending Judge Katherine Polk Failla has yet to rule on Roman Storm’s Rule 29 acquittal motion (argued 9 April) on his §1960 conviction; DOJ has asked for an October retrial (proposed 5/12 Oct) on the deadlocked money-laundering and sanctions counts. The outcome shapes how far criminal liability reaches non-custodial code. Source: SDNY docket; DeFi Education Fund; The Block.
