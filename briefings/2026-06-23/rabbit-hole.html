The map that shrank Africa — and hung on classroom walls for 400 years

Gerardus Mercator · 1569 · Cartography as Politics

The world map most of us grew up with was designed for 16th-century sailors and makes Greenland look as big as Africa — Africa is 14 times larger. A navigational tool became the world's default image of itself, and the distortion was anything but neutral.

TL;DR

In 1569, Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator solved the sailor’s oldest problem: how to draw a straight compass course on flat paper. His solution was mathematically brilliant but came with a hidden cost — to keep angles accurate, the map had to stretch landmasses the further they were from the equator. Europe grew. Africa shrank. Greenland ballooned to apparent parity with a continent 14 times its size. For four centuries, this navigation instrument hung on classroom walls, quietly teaching the world that the countries of the colonial north were larger — and by implication more important — than the tropical south. The math was impeccable. The geopolitics were accidental. The damage was not.

Section 01

The Story

In the sixteenth century, the most dangerous thing about ocean navigation was not the weather or the pirates. It was the map. Sailors had known for centuries that the Earth was a sphere, which meant that drawing it flat required choosing what to sacrifice — you could not preserve both shape and area, both distance and direction, on a flat surface simultaneously. The result was chaos: charts made by different cartographers used different conventions, and a straight line on one man’s map curved treacherously into something else on another’s.

Gerardus Mercator, a Flemish geographer working in the city of Duisburg, understood the core problem precisely. A sailor setting a compass heading of, say, northeast wanted to follow that heading to his destination. On the curved surface of the globe, a constant-bearing course — called a rhumb line — is actually a spiral, curving gradually toward the poles. Plot it on a flat map without correction and the straight line the sailor draws is a lie. Ships ran aground, missed landfalls, and sank because of this.

In 1569, Mercator published a new world map — eighteen separate printed sheets forming an image 202 centimetres wide and 124 centimetres tall. His innovation was elegant: he stretched the map vertically near the poles, spacing the lines of latitude further and further apart as you moved away from the equator. The amount of stretching was precisely calibrated to match the east-west distortion caused by flattening a sphere. The result was a conformal projection — one that preserved angles exactly. A navigator could now draw a straight line between any two ports and read off the compass bearing directly. The map was titled, with justifiable pride: “New and more complete representation of the terrestrial globe properly adapted for use in navigation.”

The Problem With Success

Mercator’s projection was adopted as the standard nautical chart by the 18th century and never fully displaced from that role. But somewhere along the way, map-printers began using the same plates for school atlases. The tool built for sailors became the image of the world for everyone else — and stayed that way for four centuries.

Mercator himself never intended the map to define the world’s sense of geography. He was solving a practical problem for a specific audience. But in the centuries that followed, his projection migrated from the ship’s chart table to the classroom wall, from the navigator’s instrument to the child’s first mental model of the world. The transformation happened gradually, without announcement or decision — the Mercator map was simply the one that existed, that printers had the plates for, that teachers used because teachers before them had used it.

The scale of the lie becomes visible only when you know the numbers. Greenland covers 2.1 million square kilometres. Africa covers 30 million — nearly fourteen and a half times larger. On a Mercator map, they appear roughly the same size.

By the twentieth century, the Mercator projection was universal in Western classrooms. It hung on school walls, appeared in encyclopaedias, and set the mental image of the world for hundreds of millions of children. Europe sat squarely at the centre, inflated above its true size. Africa and South America, straddling the equator where distortion is least, appeared small and peripheral. The map, in short, looked exactly like a colonial-era vision of which places mattered.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The mathematics behind Mercator’s distortion is a consequence of an inescapable geometric fact: you cannot flatten a sphere without tearing or stretching it. Every map projection is a choice about what to preserve and what to sacrifice. Mercator chose to preserve angles — specifically, the angle between any two paths crossing at a point. That choice is called conformality, and it is what makes his projection invaluable for navigation and deeply misleading as a picture of the world.

Here is the trade-off in practice. Imagine peeling an orange and trying to press the skin flat. It tears at the edges. A cartographer must mathematically “fill in” those tears. Mercator did it by stretching: as you move a degree of latitude away from the equator, he stretched the map vertically by a corresponding amount — an amount precisely equal to the stretching that flattening the sphere caused horizontally. Because both dimensions are stretched by the same factor at any given latitude, angles are preserved. But areas are not. A one-degree square near the North Pole is stretched enormously in both dimensions, so it appears vastly larger on the map than it actually is.

The Numbers

At the equator, the Mercator projection is accurate. At 60 degrees latitude — roughly the latitude of Oslo, Anchorage, or St. Petersburg — areas are stretched to four times their true size. At 80 degrees, the stretch factor is 38 times. Greenland sits between 60 and 83 degrees north. Africa straddles the equator, where distortion is minimal. The projection makes Greenland appear 73.9% larger than it actually is.

The consequences ripple across the entire map. Europe appears roughly the same size as South America. In reality, South America is nearly twice as large. Alaska looks larger than Mexico; Mexico is actually larger. Antarctica, at the very edge of the map, stretches into an infinite white continent. The more poleward a country sits, the more the Mercator map flatters it. The map did not set out to exaggerate the importance of northern, temperate, historically colonial nations — it exaggerated them as a side effect of solving an unrelated problem.

The Mercator projection is one of the most politically consequential pieces of applied mathematics in history — not because anyone intended it, but because a tool optimised for one audience was handed, unchanged, to a completely different one.

The alternative — an equal-area projection — preserves the relative sizes of landmasses but distorts their shapes. Equal-area maps look “wrong” to eyes accustomed to Mercator: Africa is elongated, Europe compressed, the overall image feels unfamiliar. This is why no projection is simply “correct”; every choice makes the world look different, and which difference you choose encodes a set of priorities. Mercator’s priority was navigational precision. The classroom wall needed something else entirely — and for four centuries, it got the wrong tool anyway.

Section 03

The Connections

The equal-area challenger. In 1974, a German historian named Arno Peters stood before a press conference in Bonn and unveiled what he called “The Peters Projection” — an equal-area map that he claimed would correct the Mercator’s implicit Eurocentrism and give the developing world its true visual weight. Peters was not a cartographer and the professional cartographic community was withering in its contempt: his projection was essentially identical to a map published in 1855 by a Scottish clergyman named James Gall. Professional geographers condemned Peters for both the plagiarism and for the shape distortions his equal-area approach introduced — the map made Africa appear stretched and elongated in ways that looked just as wrong as Mercator’s size errors. Nevertheless, the United Nations, UNICEF, and various development agencies adopted the Gall-Peters projection precisely because of its political symbolism. The controversy ran for nearly two decades.

Boston, 2017. When the debate crossed from academia to classrooms, the geography got personal. In March 2017, Boston Public Schools became the first major American school district to formally replace Mercator maps in classrooms — switching to the Gall-Peters projection for second, seventh, and eleventh grades. The stated aim, in the words of Colin Rose, the district’s assistant superintendent, was “the start of a three-year effort to decolonize the curriculum.” With 86% of students being people of colour, the district felt that presenting children with maps that made their continents appear small and marginal was not a neutral pedagogical choice. Cartographers pointed out that the Gall-Peters map introduced different distortions, merely trading one inaccuracy for another. The debate encapsulates the core problem: there is no neutral map, only maps that encode different priorities.

National Geographic, 1998

After decades of using the Robinson projection — itself a 1963 compromise — the National Geographic Society switched in 1998 to the Winkel Tripel, a projection designed by German cartographer Oswald Winkel in 1921. The name comes from the German word for “triple”: Winkel aimed to minimize three types of distortion simultaneously — area, direction, and distance — without perfecting any of them. Studies have since confirmed it achieves the lowest mean overall distortion of any standard projection. It is still not “correct.” It is the best available compromise.

The irony of Google Maps. When web mapping arrived, engineers at Google reached for Mercator — not for geopolitical reasons but for a purely computational one. The Web Mercator projection (formally EPSG:3857) makes map tiles trivially easy to calculate: every zoom level is a simple power-of-two grid, and the math for placing objects on the map is clean and consistent. Mercator’s conformal property means that small areas look locally correct, which is all that matters when you are navigating to a restaurant. For street-level navigation, the projection that distorts the global picture is actually the right tool — exactly as Mercator intended. Google now shows a 3D globe view when you zoom out far enough, precisely to avoid the Mercator distortion problem at planetary scale.

What maps reveal about their makers. Every map is an argument. Medieval European maps placed Jerusalem at the centre. Chinese maps traditionally put China at the centre. Arabic maps from the 12th century were sometimes drawn with south at the top, placing the Arab world prominently in the upper half. The Mercator projection placed Europe near the top and near the centre not because Mercator intended to make a political statement — he was solving a different problem entirely — but because the map was then handed to institutions with their own interests in how the world looked. The most consequential maps are rarely the ones anyone chose for their politics.

⚊
The Rhumb Line vs. the Great Circle

The straight-line course Mercator preserves (a rhumb line) is not the shortest path between two points. The great circle route — an arc across the globe — is shorter, but the bearing changes constantly. Modern navigation uses great-circle routes; Mercator-straight lines are a historical approximation.

📺
The West Wing, Season 2

In a 2001 episode, the fictional Organization of Cartographers for Social Equality petitions White House staffers to officially switch the US government to a Peters projection — and to flip the map so south is at the top. The scene is played for laughs but the geography is real.

♁
The AuthaGraph Projection

A 2016 Japanese projection that folds the globe onto an unfolded tetrahedron to minimize distortion across oceans and continents simultaneously. It won Japan’s Good Design Award and caused a brief media frenzy with its distinctly unfamiliar shape — recognizable but deeply strange.

🕐
The Longitude Problem

The Mercator map solved the problem of drawing a bearing accurately. But sailors still could not know their east-west position. The solution — accurate chronometers — came 200 years later, championed by clockmaker John Harrison after the British Admiralty offered a £20,000 prize and then spent decades refusing to accept his answer.
