Alpha — Orderbook Depth & Spread

Monday 23 June 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #58 11 min read
TL;DR

Order book depth is the resting limit-order size stacked at each price level; the spread is the gap between best bid and best ask — together they price the cost of immediacy and reveal what the market can and can't absorb. Read depth asymmetry and spread regime, not just the mid: a tight spread sitting over a thin book is a trap, and the side with thinner resting size is usually the way price travels next.

§01
What It Is
Depth, spread, and the price of immediacy
Depth is the resting limit-order size stacked at each price level; the spread is the gap between best bid and best ask — the live price of trading right now.

The top of book is one number on each side: the best bid, the best ask, the last print. That is where trading happens now. Depth of Market (DOM) — the Level 2 ladder — is everything behind that number: how much size rests at every price below the bid and above the ask. The spread is the width at the very top of that ladder. The mid-price tells you where the last trade fired; depth and spread tell you what the market can absorb and what immediacy costs. That gap is the edge — the price chart is a result, the book is the cause.

The spread is not arbitrary. It is the market maker's quoted price for providing immediacy, and microstructure research decomposes it into three costs: order-processing, inventory risk (the cost of holding a position the maker didn't want), and adverse selection (the risk of trading against someone better informed). When uncertainty rises, the adverse-selection component blows out and makers widen the quote — frequently before price moves at all. A spread that widens on no move is information, not noise.

Depth and spread are two readings of one thing. A 2021 study of intraday limit-order books documents an inverse relation between depth and spread — when the book is deep, the spread is tight; when depth thins out, makers widen the spread to be paid for the rising risk. So a widening spread over a thinning book isn't two signals; it's the same signal twice: liquidity is leaving.

§02
Mechanics
How depth and spread generate signal
Aggressive market orders eat depth and widen the spread; passive limit orders refill depth and tighten it — the tug between them is the signal.

1. The book is a queue. Limit orders rest at each price under price-time priority. A market buy lifts the best ask and consumes that level's size. If the order is larger than the level, it walks up to the next ask, and the next — each step is worse fill. That walk is slippage, and for the moment it takes makers to react, the spread widens.

2. Refill or retreat. Right after a sweep, makers either reload the consumed level — a resilient book, where the spread snaps back in milliseconds — or they step away. A book that doesn't refill is a thin book, and the spread stays wide while price gaps to the next pocket of size. Resiliency is the recovery half of this same mechanic.

3. Asymmetry is the read. Sum the resting size on each side within a band (say ±0.5% of mid). The thinner side is the path of least resistance — price travels toward less resting size, not more. This is the single most useful thing the book tells you, and it's invisible if you only watch the mid.

Quoted spread = Askbest − Bidbest
Relative spread = (Spread / Mid) × 10,000  →  basis points
Depth imbalance (band) = (ΣBidSize − ΣAskSize) / (ΣBidSize + ΣAskSize)  →  +1 bid-heavy … −1 ask-heavy
Microprice = (Bid×AskSize + Ask×BidSize) / (BidSize + AskSize)
The microprice leans toward the side with less size — it's a depth-weighted mid that front-runs the naive (bid+ask)/2, because the thin side is where the next print is likeliest.

The cost you actually pay is rarely half the quoted spread. Small orders kiss the top of book; size walks it. That's why the spread you see and the spread you pay are different numbers:

§04
In Practice
Trade the book, not the mid
The one rule: trade toward thin depth and treat a widening spread as a stand-aside signal — never add size into a blowing-out spread.
1

Read asymmetry, not absolutes

Sum cumulative depth within ±0.5% on each side; the lighter side is the likely path. "There's a huge bid wall" means nothing alone — only net depth imbalance versus the other side is the read.

2

Spread regime is risk regime

Tight, stable spread = orderly, size-friendly. A spread widening with no price move means makers are pulling and adverse selection is rising — cut size or stand aside. Don't market in.

3

Size to executable depth

Before sending a market order, sum the resting size you'd actually consume to your price — that's your real slippage, the effective spread. In a thin book, use limits or scale in.

4

Pair depth with flow

Static depth lies — it can be spoofed or pulled. Confirm with the tape: depth that holds while aggressive flow hits it is absorption; depth that vanishes before contact is a spoof.

§07
Common Traps
Where depth-and-spread reads go wrong
The single biggest mistake: trusting displayed depth as if it were real, executable size.
1
Displayed depth ≠ executable depth

Much of the resting size on a crypto book is conditional — it cancels or reprices the instant aggressive flow approaches. Size to what holds when hit, not what shows when calm.

2
Treating the spread as a fixed cost

The spread is a live signal, not a constant tax. A spread that widens with no price move is makers pricing in risk — it leads volatility. Ignore it and you'll add size into the worst fills.

3
Reading depth absolutes instead of asymmetry

A "big bid wall" is meaningless without the other side. A wall with a bigger wall opposite is not support — it's the heavy side of an imbalance leaning the wrong way for you.

4
Mistaking a spoof wall for demand

A large, lone level that never trades and pulls before contact is spoof- or iceberg-bait, not resting demand. Confirm whether it actually absorbs flow before you lean on it.

5
Overhyped: "order-book depth = liquidity"

Honest call — this is a half-truth, and even CME now warns that depth overstates true liquidity. In crypto's fragmented, cancel-heavy books the displayed ladder is a hypothesis, not ground truth. Depth is necessary, not sufficient; pair it with flow and resiliency or it'll get you faded.
