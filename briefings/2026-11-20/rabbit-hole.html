The Cathedral Window That Never Sagged — and the Factory Floor Behind the Thick Bottom

Why old cathedral windows are thicker at the bottom, why glass does not creep at room temperature, and how a myth survived for centuries.

Medieval cathedral windows are often thicker at the bottom, and the popular explanation is that glass is a supercooled liquid that has been oozing downward for centuries. It is not: at room temperature, window glass is so viscous that visible flow would take far longer than the age of the universe, and the thick bottoms come from how the sheets were made and how glaziers set them in the frames.

TL;DR

Stand in front of an old stained-glass window and you may notice the lower edge of each pane looks heavier than the top. For centuries that observation was taken as proof that glass is a very slow liquid, quietly sagging under its own weight. The physics says otherwise. Window glass is an amorphous solid whose viscosity at room temperature is so enormous that any visible flow would need time scales far beyond human history. The real story is a manufacturing process that produced uneven sheets, and a practical installation habit that put the heavy edge at the bottom.

Section 01

The Story

The myth is old enough that it has its own folklore: glass is a liquid that never quite finished setting, so over generations it drifts downward and thickens the bottom of every pane. It is a satisfying picture, and it has the advantage of matching what you can see. Many old windows really are thicker at the bottom. The question is what caused it, and the answer depends on whether gravity can move glass on a human timescale at all.

The modern debunking is largely the work of Edgar Zanotto, a materials scientist at the Federal University of São Carlos in Brazil. In a 1998 paper in the American Journal of Physics titled "Do cathedral glasses flow?", he took viscosity curves for several modern and ancient glass compositions and asked how long a real window would need to deform at ordinary temperatures. His conclusion was that window glass could only flow at room temperature over periods that exceed the limits of human history. A year later, Zanotto and P. K. Gupta published additional remarks in the same journal, revising the room-temperature estimate. The revision moved the number by several orders of magnitude, but it kept the same verdict: the flow is not observable.

Later experimental work pushed the argument from calculation toward measurement. A study of medieval glass compositions reported viscosity values at low temperatures and concluded that the flow story remains a myth. The American Ceramic Society's write-up reports that the measured values differ enormously from the values assumed in older estimates, which were based on modern soda-lime glass. It is a useful reminder that the myth was never tested against the material that actually made the windows.

One detail is worth keeping in mind for the rest of the story. The thickness variation is real, and it is not evenly spread. Some panes are thicker at the bottom, some are thicker at the top, and many are simply uneven. A gravity-driven flow would make every pane thicker at the bottom, and the observed pattern does not look like that. The visible evidence is genuine; the explanation attached to it is not.

Section 02

The Mechanism

Glass is a solid in the practical sense, but its atoms are not arranged in a repeating crystal. Silica forms a random network of linked silicon-oxygen units, and in soda-lime window glass, sodium and calcium ions sit in gaps in that network. The structure is disordered like a liquid's, which is why glass is sometimes called a supercooled liquid. The useful question is not whether the structure is disordered, but how fast the disordered structure can rearrange itself. That rate is set by viscosity.

Viscosity falls steeply as glass is heated. Near the glass transition temperature, which for window glass is roughly 500 to 600 °C, the material changes from effectively rigid to something that can slump and be worked. Below that point, viscosity climbs so fast that the glass behaves as a solid for every practical purpose. Zanotto's approach was to take measured viscosity data at high temperature, extend the curve down to room temperature using the standard empirical fits used in glass science, and then estimate how long it would take a pane to deform by a visible amount. The number that comes out at room temperature is around 10²⁰ Pa·s, a value so high that the time needed for a measurable sag is absurdly long.

The scale check

Zanotto's 1998 model gives a visible flow of about 800 years at 414 °C. At room temperature, the quoted relaxation times run to around 10²³ years in the summaries of the 1998 and 1999 work, which is far beyond the roughly 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years since the Big Bang. The 1999 remarks revised the earlier figure downward by several orders of magnitude, so treat the exact exponent as approximate. The conclusion does not depend on it.

The manufacturing explanation fits the evidence. Much of the medieval and early modern window glass was made by the crown or cylinder method: a glassblower spun or blew a bubble, the bubble was opened and flattened into a disc, and the disc was cut into panes. The disc was thicker near its outer edge, where the glass collected as it spun, and thinner towards the centre. A pane cut from a disc therefore carried the variation with it. A glazier who wanted a stable pane in a frame would often place the heavier edge at the bottom. That single habit is enough to produce the look of a window that has sagged, without any flow at all.

The strongest test is consistency. If gravity were moving the glass, every old window should show the same bias, and every antique pane should be thicker at the bottom. Nick Labino, a glass chemist, made the reverse argument: if colonial windows had flowed this way, ancient glass from Egyptian tombs should be a puddle by now, and it is not. The physics and the historical record point the same direction. The viscosity is high enough that the glass holds its shape, and the thickness pattern is mixed in the way a manufacturing and installation pattern would be, not in the way a slow flow would be.

Section 03

The Connections

Why the myth felt right. The myth is seductive because it converts an observation into a theory with a single cause. Gravity is visible, slow change is intuitive, and a pane that looks heavier at the bottom invites a story about time. Explanations built on one cause are cheaper to hold than explanations built on a production line, a set of craft habits, and a mixed distribution of defects. The same pull toward a tidy story shows up in archaeology, in medical folklore, and in the way people explain their own bad luck.

The honest version of the claim. Some real materials do creep at ordinary temperatures, and it is tempting to borrow that intuition for glass. Lead is the usual example. Lead melts at a low temperature, so at room temperature it sits close enough to its melting point to deform slowly under load, which is why old lead roofing and lead came in stained-glass panels can sag. Glass is different. It has no low-melting ingredient to speak of, and its viscosity at room temperature is far too high for a comparable effect. Knowing which materials creep and which do not is the difference between a sound explanation and a plausible one.

A pattern that is measured, not understood. The window myth has a close analogue in quantitative work. A backtest can show that a strategy performs better on one side of a ranking, and a trader may conclude that the market is behaving in a particular way. The real cause can be a construction artefact: the sample was cut from a set that was already skewed, the data were filtered in a way that favours one side, or the sizing rule happened to pick the thick edge. As with the glass, the pattern is real and the explanation is wrong. The check is the same as Labino's: does the pattern hold across every sample, or only across the ones that were cut from the same disc?

Live now: models that explain the pattern they were fed. Large models trained on enormous text and image corpora often produce confident explanations for patterns that come from data collection rather than from the world. A system that learns that old photographs look a certain way may describe the look as a physical process unless someone checks the production history. The window case is a useful template. Before accepting a mechanism, ask whether the feature shows up uniformly in the population the mechanism predicts, and whether the production process could have created it without any of the proposed cause. Most of the time, the answer will be that the factory was the cause all along.

Further Down the Hole

📖
Do cathedral glasses flow?

Zanotto's 1998 paper in the American Journal of Physics is short, readable, and shows the viscosity arithmetic step by step. It is the place to start if you want to check the claim yourself.

🔬
Additional remarks (1999)

The follow-up by Zanotto and Gupta revised the earlier relaxation estimate downward. It is a good example of a scientist correcting their own number while keeping the conclusion.

🪟
The crown-glass process

The manufacturing story is the real explanation for the thick bottoms. Looking up how crown and cylinder glass were made shows why a disc-cut pane carries its thickness pattern with it.

🧪
Which materials actually creep

Lead and some polymers really do deform at room temperature. Comparing them with silicate glass sharpens the question of when a slow-flow story is physically plausible and when it is not.
