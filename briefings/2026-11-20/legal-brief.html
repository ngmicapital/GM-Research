Update Utah Tells Court CFTC Cannot Upend Kalshi Loss as Prediction-Market Split Deepens

“affirmed the state’s ability to regulate what is clearly sports wagering.” Jeremy Baker, Acting Director, Maryland Lottery and Gaming Control Commission — Reuters, 17 Nov 2026

Update: Utah’s attorneys, in filings reported on 19 November, argued that the CFTC cannot revive its claim to oversee sports offerings on prediction platforms, because the point has already been settled in parallel Utah litigation. The federal government has its own suit against Utah’s enforcement, so the dispute now runs on two tracks.

The Fourth Circuit’s 2-1 ruling on 17 November let Maryland keep enforcing its gambling laws against Kalshi, but it produced no majority rationale. The three judges gave three different answers on whether the contracts are Commodity Exchange Act swaps. Judge DeAndrea Benjamin’s dissent treats them as swaps within exclusive CFTC jurisdiction, which is the position the agency and the platforms share. Kalshi has since dropped its preemptive suit against Louisiana officials without prejudice.

The split now heads toward the Supreme Court, and the CFTC’s 2026 rulemaking on event-contract swaps is still pending.

📎 Historical Precedent: Judge Abelson’s August 2025 denial of Kalshi’s preliminary injunction in Maryland held that Congress had not spoken clearly enough to displace state gambling authority, the same concurrent-authority logic the Fourth Circuit has now accepted.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The preemption contest turns on whether event contracts are “swaps,” not on state gambling law as such. Robinhood is the most exposed listed name, given its Kalshi contract volume, per Prediction News, and Kalshi faces state enforcement in several jurisdictions at once. The Brief’s read: expect a certiorari push and a patchwork of state restrictions in the interim, with the CFTC’s own swap rules likely to do more durable work than the litigation.

🔄 Contrarian Take

CFTC Chair Mike Selig, the lone sitting commissioner, argues that event contracts trade on regulated derivatives exchanges and are therefore preempted from state regulation, as reported by Reuters-syndicated coverage. Nine Democratic senators have pressed the agency to drop its preemption suits, per Prediction News. The Brief’s read: the preemption case is only as strong as the swap characterisation, and a 2-1 panel with three rationales gives the agency no majority precedent to lean on.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Kalshi, valued at $40 billion in a recent funding round per Reuters, is absorbing state orders while it litigates. Robinhood carries listed-equity exposure through its Kalshi volume, and Coinbase is named among the operators the CFTC directive covers. The Brief’s read: state-by-state geofencing is the operating cost to watch, and it favours firms with the deepest compliance budgets.

SEC Charges Eight Entities in $37M Fake Crypto Platform Scheme Targeting US Investors

“The SEC continues to seek out and expose fraudulent investment scams that prey on U.S. investors,” Laura D’Allaird, Chief, Cyber and Emerging Technologies Unit, SEC Division of Enforcement — SEC Press Release 2026-115, 19 Nov 2026

The SEC on 19 November charged eight Colorado-based entities, which it says were likely run from overseas, with defrauding US investors of more than $37 million. The complaint alleges violations of Section 10(b) of the Exchange Act and Rule 10b-5, and for some defendants a breach of Section 207 of the Advisers Act. The scheme ran in four adviser-platform pairs from August 2024 through May 2026, with operators abandoning each pair after a few months.

Victims were reached through social-media advertisements and moved into group and private chats, then funnelled to a supposed crypto trading platform where they believed they were buying real assets. The defendants forged SEC certificates and pointed skeptical investors to filings they claimed were made with the SEC, FINRA and FinCEN.

📎 Historical Precedent: The SEC’s FY2026 annual results (10 Nov) cited three alleged crypto trading platforms and four investment clubs that defrauded retail investors of at least $14 million, so this is a continuing pattern of platform-impersonation fraud rather than a new theory.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The charging theory is antifraud (10(b) and 10b-5) plus adviser-conduct liability, with no registration-based claim, which makes this a clean case for the Cyber and Emerging Technologies Unit. The losers are the impersonated brands and any intermediary that onboarded these accounts. The Brief’s read: fraud-only theories are the agency’s clearest lane under the current Commission, and they leave the registration debate untouched.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The SEC’s annual tally gave no separate count for crypto-related cases, so headline fraud figures can overstate how much crypto enforcement is happening in aggregate, as TokenPost noted in its coverage of the annual results. The Brief’s read: the counter-argument is weak on these facts, because fake platforms have no licence to defend, so the case says little about how the Commission will treat licensed exchanges.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Depository institutions are being drawn into scam monitoring. PYMNTS reports that banks see customers moving money into crypto businesses or scam-linked beneficiaries, which raises the monitoring cost for banks and makes traceable flows a differentiator for exchanges. The Brief’s read: blockchain-analytics vendors such as Chainalysis and Elliptic gain demand, though neither has a listed pure-play in these sources.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish Blockchain-analytics demand — private vendors only; no listed pure-play cited in the sources.

🔴 Bearish Unregistered offshore platform operators — no listed exposure; a sector-level signal only.

⚪ Neutral JPM — transaction-monitoring costs rise, with no direct earnings hit identified.

📋 What To Do — Before 1 July
TraderVerify any new venue’s registration before depositing, and avoid any platform reached through group-chat referrals.
CounselReview Section 10(b) and Advisers Act Section 207 exposure for any adviser that introduces clients to third-party platforms.
ComplianceAdd checks for claimed SEC, FINRA and FinCEN filings to onboarding and escalate any unverifiable registration claims.
FounderPublish a verified-platform list and warn customers about impersonators before a regulator or journalist does.

SEC Enforcement Filings Fall to the Fewest Since 1991, With Crypto Fraud Highlighted

Chair Paul Atkins said enforcement actions and financial remedies are important activity measures but not the only or most telling ones. Paul S. Atkins, SEC Chair — as reported by TokenPost, 11 Nov 2026 [paraphrased]

The SEC reported 348 enforcement actions for fiscal 2026, down 23.7% from 456 a year earlier, with 235 standalone actions against 303 in fiscal 2025. A Reuters review of agency records places the total at the fewest since 1991, Atkins’ first full fiscal year as chair. Ordered monetary relief reached $1.095 billion, made up of $752 million in disgorgement and prejudgment interest and $343 million in civil penalties, and about $731 million was returned to more than 250,000 harmed investors.

The release highlighted crypto matters, including a bitcoin-mining founder accused of misappropriating $48.5 million and an audit matter tied to FTX, but it gave no separate count for crypto cases. The SEC’s new Office of Super Intelligence and Analytics uses blockchain and financial-flow analysis, which points to fewer but more data-driven cases.

📎 Historical Precedent: The 456-action FY2025 total is the comparison base, and the agency’s figure differs from an earlier 308-action estimate because the two use different counting methods.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Lower volume with a higher share of individual-defendant fraud cases means case selection, not sweeping registration theories, is the enforcement signal to read. Two-thirds of standalone actions named at least one individual, which raises personal-liability exposure for principals of crypto businesses. The Brief’s read: compliance budgets should follow fraud-detection and individual accountability rather than registration remediation.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Registered exchanges and custodians face fewer registration-driven actions, while fraud-facing intermediaries face more tracing demands. The Brief’s read: firms with traceable flow data and documented onboarding controls are best placed for the next wave of fraud cases.

SEC Staff Treats Decentralisation and Functionality as Separate Tests in New Crypto FAQs

Unaffiliated participants in a broad governance system, with no agreement to act together outside it, are generally not treated as a single central party, according to SEC staff. SEC Division of Corporation Finance, crypto-asset FAQs update, 2 Nov 2026 [paraphrased]

On 2 November the SEC’s Division of Corporation Finance added three questions to its crypto-asset FAQs, extending the agency’s 2026 classification framework. The most consequential point is that functionality and decentralisation are distinct inquiries, so a network can be functional without any single central party controlling it. The reasoning, as summarised by Kelman.Law, is directly relevant to prediction-market designated contract markets and reaches any DCM offering crypto derivatives through state-sensitive onboarding.

The same week, on 3 November, staff issued a no-action letter for the native token of a privacy-focused blockchain used by institutional market participants, in response to a request filed on 30 October. Latham & Watkins tracks it as part of a run of staff relief that narrows enforcement risk for specific fact patterns.

📎 Historical Precedent: The joint SEC–CFTC interpretive release of 17 March 2026 superseded the SEC’s 2019 staff-level digital asset framework and binds both agencies’ enforcement and supervisory staff, so these FAQs refine a binding baseline rather than create a new one.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The FAQs give token issuers a documented staff pathway to argue decentralisation, but they do not supply a safe harbour. Each governance design still needs a fact-specific classification, and the no-action letters are fact-bound. The Brief’s read: counsel should treat the FAQ as a checklist for governance documentation, not as a clearance.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Institutional privacy-network token issuers gain the most from the November relief, and designated contract markets offering crypto derivatives gain a clearer classification path. The Brief’s read: projects with broad but unaffiliated governance are the likeliest beneficiaries, while projects with a clear core team still face the central-party test.
