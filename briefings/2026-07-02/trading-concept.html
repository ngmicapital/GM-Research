Alpha — Iceberg Refresh

Thursday, 2 July 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue 66 11 min read
TL;DR

Iceberg refresh is the operational tell that separates a genuine hidden order from a random thick level — watch a price absorb trade after trade at the same visible quantity, and if that quantity reappears instantly and identically after every partial fill, you're not looking at static depth, you're looking at a reserve order reloading in real time. The visible "tip" everyone talks about is the least useful part; the refresh cadence — same size, same price, same speed — is what tells you whether size is still behind the level or the reserve just ran out. Trade with a live reload underneath you, not against it, until the refresh stops.

§01
What It Is
The reload, not the tip
An iceberg's tip is just the display quantity; the tell that it's real hidden size is the refresh — the same quantity reappearing at the same price after every partial fill.

Everyone who's read a glossary entry knows an iceberg order shows a small display quantity — the "tip" — while a much larger reserve quantity sits hidden behind it. That definition is table stakes; we covered it as a standalone concept three weeks ago (Issue for 2026-06-12). What actually makes an iceberg tradeable is a different observation entirely: the refresh. When the visible tip gets fully hit, the exchange automatically pulls more size from the reserve and reposts it at the same price — often within the same matching cycle. If you watch that price level absorb trade after trade and the displayed size keeps reappearing identical to what it was before, you're not looking at a coincidentally thick level. You're watching a hidden order reload in real time.

This distinction matters because a thick static level and a reloading iceberg look identical in a single snapshot — same size sitting on the book — but behave completely differently under pressure. A static level depletes: hit it enough and it thins out, then breaks. A reloading iceberg doesn't thin — it snaps back to full size after every fill, and it keeps doing that until the reserve itself runs out, which you cannot see coming until it happens. The refresh cadence, not the tip size, is the actual information content.

Institutional desks and execution algos use icebergs specifically to avoid signaling size — showing a 5,000-contract sell order invites front-running, but showing 40 contracts that reloads twenty times achieves the same fill with far less market impact. The refresh pattern is the residue that discretion leaves behind, and it's readable by anyone watching time & sales closely enough.

Remember The tip tells you size is present. The refresh tells you size is still there after you tried to take it — that's the difference between decoration and a wall.
§02
Mechanics
How an exchange actually refreshes a hidden order
Exchanges replace filled display quantity from a hidden reserve automatically, and whether the refreshed slice keeps the same order ID or gets a new one tells you if you're watching a native or synthetic iceberg.

Mechanically, the refresh is not discretionary — it's an exchange matching-engine behavior, and the details differ by venue and by who's holding the reserve. CME Group's own Display Quantity Order documentation splits icebergs into two categories that behave differently on refresh, and the distinction is directly useful for reading order flow:

Native iceberg = exchange holds the reserve → refreshed slice keeps the same Order ID as the original
where the matching engine itself manages the reload — you're watching one persistent order, just partially disclosed
Synthetic iceberg = a trading algo (ISV) holds the reserve off-book → each refresh is submitted as a new order with a new Order ID
where the "iceberg" behavior is simulated client-side by resubmitting fresh limit orders — functionally identical on the tape, structurally different in the order-ID stream

For a retail or prop trader without direct MBO (Market-by-Order) feed access, this ID distinction is invisible — you can't query order IDs from a standard Level 2 or time & sales feed. What you can see, and what actually matters for trading, is the behavioral signature both types leave: a level that absorbs a print, the book depth at that price drops to near-zero for a beat, then jumps back to the same number. Do that reset-to-identical-size three or more times in a row and you've got a statistically meaningful reload pattern — not noise, not a coincidence of two large resting orders happening to be similar size.

SignalStatic thick levelReloading iceberg
Behavior under repeated hitsThins progressively, then breaksSnaps back to identical size each time

That last row is the trap and the edge simultaneously: a reloading iceberg gives you zero warning before the reserve runs dry. The tenth reload looks exactly like the first, right up until there's an eleventh hit and nothing comes back.

§04
In Practice
Trading the reload, not guessing at it
Count the refreshes, not the size — three or more identical reloads at one level is a real signal; a level that reloads once and then breaks was never an iceberg worth trading around.
1

Count reloads before you trust the level

One refresh is a coincidence; two could still be two separate resting orders. Three or more identical reloads at one price, on one side, is the threshold where you're statistically looking at a genuine reserve order rather than noise.

2

Trade with the reload, exit before you'd be fighting it

A level that's reloaded four times is functionally a floor (or ceiling) — fade moves away from it, or use it as invalidation for a position in its direction. Don't hold through a fifth test hoping for a sixth reload; you have no way to know the reserve size.

3

Watch refresh speed, not just refresh count

A level that reloads instantly (sub-second, same matching cycle) is a native iceberg or a very fast algo — high conviction. A level that takes several seconds to "refill" is more likely a human or slower bot replacing a filled limit order — treat it as weaker evidence.

4

The break, when it comes, is often violent

Because reload masks the true remaining size, exhaustion isn't gradual — the level simply stops refilling and the next aggressive print goes straight through with none of the resistance the prior five prints met. Size your stop for a gap-like move through the level, not a slow grind.

Worked example — fading a failed reload
SetupBTC bid at $58,400 reloads to ~40 BTC display four times across 90 minutes (30 Jun, ~19:00–20:30 UTC)
Entry triggerFifth aggressive sell burst hits $58,400 and the level does not refresh within 2–3 seconds — first sign of exhaustion

Stop$58,520 (back above the former reload zone — invalidates the exhaustion read)

§07
Common Traps
Where reload-reading goes wrong
The single biggest mistake is trading the first refresh as confirmation — one reload is noise; the pattern only becomes tradeable on the third or fourth identical reload.
1
Trading the first refresh as confirmation

One reload proves nothing — resting size at a round number is common without any hidden reserve. Wait for three or more identical reloads before treating the level as structurally defended.

2
Assuming a reload guarantees the next reload

Every refresh looks identical right up until the reserve is empty — there is no visible warning shot. Size stops for a level that can vanish instantly, not gradually.

3
Confusing iceberg refresh with generic "order book resiliency"

Resiliency (how fast any liquidity reloads market-wide after a sweep) and iceberg refresh (one specific order reposting identical size) look similar on a heatmap but come from different mechanics — resiliency is a market-wide statistic, refresh is a single-order signature. Don't conflate a resilient book with a specific hidden order.

4
Retail platforms rarely give you true MBO data

Standard Level 2 feeds show aggregated size at a price, not individual order IDs — so you're inferring reload behavior from repeated depth-and-time patterns, not directly observing an order ID persist. Be honest about the resolution limit of your data.

5
[Overhyped] "Iceberg detection" as a paid signal service

Several retail tools sell real-time "iceberg alerts" as a premium edge. The underlying pattern — watch a level, count the reloads — is free and visible on any decent time & sales feed; you're paying for convenience and visualization, not for information you couldn't derive yourself with patience. Worth it if it saves you screen time, not worth it if marketed as an alpha unlock nobody else has access to.
