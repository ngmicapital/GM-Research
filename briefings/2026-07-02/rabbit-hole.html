The Dunes That Sing

How a handful of sand grains, synchronized by gravity, can produce a roar louder than a rock concert.

Certain desert dunes emit a sustained, bass roar — up to 105 decibels, audible 10 kilometres away — when sand avalanches down their faces. The sound is not a freak echo or wind effect: it is caused by millions of microscopic grain collisions locking into a shared frequency, turning a slipping slope into an accidental musical instrument.

TL;DR

In about three dozen locations worldwide — Morocco, Oman, Nevada, Patagonia, the Gobi — certain dunes emit a deep, sustained drone when sand avalanches down their slip faces. The phenomenon puzzled Marco Polo, baffled Darwin, and eluded physicists for a century. The mechanism turns out to be grain synchronization: as sand grains of near-identical size tumble and collide, their individual shocks — each inaudible — phase-lock into a shared frequency determined by grain diameter alone. The result is a natural instrument that can hold a single note for several minutes, loud enough that normal conversation becomes impossible.

Section 01

The Story

Sometime around 1271, Marco Polo crossed the Lop Desert on the edge of the Taklamakan. He wrote of sounds that seemed to fill the air with "all kinds of musical instruments, and also of drums and the clash of arms" — sounds that appeared from nowhere and vanished as suddenly. He attributed them to desert spirits who lured unwary travellers from their caravans. His explanation was wrong. His observations were precise.

The song of sirens who lure travelers to a waterless doom, the tolling of underground bells in sand-engulfed monasteries.
— Ralph A. Bagnold, The Physics of Blown Sand and Desert Dunes, 1941

In 1835, Charles Darwin encountered the same phenomenon at a dune near Copiapó in Chile, locally called El Bramador — "the Roarer." He admitted he had not paid close enough attention at the time. Earlier European naturalists Seetzen and Ehrenberg had noted similar sounds from the sands near Mount Sinai. These were not isolated curiosities: the phenomenon occurs in roughly thirty locations scattered across the world's great deserts — Morocco, Oman, Kazakhstan, Nevada, Inner Mongolia, Patagonia — and in each place the sand produces the same thing: a sustained, resonant bass drone, louder than a jackhammer, sometimes lasting several minutes after the triggering avalanche has stopped.

The British Army officer Ralph Bagnold, who became the twentieth century's foremost sand physicist, described experiencing it in the Libyan Desert in the 1920s: the sound started so suddenly and so loudly that normal conversation became impossible. Bagnold founded the discipline of quantitative desert geomorphology, but even he could not fully explain what was generating the sound. The question sat unanswered for most of the century that followed.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The key insight arrived in stages. Stéphane Douady and colleagues in Paris showed in 2006 that the frequency of a booming dune's sound is controlled almost entirely by grain diameter. The relationship is not intuitive: smaller grains produce higher frequencies. A Moroccan dune whose grains average 160 microns booms at roughly 105 Hz — a low G-sharp. Omani dunes carry mixed grain sizes between 150 and 300 microns and produce a chord spanning nine notes simultaneously, each sub-population singing at its own pitch. In laboratory experiments, when sand was sieved to a perfectly uniform grain size, the dune emitted a single, pure tone.

The Grain Formula

Douady's team derived the governing relationship as approximately Γ ≈ 0.4 × √(g/d), where g is gravitational acceleration and d is grain diameter. This means a grain twice as wide produces a frequency roughly 70% lower — which is why dune songs are always in the bass register, and why no two booming dunes sound quite the same.

The underlying mechanism is collision synchronization. As sand avalanches down a slip face, each grain tumbles and collides with its neighbours. Each individual collision produces a shock lasting microseconds — far too faint and brief to hear. But in a dry, well-sorted dune, grains of nearly identical size collide at nearly identical rates. These micro-impacts begin to phase-lock: they align in time, like audience members clapping randomly who gradually synchronize into a single beat. Millions of synchronized collisions summing together produce a coherent pressure wave — sound — at the collision frequency. The dune is not amplifying a sound that already exists. It is collectively creating one.

A 2012 experiment by Dagois-Bohy, Courrech du Pont, and Douady clinched this picture. They demonstrated that booming could be produced on a hard plate in a laboratory — with no dune beneath the sand at all. The dune's interior structure is not the source. The sound emerges from the shearing surface layer itself, a few centimetres thick, where grains are in active motion. Caltech researchers Nathalie Vriend and Melany Hunt found that within the actual dune, this active layer sits over a stiffer, compacted substrate that acts as a waveguide — trapping and amplifying the waves — but the grain synchronization at the surface is what initiates and sustains the note.

Section 03

The Connections

The specificity of the recipe. Booming requires a remarkably narrow set of conditions. Grains must be between 0.1 and 0.3 mm in diameter — overwhelmingly quartz — polished to near-spherical smoothness by long aeolian transport, sorted to a narrow size band, and completely dry. A single overnight dew is enough to break synchronization by adding capillary adhesion between grains that prevents free collision. This is why most dunes are silent: the Sahara has approximately 2.5 million square kilometres of dunes, but only a handful produce the sound. The recipe is geologically old — booming dunes tend to be ancient megadunes where millennia of wind have done the sorting work.

Phase-locking beyond physics. The grain synchronization mechanism is the same class of phenomenon as fireflies flashing in unison, neurons firing in concert, or pacemaker cells in the heart. In each case, coupled oscillators — elements that influence each other's timing — spontaneously organize from noise into coherence. The mathematician Steven Strogatz, in his book Sync (2003), identifies this as one of nature's deepest tendencies: systems of weakly coupled oscillators will often self-organize toward a common frequency without any conductor. The booming dune is one of the few places where you can hear that process happening in real time.

The geological archive. The frequency a dune sings at encodes information about its grain population — effectively a phonetic fingerprint of its sedimentary history. Researchers have used this to infer the age and provenance of dune sand without extracting core samples. The same logic applies in reverse: palaeoclimatologists studying ancient dunes in rock record can estimate past grain-size distributions and therefore past wind regimes from the geometry alone, informed by the relationship between grain diameter and acoustic frequency. The dune is, in a sense, a geological instrument that kept playing even after its sand was lithified.

Markets, models, and emergent order. The grain synchronization story is a live example of why emergent behaviour confounds simple models. For decades, the proposed explanations for booming sands invoked the dune's gross structure — its size, shape, internal layers — rather than the grain-scale interactions. The correct answer was at the microscale all along, invisible to measurements taken at the macroscale. This is a recurring pattern in complex systems: markets, traffic flows, disease spread, and neural networks all contain dynamics where aggregate behaviour emerges from individual interactions that are individually unremarkable. The booming dune is a rare case where the emergent phenomenon is both dramatic and exactly derivable from first principles — a model system that vindicates the micro-to-macro approach.

Further Down the Hole

🎵
Hear it yourself

NOVA / PBS has a short documentary segment on booming sands with field recordings from Sand Mountain, Nevada — one of the most accessible booming dune sites in North America. The contrast between the silence before the avalanche and the onset of the drone is striking.

📖
Bagnold's original account

<em>The Physics of Blown Sand and Desert Dunes</em> (1941) remains in print and is unusually readable for a physics text — Bagnold wrote for a general audience as much as a scientific one. The chapter on musical sands is where his quote about sirens and underground bells appears in full context.

🔬
The lab experiment that settled the debate

Dagois-Bohy, Courrech du Pont, and Douady (2012) in <em>Geophysical Research Letters</em> showed booming on a flat plate with no underlying dune. It is a short paper and freely readable — the method is elegantly simple.

📡
Steven Strogatz on coupled oscillators

<em>Sync: The Emerging Science of Spontaneous Order</em> (2003) builds the broader theory of phase-locking that the booming dune exemplifies — covering everything from cardiac rhythms to power grids. If the grain story makes you want the general principle, this is the place to go.

🏜️
Khongoryn Els, Mongolia

The Khongoryn Els dunes in the Gobi — locally called the "Singing Dunes" — are among the most reliably active booming dune systems in the world and are accessible to visitors. The dunes reach 300 metres in height and boom most reliably in dry summer months after a sustained wind builds a fresh slip face.
