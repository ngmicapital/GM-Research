Alpha — Orderbook Depth & Spread

Wednesday, 18 November 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue 7 11 min read
TL;DR

Orderbook depth is the resting size stacked on each side of price, and it decides how far a market order walks before it fills. The spread only prices the first unit; depth prices every unit after it. Practically, check the depth band against your order size before you trade, because a tight quote over a thin book is the cheapest-looking trap on the screen, and BTC's November tape showed the book thinning just as sellers arrived.

§01
What It Is
Two numbers, two different questions
Depth is the resting size on each side of price, and the spread is the gap between the best bid and best ask.

Orderbook depth is the resting size stacked on each side of the price, and it tells you how far a market order will move price before the orders run out. The spread is the gap between the best bid and the best ask. It is the toll for crossing the top of the book, and it is the number most traders glance at first. My position: the spread answers a smaller question than depth does, and reading a tight spread as proof of liquidity is the most common mistake on the screen.

Depth is measured as cumulative size across price levels, usually inside a band around mid such as 0.5%, 1% or 2%. A book can show a narrow spread with only a handful of coins resting near mid, so a large order fills across a ladder of progressively worse prices. Coinglass frames depth as how many buy and sell orders sit within a given price range, and that range is the part that decides execution. Each exchange keeps its own book, so depth on one venue is not the depth of the whole market.

Best bid / askHighest resting buy and lowest resting sell: the top of book.
SpreadBest ask minus best bid: the cost of crossing right now.
MidAverage of best bid and best ask: the reference for depth bands.
Depth bandResting size within a fixed distance of mid, such as plus or minus 1%.
Remember If you only remember one thing: the spread tells you what the first unit costs, while depth tells you what the hundredth unit costs.
§02
Mechanics
From resting size to your fill price
A market order consumes resting levels in price order, so the book's shape sets the average fill and the impact cost.

Depth is built by summing resting limit orders. Each price level carries a size, and you accumulate those sizes outward from mid on each side to draw the depth curve. A market order then eats levels in price order, best price first, until its size is filled. The fill price is the size-weighted average of the levels it touched, and the gap between that average and mid is the impact cost you actually pay.

Spread = best ask − best bid
The top-of-book cost of crossing right now. It says nothing about the size behind the top level.
Depth(1%) = Σ size of bids at or above mid × 0.99 + Σ size of asks at or below mid × 1.01
Resting liquidity inside a 1% band. Compare it with your order in coins, not just in dollars.
Impact = (VWAP of fill − mid) ÷ mid
VWAP of fill is the size-weighted average price across every level your order consumed.

Two books can share an identical spread and produce very different impact. If the first lots fill at a tight price and the depth then falls away sharply, the average fill drifts quickly. Depth is also never fixed. It is posted, pulled and re-posted, so any single snapshot is one frame of a moving picture.

MeasureWhat it answersBlind spot
Top-of-book spreadWhat does the first unit cost to cross now?Silent on size beyond the best level.
Band depth (±1%)How much can rest before my order moves price?Resting size can be pulled before you arrive.
Impact (VWAP vs mid)What will my actual order cost?Needs the order size and a current snapshot.

§04
In Practice
Size against depth before you click
Judge a trade by the depth it needs, not by the spread it shows.
1

Size the order against the band

Convert your order to coins and compare it with the resting size inside the 0.5% and 1% bands. If your order is a material share of the band, expect the fill to walk.

2

Read the slope, not the single wall

A steep cliff of size past the top two levels tells you more than one large bid sitting at the top. A gentle slope means each extra coin costs more.

3

Require the size to persist

Size present across several consecutive snapshots carries more weight than size that appears and disappears. The spoofing issue from 16 November covers why that matters.

4

Switch to a limit when the walk is steep

If the band is thin, set a limit at the worst price you will accept rather than sending a market order. You give up certainty of fill to cap the walk.

Worked: 5 BTC market buy against an illustrative book
LevelSize (BTC)Price
Ask 10.8$64,420
Ask 21.2$64,450
Ask 32.5$64,500
Ask 40.5$64,560

Average fill is (0.8 × 64,420 + 1.2 × 64,450 + 2.5 × 64,500 + 0.5 × 64,560) ÷ 5 = $64,481.20. Against the top ask that is $61.20 per BTC, or $306 across 5 BTC. The top-of-book spread looked like $10, but the walk cost about 6.6 times that spread. Mid in this example is $64,415.

Timeframe fit: depth is an execution read for entries and exits on the 1 to 15 minute scale; use the 4H tape for directional bias and the book only to decide how to get filled.

§07
Common Traps
Where a clean-looking book misleads
Treating a narrow spread as proof that a large order will fill cleanly.
1
Reading a tight spread as liquidity

A narrow spread only prices the first unit. Check the band depth against your order size first, and treat the spread as the smallest of your costs.

2
Trusting a single large wall

One big bid at the top is the easiest thing in the book to fake or pull. Require the size to persist across several snapshots and across the slope, not just one level.

3
Using one venue as the whole market

Exchange books differ sharply, as the Talos figure and the Hyperliquid perp book show. Compare the venue you trade with at least one other before sizing up.

4
Overhyped: depth charts as forecasts

Depth tells you the cost and capacity of trading now, not the direction of the next move. Blofin's own guide calls the order book not a prediction tool, and that is the honest read.
