The Solid That Has Been Dripping Since 1930

A funnel of black pitch, a 1927 classroom demonstration, and the world's longest-running lab experiment, which has produced nine drops and no witnessed fall.

A black tar that shatters when struck with a hammer is in fact a liquid, and a University of Queensland physicist set up a funnel in 1927 to prove it, a demonstration that has been dripping ever since. Nine drops have fallen since the stem was cut in 1930, no person has been recorded watching one separate, and the tenth is still forming.

TL;DR

Pitch, a black material distilled from coal or wood tar, looks and behaves like a solid at room temperature, and it will crack under a hammer blow. In 1927 Thomas Parnell, the first professor of physics at the University of Queensland, sealed a sample in a glass funnel to show his students that it was really a fluid. The stem was opened in 1930, and the drips have been almost impossible to catch ever since: the first fell about eight years later, the ninth separated in April 2014, and the tenth is still forming. The reason is viscosity, and the reason the timing is so unpredictable is temperature.

Section 01

The Story

Almost a century ago, a physics professor at the University of Queensland wanted his students to confront a fact that their senses rejected. He heated a batch of pitch, a black, tar-derived material that was once smeared on boats to keep water out, poured it into a glass funnel, and let it cool. At room temperature pitch feels like a solid and fractures under a sharp blow, so the demonstration was really an argument that appearances mislead. The funnel was meant to be a lesson, not a long-term project.

Parnell waited for three years, and in 1930 he cut the sealed stem so the material could leave under gravity. Nothing dramatic followed. The first drop did not detach until about 1938, eight years after the cut, and Parnell lived to record only two drops before he died in 1948. Custody then passed to John Mainstone in 1961, who guarded the funnel for decades and also died without seeing a drop fall. The ninth drop separated in April 2014, and by then the funnel had outlived the two men who had cared for it most.

about an atom a second
— Prof. Andrew White, University of Queensland, as reported by ABC News, 8 November 2026 (describing the speed of the pitch's motion)

The viscosity figure is where the story gets messy. A 1980s estimate put pitch at roughly 100 billion times the viscosity of water, while figures published after the eighth drop in November 2000 put it near 230 billion times. These are not two careful measurements of one fixed quantity. They were produced in different decades, from a funnel whose temperature has changed, so the multiplier should be read as an order-of-magnitude figure rather than a precise constant. The most defensible claim is that pitch is many orders of magnitude more viscous than honey, and that its flow rate depends on the weather.

Section 02

The Mechanism

Viscosity is resistance to gradual deformation. Water pours because its small molecules slide past one another with little friction; honey resists because its molecules are larger and entangled. Pitch is an amorphous mixture of large organic molecules, with no crystal lattice of the kind that gives salt or ice a grain along which they can shear. That disorder explains both halves of the paradox. Under a sudden blow the material has no time to rearrange, so it behaves elastically and cracks like glass. Under a slow, steady load, the same molecules have time to creep past one another. The hammer and the funnel are testing the same material on two different clocks.

The numbers that matter

Stem cut: 1930. First drop: about 1938 (eight years later). Ninth drop: April 2014, the first one caught on camera. Viscosity estimate: about 230 billion times water (2000), against an earlier estimate of about 100 billion. Tenth drop: still forming as of November 2026, with a fresh beaker waiting beneath it.

At the molecular level, flow in a dense, disordered fluid happens through thermally activated rearrangements. A segment of molecule gets enough thermal energy to hop into a neighbouring gap, and the rate of these hops falls steeply as the material cools. Gravity supplies a constant but tiny bias, so the net motion is the small surplus of downward hops over upward ones. A drop therefore has to build up as a neck that thins and stretches over years, and then separates. This is why one drop takes roughly a decade to form and yet falls in a fraction of a second: the stretching is the slow part, and the snap is the fast part. The fall itself is almost instantaneous; what takes years is the stretching that makes the fall possible.

Temperature is the lever that the experiment cannot control, and that is the most practical part of the mechanism. The funnel is kept in a display case rather than a climate-controlled laboratory, so the flow tracks seasonal temperature. Air-conditioning installed in nearby lecture theatres from the 1990s cooled the pitch and slowed it further, and the gap between the seventh and eighth drops stretched to over twelve years. Because viscosity is so sensitive to temperature, the funnel behaves less like a clock and more like a thermometer that happens to drip.

The ninth drop shows that the event is a boundary, not an object. Rather than falling freely, it grew until it touched the eighth drop below, which is why it was counted only after the fact, and the beaker holding the earlier drops had to be replaced so that new drops would not fuse with them. Nobody saw the separation as it happened, and the record of the first eight drops rests largely on the observer's daily check and on what had changed in the beaker. A drop is recognised by the evidence it leaves behind, which is a surprisingly modest way to certify a physical event.

Section 03

The Connections

The solid that isn't. Pitch belongs to a family of materials that are amorphous, meaning they lack a repeating internal order, and that family is where the solid-versus-liquid boundary becomes blurry. Ordinary window glass is the usual cousin in this argument. Physicists generally treat it as an amorphous solid on any human timescale, and the popular claim that old church windows have sagged into thicker bottoms is a myth, not an observation. The pitch funnel is an honest test of the same question: what counts as a solid depends on how long you are willing to watch.

Why the obvious explanations fall short. The intuitive answer is that the pitch is simply stuck, and the funnel is a curiosity with a trivially explained delay. That misses the point. The flow is real, measurable and temperature-dependent, so the delay is not a stuck valve but a genuine property of the material. A related case is the basilisk lizard, which can sprint across a pond surface because each slap of its foot briefly makes the water behave like a solid, while the viscosity of ordinary liquid pulls it back between steps. Both systems show that rate of loading decides whether a fluid acts like a solid, and a simple 'it is a solid' or 'it is a liquid' label throws that information away.

The ironic coda. A teaching demonstration built to be watched has produced nine events that almost nobody saw. The most patient custodians both died before the first drop they were waiting for, and the lesson survived them because it was recorded in beakers and logbooks rather than in anyone's memory of a single moment. The Ig Nobel Prize awarded in 2005 to Parnell and Mainstone recognised a joke with a serious core: a physics experiment can be meaningful precisely because nobody is paying attention to any given hour of it. The experiment's value is in its record, not its spectacle.

The live bet. The tenth drop is still forming, and it has become a small prediction-market question. A Manifold Markets contract on its timing drew a number of traders by April 2026, which means people are now pricing the moment of separation of a process they cannot watch in real time. The same structure appears in markets and in AI systems. Positions that look rigid can be flowing underneath, liquidity can vanish at the moment a stress is applied rather than gradually, and long-running models drift for a long time before the break is visible. The pitch is a reminder that a slow process can be perfectly real while still being invisible to the people who most need to see it.

Further Down the Hole

🔬
Watch the funnel yourself

The University of Queensland keeps the experiment on public display in the Parnell Building foyer and runs a live video stream. Checking it for a few minutes makes the scale of the wait concrete.

📄
Old and new puzzles in fluid dynamics

An arXiv review of unresolved problems in fluid dynamics places the pitch drop beside the basilisk lizard and other cases where a fluid acts like a solid. It is a useful next step for the mechanism.

🏛️
The Aberystwyth funnel

A pitch-drop experiment at Aberystwyth University in Wales was rediscovered in 2014 and dates from 1914, thirteen years before the Queensland setup. It is a good reminder that the experiment has rivals.

🧭
The Trinity College sibling

Trinity College Dublin's pitch drop was noticed forming in 2013 and gives a second, independent test of the same material. Comparing the two shows how much the timing depends on the specific sample and its temperature.
