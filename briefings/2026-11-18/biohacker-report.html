Section 01
Longevity & Performance Science

Ergothioneine is a sulfur-containing compound that humans obtain almost entirely from food, and it is taken up into tissues by a dedicated transporter. In a Frontiers in Aging study, five distinct lifespan-extending treatments all raised it in the brain and plasma of male UM-HET3 mice. Each tissue also showed its own set of standout metabolites, so the overlap is specific to ergothioneine and lipid remodelling rather than a general stress signature. The mechanistic reading is that these interventions may converge on a shared redox-buffering pathway. The caveat is that metabolomic markers show association with treatment; they do not show that raising ergothioneine causes longer life.

Five lifespan treatments, one antioxidant signal: ergothioneine in UM-HET3 mice
Mech

Bretton Badenoch and colleagues profiled multi-tissue metabolomes after five longevity interventions in male UM-HET3 mice. Ergothioneine rose in both brain and plasma across the treatments, and it was the most influential brain metabolite for predicting which animals had received a longevity treatment. Survival outcomes diverged between the interventions even though the metabolic overlap was shared, which argues that the marker is a useful readout rather than the whole explanation.

Source: Frontiers in Aging (2026), DOI 10.3389/fragi.2026.1972744

Practical takeaway: there is no human dosing evidence here, so do not change intake on this basis. If you eat mushrooms regularly, they are the main dietary source and there is nothing to act on yet.

Protein restriction: a 350-study review challenges the high-protein default
Expert

A major review covering more than 350 studies argues that eating less protein may slow ageing and improve metabolic health, and it questions the widely held view that more protein is always better. The evidence is largely observational and population-level, and the review design and primary journal were not confirmed in this run, so treat the claim as a hypothesis rather than a settled finding.

Source: ScienceDaily summary of the 2026 review
⚡ Implication: Do not add ergothioneine to the stack on mouse data; log it as a biomarker to revisit when human intervention trials report. For protein, keep intake steady through the current hypertrophy block and wait for the full review before changing targets.
Section 02
Training & Performance
ACSM 2026 position stand: about 10 weekly sets per muscle for hypertrophy
Guide

The American College of Sports Medicine’s first update in 17 years draws on 137 systematic reviews covering more than 30,000 participants. For hypertrophy it recommends higher weekly volume, around 10 sets per muscle group, and it finds that consistency matters more than a complicated programme. It also reports that elastic bands, bodyweight work and home routines produce meaningful gains in strength and size.

Source: ACSM Position Stand, Med Sci Sports Exerc 2026;58(4):851-872

Practitioner mapping: on a PPL split, chest, shoulders and triceps each get roughly two sessions a week, so a baseline of about 10 hard sets per muscle per week is achievable without extra days. Add sets only when recovery holds.

Narrative review: 12 to 20 weekly sets as a starting range
Expert

A 2026 narrative review covering studies from 2020 to September 2026 proposes a fatigue-management framework for trained lifters. Pooled analyses show a non-linear relationship between weekly volume and hypertrophy, with 12 to 20 weekly sets per muscle group as a defensible starting range. The review also emphasises proximity to failure and exercise selection, which lines up with the ACSM recommendation at the lower end of that range.

Source: Journal of Functional Morphology and Kinesiology 2026;11(4):454
⚡ Update: a 120% volume jump did not beat a 20% jump for muscle growth
RCT

In resistance-trained adults, an abrupt increase in weekly volume of 120% did not attenuate hypertrophy compared with a modest 20% increase. Whole-muscle cross-sectional area favoured the larger jump in one measure, but fibre-level size did not increase significantly in either protocol, and acute anabolic signalling was not enhanced under the higher-volume protocol. The mechanism here is that the body does not reward a sudden spike in workload with proportionally more growth.

Source: Journal of Applied Physiology 2026, VOL120 vs VOL20 trial
Frequency matters less than weekly volume when volume is equated
Preprint

An October 2026 medRxiv preprint pooled volume-equated trials across one to three sessions per muscle per week. It concludes that frequency has at most a small and currently undetectable independent effect on hypertrophy, and that weekly volume is what drives the outcome. This is not yet peer-reviewed, so weight it accordingly.

Source: medRxiv preprint, doi 10.64898/2026.10.07.26365015
Integrate now
  • Hold about 10 hard sets per muscle per week as the baseline, rising toward 12 to 15 if recovery allows.
  • Choose session frequency by schedule and recovery, since volume is the main lever.
Caveats
  • Ramp volume gradually; the abrupt 120% jump gave no added benefit.
  • The frequency finding is a preprint, and the narrative review selects its studies.
Section 03
Supplements & Nutrition
CoQ10 raises blood levels but not mitochondrial function in older adults
RCT

Schmücker and colleagues ran a 12-week, placebo-controlled trial in 40 older adults. CoQ10 supplementation raised plasma CoQ10, but the researchers measured no change in mitochondrial function, blood glucose or physical performance. The mechanism is that circulating levels and tissue function appear to be decoupled at this dose and duration, so a blood test showing higher CoQ10 is not evidence that the mitochondria work better.

Source: GeroScience 2026;48(6):8049-8063

Evidence rating: moderate for raising plasma levels, low for any functional benefit. Do not buy CoQ10 for mitochondrial or performance claims.

Ergothioneine as a supplement: promising mouse signal, no human dosing data
Mech

Because ergothioneine accumulated across five treated tissue sets in mice, it has attracted supplement interest. The evidence base here is metabolomic and preclinical: it shows a consistent association with treatment, not a dose-response or a functional outcome in humans. The mechanism is plausible as an antioxidant that tissues accumulate through a specific transporter, but that is inference from mouse data.

Source: Frontiers in Aging (2026)

Evidence rating: low. Do not supplement on this basis yet.

Plant-compound muscle supplements: mixed results in adults 55 and over
Expert

A review of five trials in adults aged 55 and older found mixed results for plant-compound supplements and muscle outcomes. The trials are few and heterogeneous, so the review cannot identify a dose or a responder group. For the practitioner, this confirms that plant-based protein or plant-compound products should not be treated as a substitute for adequate total protein and progressive training.

Source: Supplements evidence roundup, November 2026 (secondary summary)
