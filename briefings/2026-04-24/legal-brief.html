HKMA Awards Hong Kong's First Stablecoin Issuer Licenses to HSBC and Standard Chartered JV — 36 Applicants Narrowed to Two

⚡ What this means: Hong Kong's Stablecoins Ordinance (in force since August 2025) required issuers to obtain a licence before offering HKD- or foreign-currency-pegged stablecoins to the public. The HKMA has now issued its first two licences — from a pool of 36 applicants. This is a tier-one bank taking operational control of stablecoin rails in Asia's largest regulated crypto hub, setting a precedent for bank-led stablecoin issuance globally.
"The HKMA has granted stablecoin issuer licences to Anchorpoint Financial Limited and The Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation Limited." — Hong Kong Monetary Authority press release, April 10, 2026

The Hong Kong Monetary Authority announced on April 10 that HSBC and a Standard Chartered-led joint venture (Anchorpoint Financial Limited — including Hong Kong Telecommunications and Animoca Brands) became the first licensed stablecoin issuers under Hong Kong's Stablecoins Ordinance. The licences were awarded from 36 applications received since the regime entered force in August 2025, demonstrating HKMA's highly selective approach. Both licensees have stated they intend to launch stablecoin operations within the coming months.

The Ordinance requires 1:1 reserve backing, segregated customer assets, and HKMA pre-approval of reserve composition — with requirements framed by Davis Polk and Sidley Austin as closer to a full bank prudential regime than to the lighter-touch MiCA EMT standard. Hong Kong has positioned its licensing regime as a direct counterweight to Singapore's MAS framework and MiCA's EU passport, forcing global stablecoin issuers to choose their Asia-Pacific home jurisdiction early.

📎 Precedent: The HKMA's 1993 virtual banking licensing framework also narrowed a large applicant pool to a small set of bank-led winners — creating a durable incumbency advantage that lasted two decades. The same dynamic is now being seeded in the stablecoin space.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
Per Davis Polk's client update on Hong Kong's stablecoin regime (2026), the Ordinance requires reserve asset pre-approval, segregation of customer funds, and ongoing HKMA supervision under a prudential model — standards that effectively exclude non-bank incumbents from the first wave of licences. Sidley Austin's August 2025 analysis flagged that the 36-to-2 ratio reflects a deliberate policy of channelling stablecoin issuance through regulated depository institutions rather than crypto-native operators. The Brief's read: this is the sharpest global divergence yet between bank-led stablecoin frameworks (Hong Kong, increasingly the US under GENIUS Act § 4(a)) and issuer-neutral frameworks (MiCA, MAS). Non-bank issuers like Tether face a structural disadvantage in Hong Kong that is unlikely to reverse.

HKMA has publicly stated further licences remain under review, and Animoca Brands' inclusion in the Anchorpoint JV signals a willingness to admit crypto-native players — if they partner with a regulated bank. The "bank-led" narrative may be premature.

PACE Act Introduced — Bipartisan Bill Opens Federal Reserve Payment Rails to Nonbank Fintechs Including Ripple and Circle

⚡ What this means: Today, only chartered banks get direct access to Fedwire (large-value transfers), FedNow (instant payments), and FedACH (bulk ACH). The PACE Act creates a new "registered covered provider" category administered by the OCC — regulated nonbanks, including licensed stablecoin issuers, could access all three Fed rails. This is the single largest structural change to US payment infrastructure proposed since the Federal Reserve Act.
"The Payments Access and Consumer Efficiency (PACE) Act would allow qualifying nonbank payment providers direct access to the Federal Reserve's payment systems." — Rep. Young Kim (R-CA) and Rep. Sam Liccardo (D-CA), bill introduction statement, April 21, 2026 [paraphrased]

Representatives Young Kim (R-CA) and Sam Liccardo (D-CA) introduced the PACE Act on April 21, creating a federal "registered covered provider" (RCP) category for nonbank payment providers. Eligibility requires money transmitter licences in ≥40 states, 1:1 reserves, OCC risk-management standards, and BSA compliance. Critically, the OCC has 180 days to deem applications complete and another 180 to approve or deny — and if the OCC misses the second window, the application is automatically granted.

Ripple and Circle are cited as the most prominent crypto-sector beneficiaries. The bill is framed as payments-neutral rather than crypto-specific, which is a deliberate political strategy after the Custodia Bank master-account litigation demonstrated that Fed discretion could not be overcome judicially. The introduction came one day before the 100-firm CLARITY Act coalition letter — a legislative hedging play that ensures payments access advances even if CLARITY Act stalls on SEC/CFTC jurisdictional questions.

📎 Precedent: The Fed's 2023 denial of Custodia Bank's master-account application (upheld by the 10th Circuit) set the precedent that statutory change — not regulatory petition — is required to alter Fed access criteria. PACE Act's automatic-approval mechanism is the direct legislative response.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
Per Hunton Andrews Kurth's April 2026 Blockchain Legal Resource analysis, the 40-state MTL threshold is designed to exclude smaller and non-US fintechs while capturing established players like Circle and Ripple. PYMNTS reporting flagged that the automatic-approval mechanism will face immediate constitutional challenge from the Bank Policy Institute on grounds it delegates a core Fed supervisory function. The Brief's read: PACE Act's real leverage is as a floor negotiating chip for CLARITY Act — its bipartisan co-sponsorship is designed to signal broad Senate appetite for crypto-adjacent payments infrastructure even if comprehensive market structure legislation stalls. Counsel advising payments-sector clients should treat the 180+180 auto-approval mechanism as the provision most likely to be struck in committee markup.
🔄 Contrarian Take
The Bank Policy Institute's prior lobbying positions on nonbank Fed access (against PayPal in 2015, against Custodia in 2023) signal that the PACE Act will face the most organised banking-industry opposition of any 2026 crypto-adjacent bill. A scenario analyst should model 35-45% passage probability, not the enthusiastic framing in industry press. The auto-approval provision is almost certainly a drafting casualty by committee markup.
🏗️ Industry Implications
Ripple's XRP-based payment network gains a direct on-ramp to domestic Fed settlement without the bank-charter route Ripple has avoided. Circle's USDC, if it secured FedNow access, becomes a viable interbank settlement layer challenging Swift's US domestic role. Per K&L Gates' April 15 analysis of the GENIUS Act state-path, PACE Act access would interact with GENIUS Act § 4(c) state-licensed issuers in ways that could let a state-regulated stablecoin issuer settle directly via Fedwire — a combination that correspondent banks (Wells Fargo, Citi) have not yet modelled in their competitive outlook.

⚪ Neutral: Bitcoin (store-of-value unaffected by rail access); pure DeFi protocols without fiat on/off ramp dependencies.

BIS General Manager Warns of "Severe Fragmentation" in Global Stablecoin Rules; Says Stablecoins "More Like ETFs Than Money"

⚡ What this means: The BIS (the central bank for central banks) has now publicly called the global stablecoin market — approximately $320 billion — a financial stability concern. The BIS General Manager's characterisation of stablecoins as "more like ETFs than money" is legally and economically consequential: ETFs require full backing and independent custody, and the framing invites central banks to treat stablecoins under investment-product rules rather than as payment instruments. Expect national regulators to lean into this framing in 2026 consultations.
"Stablecoins function more like exchange-traded funds than money due to redemption frictions and price deviations from par." — Pablo Hernández de Cos, General Manager, Bank for International Settlements, Tokyo, April 20, 2026

On April 20, 2026, BIS General Manager Pablo Hernández de Cos delivered "Stablecoins: framing the debate" at a Bank of Japan seminar in Tokyo. The speech — published in full on BIS.org (sp260420) — contains the strongest public financial-stability framing the BIS has issued on stablecoins to date. Hernández de Cos argued that divergent regulatory frameworks across jurisdictions are already creating "severe fragmentation" and enabling harmful regulatory arbitrage.

The speech flagged four specific policy concerns: impact on credit provision, monetary policy transmission, financial integrity, and regulatory evasion. It argued stablecoins have "found limited commercial use" as payment instruments and instead operate as speculative trading collateral. Hernández de Cos called for coordinated global progress on two fronts: addressing weaknesses in current stablecoin arrangements (particularly if used for payments) and harnessing tokenisation benefits through a two-tier system that preserves monetary singleness.

📎 Precedent: The 2023 FSB High-Level Recommendations on Stablecoins followed similar BIS public framing and became the basis for MiCA's EMT architecture and Singapore's MAS framework — BIS leadership speeches have historically preceded binding international soft-law.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
Per Coindesk's April 20 coverage, the BIS speech arrived as global rulemaking visibly slowed — the US GENIUS Act, EU MiCA, UK FSMA 2026, Hong Kong Ordinance, and Singapore MAS framework are now divergent enough that a single stablecoin issuer cannot operate uniformly across jurisdictions. The Block's reporting quoted de Cos's "ETF, not money" framing as the most consequential rhetorical shift. The Brief's read: this framing invites regulators to apply investment-product rules (prospectus, custody, redemption guarantees) rather than payment-instrument rules (narrower supervision) — which would materially raise compliance costs for non-bank issuers. Counsel advising stablecoin issuers should expect this framing to surface in FSB and IOSCO consultations in Q3 2026.
🔄 Contrarian Take

A contrarian stablecoin industry view: BIS concerns are systemically motivated, not technically accurate, and regulators should weight issuer-specific facts (reserve composition, redemption history) over generalised framings.

Treasury Proposes GENIUS Act § 4(c) State-Regime Principles — $10B Threshold Opens State Path for Smaller Stablecoin Issuers

⚡ What this means: The GENIUS Act gives stablecoin issuers two licensing paths: federal (via FDIC, OCC, or Fed) or state — but only if Treasury certifies the state regime as "substantially similar" to federal standards. Treasury has now proposed the principles for determining "substantially similar." The rule only lets smaller issuers (<$10B outstanding) use the state path. For Circle, PayPal, and Paxos this is moot — they exceed $10B. For mid-tier issuers and new entrants, the state path is a structural alternative to federal banking oversight.
"The proposed rule distinguishes between uniform requirements — standards that must apply identically under state and federal law — and state-calibrated requirements — standards for which states retain flexibility." — US Treasury NPRM, Federal Register Doc. 2026-06489, April 3, 2026 [paraphrased]

On April 3, 2026, the US Treasury published a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM) implementing Section 4(c) of the GENIUS Act, which permits permitted payment stablecoin issuers (PPSIs) with $10 billion or less in outstanding stablecoins to operate under qualifying state regulatory regimes as an alternative to federal authorisation. The NPRM establishes the "substantially similar" test, the evidence Treasury will examine, and the principles distinguishing uniform versus state-calibrated standards. Comments close June 2, 2026.

The $10 billion threshold is material — it excludes Circle (USDC: ~$60B), PayPal (PYUSD), and Tether, but captures mid-tier and new entrant issuers who could now pursue a Wyoming, New York, or Texas state regulatory regime rather than the federal OCC/FDIC route. State banking commissioners have incentives to pitch their regimes as the less-burdensome path, creating direct competition between the states and federal regulators for stablecoin chartering.

📎 Precedent: The 1864 National Bank Act's dual banking system (state vs national charter) created the template for regulatory competition between states and federal regulators; GENIUS Act § 4(c) is an explicit import of that dual system into stablecoin issuance.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
Per Baker McKenzie's April 20 Blockchain alert on Treasury's "substantially similar" principles, the uniform/state-calibrated distinction is the rule's most important structural choice — reserve composition and redemption timing are proposed as uniform, while examination cycles and exam staffing are state-calibrated. K&L Gates' April 15 analysis noted the $10B threshold will be the primary factor in issuer strategy decisions, with new entrants likely to pursue state paths first and graduate to federal oversight only after growth. Per Arnold & Porter's April GENIUS Act implementation overview, state banking commissioners in Wyoming (Wyoming Division of Banking) and New York (NYDFS) are already preparing regime-eligibility submissions. The Brief's read: this creates a state-level regulatory competition that resembles the post-2014 BitLicense arbitrage dynamics, but with Treasury as the ultimate arbiter. Counsel advising new stablecoin issuers should begin state-regime mapping immediately — the June 2 comment period is where regime standards get shaped.
🔄 Contrarian Take
ABA Banking Journal's April coverage warned that the rule's "wide latitude" for states could create systemic fragmentation — a weak state regime, once certified, effectively passports issuers into interstate activity with standards below federal levels. The Consumer Financial Services Law Monitor flagged this as a consumer-protection risk, not just a technical-compliance issue. If Treasury's final principles are too permissive, the rule is ripe for legislative amendment in the next Congress.
🏗️ Industry Implications
Per PYMNTS' analysis, smaller and new stablecoin issuers — including institutional treasury tokens, niche-use-case stablecoins, and state-bank-backed tokens — gain a meaningful alternative licensing path. Wyoming's SPDI (Special Purpose Depository Institution) framework is the leading candidate for early certification, directly competing with New York's BitLicense for stablecoin issuance charters. Tokenised bank-deposit products issued by state-chartered banks may structurally benefit from the state path while remaining under federal deposit insurance.

🔴 Bearish: Federal-only chartering infrastructure (OCC preemption argument loses leverage); Tether and Circle (above $10B, can't use state path but face state-path competition from below).

EU Preparing to Classify ChatGPT as VLOSE Under DSA — First Standalone AI Product in Strictest Regulatory Tier

⚡ What this means: The Digital Services Act (DSA) gives the European Commission the power to designate platforms with 45M+ monthly EU users as "Very Large Online Search Engines" (VLOSEs) or "Very Large Online Platforms" (VLOPs). These designations carry mandatory algorithmic transparency, content-moderation audits, and potential fines up to 6% of global revenue. ChatGPT's 120M+ EU monthly users means it exceeds the threshold — if designated, it would be the first standalone AI product in the DSA's strictest tier.
"ChatGPT's classification as a VLOSE is expected within days." — Computing.co.uk, citing European Commission sources, April 2026 [paraphrased]

The European Commission is reported to be preparing a DSA designation of OpenAI's ChatGPT as a VLOSE. ChatGPT's reported 120M monthly EU users substantially exceeds the DSA's 45M threshold. Under the DSA, designated services have four months to meet obligations: regulatory contact channels, suspected criminal activity reporting, accessible terms of service, advertising transparency, recommendation algorithm transparency, and annual independent compliance audits. Non-compliance carries fines up to 6% of global annual revenue.

The designation interacts with the EU AI Act — which becomes fully applicable August 2, 2026 — creating a dual-regulation architecture for high-impact general-purpose AI (GPAI). Under the AI Act, GPT-4-class models are separately classified as systemic-risk GPAI with evaluation and incident-reporting obligations. OpenAI would face simultaneous DSA and AI Act compliance by Q3 2026.

⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
