Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

Two forces are colliding: tools now strip friction from cognitive work, while learning science — Robert Bjork's "desirable difficulties" — shows that very friction is what builds durable skill. MIT's "Your Brain on ChatGPT" preprint and a peer-reviewed Microsoft/Carnegie Mellon study find heavy AI users show weaker neural engagement, lower ownership and less diverse output. The strategic move isn't to refuse the tools but to choose your hard things deliberately — synthesising Mark Manson, David Senra's talk with Patrick O'Shaughnessy, James Clear and Michael Easter.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Newsletter · Mark Manson · "Don't outsource your thinking" · Jun 25

The hard part isn't the obstacle — it's the work

Asked how The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F*ck would differ if AI had existed when he wrote it, Mark Manson's answer was blunt: "It would've gotten written slightly faster. That's it." His point isn't anti-AI — he concedes that three minutes with a chatbot would have replaced hours of slogging through psychology papers, Buddhist stories and Reddit threads.

But a book, he argues, is "20% writing, 80% excavation" — and the excavation is where the thinking happens. AI compresses the part that was never the bottleneck while leaving untouched "the part where you wrestle with what you actually believe — that's still 100% human." Outsource that and you may still publish something, "but it won't be yours."

Manson is describing, in a writer's vocabulary, a measurable phenomenon: the felt difficulty of a task and its developmental value are not the same variable — and the tools are getting very good at removing the first while quietly draining the second.

Research · Robert A. Bjork, UCLA · Desirable Difficulties

The science: difficulty is the mechanism, not a tax on it

UCLA psychologist Robert Bjork's framework, introduced in 1994, is the intellectual spine of the whole question. He separates performance (what you can do right now) from learning (what you retain and can transfer later) — and shows they often move in opposite directions. Conditions that slow you down and make you stumble — spacing practice, interleaving topics, self-testing, generating an answer before checking it — feel like worse progress but produce dramatically more durable, transferable skill.

The mechanism is two kinds of memory strength: storage strength (how deeply something is encoded) versus retrieval strength (how easily you can reach it today). The struggle to retrieve is precisely what strengthens future retrieval. Ease optimises the wrong variable.

The crucial caveat — and the one most "embrace the grind" takes miss — is that Bjork is explicit that difficulty itself is not the goal. Noise, fatigue, confusing instructions and unrelated complexity are undesirable difficulties: they make work harder without building anything. The test is whether a difficulty challenges retrieval and reconstruction. That distinction is the entire game.

Research · MIT Media Lab + Microsoft / Carnegie Mellon · 2025

"Cognitive debt": what the frictionless tools quietly remove

Two 2025 studies put numbers on the worry. MIT Media Lab's "Your Brain on ChatGPT" (Kosmyna et al.) ran EEG on 54 people writing essays in three conditions — brain-only, search-engine, and LLM. Brain-only writers showed the strongest, most distributed neural connectivity; LLM users showed the weakest. The AI group also reported the lowest ownership of their own essays and, tellingly, struggled to quote text they had written minutes earlier — the authors' phrase for the residue is "cognitive debt." (It's a preprint with a small sample, and peer reviewers have urged caution on the strongest claims — treat it as suggestive, not settled.)

The sturdier evidence is a peer-reviewed Microsoft Research & Carnegie Mellon survey of 319 knowledge workers (CHI 2025, Lee et al.). The headline correlation: the more workers trusted the AI, the less critical thinking they did — while higher confidence in their own judgment predicted more. Roughly three-quarters reported expending less effort on knowledge, analysis and synthesis, and AI-assisted work converged on "a less diverse set of outcomes."

Read together: the tool reliably removes the exact retrieval-and-reconstruction effort Bjork identifies as the engine of durable skill — and homogenises output as a side effect. Convenience and capability-building are, at the margin, in tension.

Podcast · David Senra × Patrick O'Shaughnessy · Founders · Jun 25

Progressive overload as a moat — and a life principle

On the Founders podcast, David Senra distilled his conversation with investor Patrick O'Shaughnessy into a doctrine of chosen difficulty. The strategic version is a moat argument: "I'm interested in stuff that's very hard to make and that's singular… The harder the business is to build, the harder it will be to copy." In a world where AI collapses the cost of the easy 80%, the deliberately hard thing is what stays defensible.

The personal version borrows from the gym: "I love the idea of progressive overload — always a little bit harder, a little bit more… The second something feels easy and I'm going through the motions, I get really uncomfortable." And the line that reframes effort entirely: "The reward for great work is more work." O'Shaughnessy's coda — "Mediocrity is always invisible until passion shows up and exposes it" — is the competitive logic of the difficulty dividend in one sentence.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · The friction audit

Don't refuse the tools — triage your difficulties

The mistake is binary thinking: either grind everything by hand or outsource everything to the machine. Bjork's distinction gives you a sharper rule — keep the difficulties that build retrieval and judgment; offload the ones that are merely toil. Run your AI use through this filter:

  • Map the 80/20 of each task. Manson's split is the template: let the tool do the excavation (gathering, formatting, first-pass synthesis); keep for yourself the 20% where you decide what you actually believe. Outsource the search, never the stance.
  • Generate before you retrieve. Draft the argument, attempt the problem, or write the rough answer first — then check it against the AI. This preserves the "generation effect" and the sense of ownership the MIT group lost. The tool becomes an editor, not a ghostwriter.
  • Name the difficulty before you remove it. Ask: is this hard because it's building something (retrieval, reasoning, taste) or just noise (formatting, boilerplate, lookup)? Kill undesirable difficulty ruthlessly; protect the desirable kind on purpose.
  • Watch for the confidence trap. The Microsoft finding — trust in the AI predicts less of your own thinking — means the danger is silent. Periodically do a task tool-free to check the skill is still yours.
Practice · James Clear & the overload principle

Engineer the right friction back in

If the tools remove effort by default, capability now requires you to re-introduce difficulty deliberately. Two cheap, high-leverage methods:

  • Use artificial constraints to force quality. James Clear's entire 3-2-1 newsletter was born from one: when 20-hour essays became unsustainable, he asked, "If I only had 2–4 hours per week, what would I do?" The constraint produced his most popular format. "Sometimes you can improve by cutting your options off."
  • Progressive-overload your craft. Borrow Senra's tell: when a task starts feeling automatic, that's the signal to add load — a harder problem, a higher bar, a rep count nobody else will match, not a smoother version of yesterday.
  • Schedule one voluntary hard thing. Michael Easter's research on "voluntary discomfort" argues we're at our best after the kind of adversity our ancestors faced. Put one genuinely difficult, un-optimised effort on the calendar each week — cognitive or physical — and protect it from the convenience reflex.
  • Have the courage to be bad first. Clear: "nobody's early work is good… the bridge between where you are and where you want to be is courage." Frictionless tools tempt you to skip the foolish-beginner phase — which is the only phase that ever produces a voice.

The discipline is not asceticism. It's selective: outsource the toil, manufacture the difficulty that compounds.

04 On the Horizon
Ai

"Cognitive debt" becomes a measurable category

Expect the 2025 EEG and survey work to spawn a replication-and-measurement wave — longitudinal studies, attempts to quantify skill erosion in real jobs, and product features that deliberately re-insert friction ("show your work first" modes, retrieval prompts). The interesting design frontier is AI that makes you think more, not less.

Work

The difficulty moat as a hiring and strategy filter

As AI flattens the cost of competent-but-generic output, the premium shifts to what can't be one-prompted: hard-won judgment, taste, and the willingness to do singular work. Senra's "harder to build, harder to copy" logic scales from individuals to org strategy — differentiation increasingly lives in the difficulties a competitor won't endure.

Mind

Voluntary discomfort goes mainstream

Cold plunges, fasting, "misogi," dopamine fasts, analog hobbies — the comfort-crisis thesis is migrating from the biohacking fringe into general culture as people intuit that engineered ease has a cost. The risk is it curdles into another optimised, purchasable lifestyle product, losing the un-optimised difficulty that was the point.

Culture

The originality premium

If AI-assisted work converges on "a less diverse set of outcomes," then distinctive voice, idiosyncratic taste and visible human struggle become scarce — and scarcity prices up. Watch for a counter-market that rewards the obviously hand-made, the un-summarisable, the work that could only have come from one person.
