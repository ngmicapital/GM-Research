Rabbit Hole

The Drug That
Nobody Understands

How Things Work 8 April 2026 · Sydney, Australia

General anesthesia has been used in over 300 million surgeries a year since 1846, yet science still cannot fully explain how it erases consciousness. The molecular mechanism remains contested — and rare cases of awareness during surgery reveal the cost of that gap.

TL;DR

General anesthesia is among the most routinely used medical interventions in history — over 300 million people are anesthetized every year. It was first publicly demonstrated in 1846, and has been continuously refined since. Yet as of 2026, no scientist or physician can fully explain the molecular mechanism by which it erases consciousness. We have strong candidates — GABA receptors, lipid membranes, disrupted brain connectivity — but no unified theory. We can reliably switch consciousness off and on in virtually any human without knowing precisely how. The tools work; the explanation does not. Somewhere between 1 in 1,000 and 1 in 15,000 anesthetized patients will experience some form of awareness during surgery — paralyzed, unable to signal distress, sometimes in pain. The mystery is not historical: it is active, alive, and ethically consequential.

Section 01

The Story

On the morning of October 16, 1846, a Boston dentist named William T. G. Morton stood in a surgical theater at Massachusetts General Hospital and made a promise no one had been able to keep. He told Dr. John Collins Warren that he could render the patient — a young printer named Gilbert Abbott, who had a benign tumor on his jaw — entirely insensible to pain during the operation. Warren had performed surgery for decades on fully conscious, screaming patients. He had seen men die of shock before the knife had finished its work. He was skeptical.

Morton administered diethyl ether vapor through a glass inhaler of his own design. Abbott fell unconscious within three to four minutes. Warren removed the tumor. Abbott felt nothing. When he woke, Warren turned to the assembled surgeons and said: "Gentlemen, this is no humbug." Within weeks, the news had traveled by ship to London and Paris. Within a year, ether had been used in surgeries across Europe. Within a decade, the practice of operating on conscious patients had become a historical curiosity.

Morton was not, it turned out, the first. A Georgia physician named Crawford Long had used ether in surgery as early as 1842, but never published his results. Nitrous oxide — "laughing gas" — had been demonstrated as a pain suppressant in the 1840s. Chloroform followed quickly after ether, adopted in 1847 by the obstetrician James Young Simpson in Edinburgh. Queen Victoria received chloroform during childbirth in 1853, which essentially ended public debate about whether anesthesia was moral or ungodly.

What none of these pioneers knew — and what remains, to a significant degree, unresolved — was why any of it worked. They had the result without the mechanism. This is more unusual than it sounds. Most drugs are developed after identifying a target — a receptor, an enzyme, a pathway — and engineering a molecule to act on it. Anesthesia ran in reverse: the tools came first, the explanation has been catching up ever since, and it still hasn't caught up completely.

The Counterintuitive Core

Medicine figured out how to reliably erase and restore human consciousness 180 years ago, and has performed the procedure hundreds of millions of times since. It is one of the most reproducible interventions in all of medicine. The precise molecular explanation remains contested. We can do the thing without knowing how we do it.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The first serious attempt to explain anesthesia mechanistically came in 1899–1901, when two researchers working independently — Hans Horst Meyer in Germany and Ernest Overton in Switzerland — noticed the same thing: the potency of an anesthetic agent correlates almost perfectly with its lipid solubility. The more readily a substance dissolves in fat rather than water, the more powerful its anesthetic effect. This became the Meyer-Overton correlation, and it pointed squarely at the cell membrane — the lipid bilayer — as the site of action. The theory: anesthetics dissolved into the membrane's fatty layer, physically disrupting its structure and somehow suppressing neuronal activity.

The lipid theory dominated for nearly a century. It had one enormous practical virtue: it explained why substances as chemically diverse as ether, chloroform, nitrous oxide, xenon gas, and the intravenous drug propofol could all produce anesthesia despite having almost nothing in common at the molecular level. They shared only one property — lipid solubility — which suggested a physical, non-specific mechanism rather than a lock-and-key receptor interaction.

But by the 1980s, problems accumulated. Anesthetic potency was clearly subject to stereoisomerism — molecules that are mirror images of each other, chemically identical but spatially reversed, produce different anesthetic effects at different doses. A non-specific lipid mechanism should affect both mirror images equally. It does not. This strongly suggested that at least part of the mechanism is protein-based: that anesthetics bind to specific receptor sites where geometry matters, the way a key fits a lock.

The leading candidate became the GABA-A receptor — a protein channel in the neuronal membrane that responds to gamma-aminobutyric acid, the brain's primary inhibitory neurotransmitter. When GABA binds, the channel opens, chloride ions flow in, the neuron becomes harder to fire. Anesthetics like propofol and etomidate appear to enhance GABA-A activity — essentially making the brain's own brakes more effective, broadly suppressing neural excitability. This is the current consensus for intravenous agents.

But it is not the whole story, and researchers know it. Propofol also shows high potency at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and HCN channels — two entirely different systems. Inhaled anesthetics like isoflurane act at multiple targets simultaneously. Ketamine works primarily by blocking NMDA receptors, a completely different pathway. And the newest research has returned, partially, to the membrane: lipid rafts — ordered clusters of specific lipids and proteins that function as signaling platforms in the cell membrane — appear to be disrupted by anesthetics in a way that activates potassium channels and suppresses firing. The lipid theory was not wrong. It was incomplete.

In Plain English

Think of consciousness as a conversation happening simultaneously between billions of neurons. Anesthesia appears to work by turning down the volume on multiple communication systems at once — inhibitory signals get louder, excitatory signals get quieter, and the physical structure of the membrane changes in ways that further suppress firing. There is no single off switch. There are many, and we do not know their precise order or relative importance. The conversation just stops, and we do not fully know why.

The deeper problem is philosophical as much as biochemical. Even if we fully catalogued every molecular target — every receptor, every lipid raft, every ion channel — that would still not explain why those changes extinguish consciousness. The relationship between neural activity patterns and subjective experience remains one of the hardest unsolved problems in science. Anesthesia research runs directly into the hard problem of consciousness: what it is to experience something, and what physical process produces that experience. Until that is solved, the mechanism of anesthesia cannot be fully described, because the mechanism is the mechanism by which something stops being conscious — and we do not know what being conscious is.

Section 03

The Connections

Awareness during surgery is a real, documented, and still-unsolved problem. Between 1 and 2 patients per 1,000 under general anesthesia will experience some form of awareness — a return of consciousness while still physically paralyzed by the neuromuscular blocking agents used to prevent movement during procedures. The lower-end estimate from anesthesiologist-reported data is 1 in 15,000. The discrepancy itself tells a story: patients and physicians systematically disagree about how often this happens. Of those who experience awareness, roughly half will develop long-term psychological consequences including post-traumatic stress disorder. The experience — awake, unable to move, unable to signal distress, sometimes in pain — is one of the most reliably traumatizing medical events in clinical records. The direct cause is our incomplete understanding of the mechanism: because we cannot fully explain what anesthesia does, we cannot reliably detect when it is not doing it.

The fact that anesthesia works on plants, worms, and insects is deeply strange. Plants under ether anesthesia stop moving in the time-lapse sense — they halt tropisms, stop electrical signaling, become temporarily unresponsive to stimuli. C. elegans, a nematode worm with exactly 302 neurons and no centralized brain, can be anesthetized. Anesthetics work on creatures so evolutionarily distant from humans that there is no shared neural architecture to point to. This is either evidence that anesthesia operates on some fundamental property of living cellular membranes that predates nervous systems — or evidence that what we call "anesthesia" in humans is actually several overlapping phenomena rather than one thing, and we are conflating the loss of consciousness with other, simpler suppression effects.

The discovery of anesthesia changed the pace of surgery itself. Before 1846, speed was a surgical virtue above all others — the fastest surgeons were celebrated because every additional second of operating time was a second of unbearable agony for the conscious patient. Robert Liston, a famous London surgeon of the 1840s, was known for performing amputations in 25 seconds. Speed had a cost: it required cutting corners on technique. Once anesthesia eliminated the time constraint, surgeons could be careful, precise, deliberate. The cognitive and technical transformation of surgery that followed — from heroic speed-cutting to systematic, minute dissection — was enabled entirely by the ability to keep patients unconscious and still. Modern surgery, with its hours-long procedures and millimeter-level precision, is the downstream consequence of October 16, 1846.

Anesthesia research is now one of the most direct windows into the neuroscience of consciousness. Because anesthesiologists can control the depth and type of consciousness with pharmacological precision in consenting human subjects, they can study what happens in the brain at the exact transition from awake to unconscious in real time. Modern EEG and fMRI studies of anesthetized patients have shown that different anesthetic agents produce distinctly different brain activity signatures — propofol creates slow oscillations; ketamine creates chaotic high-frequency activity; nitrous oxide creates theta bursts — even though all three produce clinical anesthesia. This suggests they are achieving the same behavioral endpoint — unconsciousness — through different neural routes. Which means "unconsciousness" may not be a single state, any more than "darkness" is a single phenomenon.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

Neuroscience The Hard Problem of Consciousness

Philosopher David Chalmers coined this term in 1995 to describe the explanatory gap between physical brain processes and subjective experience — why there is "something it is like" to see red, or feel pain. Anesthesia research crashes directly into this problem: even a complete map of every molecular target would not explain why those changes produce unconsciousness rather than some other state. The mechanism of anesthesia cannot be fully described until the mechanism of consciousness is.

History of Medicine The Priority Dispute That Destroyed Morton

William Morton spent the rest of his life — and his fortune — fighting for recognition and financial reward from the US government for his 1846 demonstration. Rival claimants included Crawford Long, Horace Wells, and Charles Jackson. Congress debated his case for years and never awarded compensation. Morton died in 1868, in debt, at age 48, reportedly in distress during a carriage ride in Central Park. He is buried in Mount Auburn Cemetery in Cambridge, Massachusetts, beneath a stone reading: "Inventor and Revealer of Anaesthetic Inhalation."

Pharmacology Propofol: The White Milk of Amnesia

Propofol, the dominant intravenous anesthetic since the 1980s, is famously milky white — it is formulated as a lipid emulsion because the active compound is not water-soluble. It produces rapid unconsciousness (under 40 seconds), rapid recovery, and often a brief euphoria that has made it a drug of abuse among medical professionals with access. It is almost certainly what killed Michael Jackson in 2009, administered by his physician for sleep. It is also one of the most widely used drugs in medicine, given during colonoscopies, intubations, and procedural sedation worldwide every day.

Philosophy / Ethics Are Anesthetized Patients Truly Unconscious?

The clinical definition of successful general anesthesia is behavioral — the patient does not move, does not respond to commands, does not recall the surgery. But some researchers argue that anesthesia may not produce unconsciousness in the philosophically strict sense. Studies using isolated forearm technique (keeping one arm unparalyzed) have found that a small fraction of patients can follow commands during apparent anesthesia. Whether they are "conscious" in the way a waking person is — or in some intermediate state — is genuinely unresolved.

Evolution / Biology Why Plants and Worms Can Be Anesthetized

The fact that anesthetics work on organisms with no brain — plants, fungi, and single-celled organisms — has been documented since the early twentieth century. Claude Bernard observed in the 1870s that ether stopped the movements of the sensitive plant Mimosa pudica. This cross-kingdom effect suggests anesthetics target something genuinely ancient: possibly the lipid membrane itself, or a class of ion channels conserved across all eukaryotic life. It may mean that whatever anesthesia suppresses in humans is not unique to consciousness, but is a property of all living cells.
