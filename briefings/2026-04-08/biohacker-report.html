TL;DR — Driving Thesis

The signal this cycle is a cascade of institutional validation for things the biohacking community has been practising for years — and a recalibration of dosing. Protein guidelines have been revised upward to 1.2–1.6 g/kg (the old 0.8 g/kg is dead), peptides are coming back into legal compound pharmacy pipelines in the US with BPC-157 and GHK-Cu leading the list, and a landmark large-sample study quantifies exactly how much lifestyle change is worth: 42 minutes of exercise and 7.2 hours of sleep per day adds nearly a decade to healthspan. The training literature is firming up around volume: 10+ direct sets per muscle group weekly is now in the official ACSM guidelines. One mechanism stands out from the noise — a Berkeley team mapped the brain circuit that links deep sleep to growth hormone release, which explains why sleep debt kills recovery faster than most people account for. The practical takeaway: if you're not hitting your protein target and sleep is inconsistent, no protocol stack matters.

01 Longevity & Biomarkers
9.35 Extra Years — and Exactly How to Get Them Observational

The most actionable longevity data published this cycle comes from a large UK Biobank cohort study of approximately 60,000 participants followed for 8 years and published in eClinicalMedicine. Researchers built a SPAN model — Sleep, Physical activity, and Nutrition — and found that hitting all three targets simultaneously added 9.35 years of lifespan and 9.46 years of healthspan versus people with the poorest habits. The three targets are not heroic: 7.2 hours of sleep, 42 minutes of daily physical activity, and a diet quality score of at least 58.

🔬 In Plain English — Healthspan
Healthspan is the number of years you live free from serious chronic disease (in this study: cardiovascular disease, dementia, COPD, and type 2 diabetes). It's not just how long you live — it's how long you live well. The study found healthspan gains were slightly larger than lifespan gains, which means the extra years are quality years.

The compounding effect is the critical insight here: small improvements across all three variables simultaneously require less change in any single variable to get a full year of benefit. As lead researcher Nicholas Koemel stated: "By targeting small improvements across multiple behaviours simultaneously, the required change for any single behaviour is substantially reduced." Single-lever changes that buy a full year: 25 extra minutes of sleep, 2.3 more minutes of exercise, or a 35.5-point diet quality improvement.

This is not a causal study — it observational with all the standard confounders — but 60,000 people over 8 years is a serious dataset. The dose-response shape (small changes → large multi-lever gains) is the operationally important finding. The 42 minutes of physical activity threshold aligns almost exactly with WHO recommendations, which lends it credibility. Observational
The Longevity Field Pivots: From "Fix Aging" to "Preserve Coordination" Expert Opinion

The 2nd World Congress on Targeting Longevity, held in Berlin on 8–9 April 2026, organised by the World Mitochondria Society (WMS) and International Society of Microbiota, is shifting the dominant framing of aging research. The emerging consensus is that aging is not a defect in any single pathway — it is a progressive loss of coordination between biological systems (mitochondria, microbiome, hormonal axes, circadian timing).

🔬 In Plain English — Mitochondria
Mitochondria are the energy-producing organelles inside your cells — often called the "powerhouses." As we age, mitochondrial function declines and their coordination with other cellular systems deteriorates. This loss of coordination is now considered a central driver of age-related disease rather than just a downstream symptom.

This framing shift matters practically: it deprioritises single-compound interventions (NMN, rapamycin, senolytics alone) and raises the importance of system-wide interventions — consistent sleep, exercise variety, dietary diversity, circadian alignment. Single-pathway bets become riskier the more the field coalesces around multi-system coordination as the target. Expert Opinion

⚠ Hype Watch
This is a conference consensus, not a published trial. The "coordination" framing is intellectually compelling but not yet operationalised into testable, falsifiable protocols. Follow for papers over the next 6–12 months before adjusting any stack based on this alone.
Genetics vs. Environment in Lifespan: Roughly Equal Observational

A study reported in the Washington Post (5 April 2026) revisits the genetics-vs-environment debate in longevity, with new findings suggesting they play roughly equal roles in determining lifespan. This challenges the prevailing assumption (based on older twin studies) that genetics dominated at around 25–30% of lifespan variance. The revised estimate — closer to 50/50 — gives lifestyle interventions more causal weight.

Important caveat: source unavailable for full-text verification — this is a summary of the WaPo article, which is behind a paywall. Primary study details (institution, sample size, methodology) could not be confirmed. Tag as Observational until primary source is verifiable.

⚡ Implication — Longevity
Prioritise hitting all three SPAN targets simultaneously: 7.2h sleep, 42 min movement, diet quality score 58+. Changing all three slightly beats optimising any single one.
02 Recovery & Sleep
The Sleep Switch: Berkeley Maps the GH-Release Circuit Mechanistic

Researchers at UC Berkeley, led by Yang Dan (with collaborators including Xinlu Ding and Daniel Silverman, plus Stanford University co-investigators), published a mechanistic study in Cell mapping the brain circuits that control growth hormone (GH) release during sleep. This is the most consequential sleep-biology paper in the last 12 months from a practical standpoint for athletes and people doing resistance training.

🔬 In Plain English — Growth Hormone (GH)
Growth hormone is a protein your pituitary gland releases in pulses, primarily during deep sleep. It drives tissue repair, muscle protein synthesis, fat metabolism, and bone density. If you're skipping sleep, you're not just feeling tired — you're cutting off the primary hormonal signal for recovery from training.

The circuit runs through the hypothalamus using two competing signals: GHRH (which triggers GH release) and somatostatin (which blocks it). Sleep activates GHRH, GH rises, then GH itself activates the locus coeruleus — the brain's alertness hub — creating a self-limiting feedback loop. More deep sleep → more GH → gradual push toward waking. The system is exquisitely balanced, and disrupting it (via fragmented sleep, alcohol, or late-night light exposure) collapses GH output disproportionately.

🔬 In Plain English — GHRH & Somatostatin
GHRH (growth hormone releasing hormone) is your body's "release now" signal for growth hormone. Somatostatin is the "stop" signal. Think of them as accelerator and brake for your GH pulse. Sleep tips the balance toward GHRH — which is why slow-wave deep sleep is when most GH is secreted. Alcohol and disrupted sleep blunt GHRH and blunt your GH output for the whole night.
🔬 In Plain English — Locus Coeruleus
The locus coeruleus is a small region in the brainstem that acts as your brain's alertness switch, releasing norepinephrine (an adrenaline-like signal) to wake you up. It's how GH — released during sleep — eventually helps pull you back toward wakefulness as the night progresses.

The practical implication is direct: protecting slow-wave deep sleep is not about feeling rested — it is specifically about GH output and recovery from training. The Berkeley paper identifies this as "a novel handle" for therapeutic intervention, suggesting future tools targeting the GHRH/somatostatin balance. For now, the levers are behavioural. Mechanistic

This is mechanistic/animal-model work published in Cell — not an RCT in humans. But the circuit maps onto what we already know from human sleep-GH studies. It confirms mechanism more than establishes new effect sizes. The therapeutic angle (gene therapy targeting specific brain cells to improve sleep quality) is years away from clinical application.
Fundamentals — Sleep Optimisation Stack

Given the Berkeley GH-circuit finding, here is the evidence-ranked sleep stack for protecting deep sleep specifically — the stage that drives GH release and physical recovery.

Temperature: Core body temperature must drop ~1–1.5°C to initiate and maintain sleep. Keep your room 16–19°C. A warm shower or bath 1–2 hours before bed accelerates peripheral heat loss and speeds sleep onset — counterintuitive but robustly supported. RCT

Magnesium glycinate: 300–400 mg elemental magnesium 30–60 min before bed. Acts on GABA receptors to reduce neural excitability. The glycinate form crosses the blood-brain barrier more efficiently than oxide or citrate forms and has less GI impact. Well-tolerated; consistent evidence for improved sleep quality in deficient individuals (very common in Australians eating a Western diet). RCT

🔬 In Plain English — GABA Receptors
GABA is your brain's primary "calm down" neurotransmitter — it reduces neural firing. GABA receptors are the docking sites for GABA signals. Magnesium enhances GABA activity, which is why it has a mild sedating effect and reduces night-time anxiety and muscle tension.

Apigenin: 50 mg — a flavonoid compound found in chamomile that acts as a mild GABA-A receptor agonist without the dependency risk of pharmaceutical sleep aids. Mechanistic / Expert Opinion (via Huberman Lab)

L-theanine: 100–200 mg — increases alpha-wave activity and reduces cortisol. Works synergistically with magnesium. Avoid if you have vivid or disturbing dreams. RCT

Light: No bright artificial light (especially blue-wavelength) after 9 PM. Morning bright-light exposure within 30 minutes of waking (10–30 min, outside or 10,000 lux lamp) anchors the circadian clock and front-loads melatonin onset by evening. This is the single highest-leverage, zero-cost sleep intervention available. RCT

Quick Reference — Tonight's Stack
  • 9 PM: last bright lights, blue-light blocking glasses if using screens
  • 9:30 PM: magnesium glycinate 300–400 mg + apigenin 50 mg + theanine 100 mg
  • Room: 16–19°C, fully dark
  • Morning: outside light within 30 min of waking, minimum 10 minutes
⚡ Implication — Recovery
Fragmented or short sleep is not just a fatigue issue — it is a direct suppression of GH output. Getting below 7 hours consistently blunts your training adaptation regardless of what you do in the gym.
03 Nutrition & Fuel
The 0.8 g/kg Number Is Dead — New US Dietary Guidelines Say 1.2–1.6 g/kg Meta

The 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans, released in January 2026, formally revised the recommended daily protein intake from the longstanding 0.8 g/kg to 1.2–1.6 g/kg of body weight. This follows decades of research — led in part by Stuart Phillips PhD (McMaster University) and Christopher Gardner PhD (Stanford Prevention Research Center) — showing the old number was set for nitrogen balance in sedentary adults, not for muscle retention, performance, or metabolic health.

For a 80 kg male doing PPL resistance training: the old guideline was 64 g/day (functionally inadequate). The new range is 96–128 g/day at minimum. For body recomposition or active muscle-building phases, the practitioner consensus sits higher: 1.8–2.2 g/kg, which for an 80 kg male is 144–176 g/day.

🔬 In Plain English — Nitrogen Balance
Nitrogen balance is the original method used to set protein recommendations. Protein contains nitrogen; when you measure nitrogen in vs. nitrogen out (in urine and stool), you can tell if the body is in "protein equilibrium." The problem: this method only tells you the minimum to avoid wasting away — not the optimum for muscle growth, recovery, or longevity. It's like recommending enough calories to not starve, rather than enough to thrive.

Meal timing: Stanford's Marily Oppezzo, PhD confirms that the "anabolic window" post-exercise extends approximately 24 hours — so an immediate post-workout shake is not necessary. What matters more is total daily intake distributed across meals. For older adults (40+), distributing protein across multiple meals triggers more muscle protein synthesis than eating most protein in one sitting.

🔬 In Plain English — Anabolic Window
The anabolic window refers to the period after training when your muscles are especially receptive to protein for repair and growth. The old claim was you had 30–60 minutes. The current evidence shows this window is closer to 24 hours. You still want protein post-training — just not a panic-driven shake the moment you rack the bar.
This is an institutional validation of what the evidence-based fitness community has been recommending for 10+ years. The practical change: if you're eating at the old 0.8 g/kg recommendation, you're likely leaving adaptation on the table. Track protein for one week and see where you actually land.
Quick Reference — Protein Targets & Meals
  • Maintenance: 1.6 g/kg bodyweight/day (80 kg = 128 g/day)
  • Building/recomp: 1.8–2.2 g/kg (80 kg = 144–176 g/day)
  • Meal idea 1: 200 g chicken breast + 200 g Greek yoghurt + 3 eggs = ~90 g protein
  • Meal idea 2: 200 g salmon + 150 g cottage cheese + 100 g edamame = ~85 g protein
  • Meal idea 3: 150 g beef mince (93% lean) + 200 g lentils + 2 eggs = ~80 g protein
Creatine's Dosing Question: Muscles Need 3–5 g, Brain Needs More RCT

New research is challenging the standard creatine protocol. Two Alzheimer's trials published in 2025 used 20 g/day over 8 weeks and produced an 11% increase in brain creatine levels — the first studies to show supplemental creatine can cross the blood-brain barrier at meaningful concentrations. Researchers at the University of Kansas Medical Center report significant improvements in cognitive markers from these doses.

🔬 In Plain English — Creatine Phosphate System
Creatine is a molecule that your body uses to rapidly regenerate ATP (the energy currency of cells) during high-intensity work. In muscles, it powers explosive efforts — sprints, heavy lifts. In the brain, the same system is used during intense cognitive work and stress states. The brain's creatine pool is separate from muscle creatine, which is why standard 3–5 g doses may not meaningfully raise brain levels.

For general muscle performance: 3–5 g/day creatine monohydrate remains the evidence-backed protocol. For cognitive performance, mood resilience under stress, and potential neuroprotection: the dose appears to need to be higher — but 20 g/day is a loading-phase dose and shouldn't be used indefinitely without monitoring kidney function.

⚠ Hype Watch — Higher Creatine Doses
The 20 g dose studies were in Alzheimer's patients, not healthy young athletes. Effect size in healthy populations at higher doses is unknown. Long-term kidney safety at 10–20 g/day in healthy individuals is understudied. Standard 3–5 g/day has a 30-year safety record. Don't chase brain dosing without physician monitoring. RCT
⚡ Implication — Nutrition
This week: calculate your actual daily protein intake for two days. If you're below 1.6 g/kg, protein is your highest-leverage nutritional intervention — above any supplement, timing strategy, or micronutrient tweak.
04 Protocols
BPC-157, GHK-Cu & 12 Other Peptides — Back in the Pharmacy Pipeline (US) Expert Opinion

On 27 February 2026, HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. announced that approximately 14 of the 19 peptides placed on the FDA's Category 2 restricted list in 2023 will return to Category 1 status — meaning licensed compounding pharmacies can legally prepare them for patients under physician prescription. This is the most significant regulatory change for the peptide space in several years.

Peptides expected to return: BPC-157 (musculoskeletal repair, gut healing), GHK-Cu (collagen synthesis, wound healing, anti-aging), TB-500 (tissue repair, inflammation), CJC-1295 + Ipamorelin (growth hormone secretagogues), Thymosin Alpha-1 (immune modulation), Selank and Semax (cognitive/anxiolytic effects), KPV (gut barrier integrity), AOD-9604 (fat metabolism), MOTS-C (metabolic regulation).

🔬 In Plain English — Compounding Pharmacy
A compounding pharmacy is a licensed facility that makes custom medications not commercially available — mixing ingredients to specific doses or forms per a doctor's prescription. "Category 1" status means a compounding pharmacy is allowed to make these peptides and dispense them to patients. This is different from being FDA-approved: these peptides still lack completed clinical trials in humans for these uses.
🔬 In Plain English — BPC-157
BPC-157 (Body Protection Compound-157) is a synthetic 15-amino-acid peptide derived from a protein found in human gastric juice. In animal studies it accelerates healing of tendons, ligaments, muscle tissue, and gut lining by modulating nitric oxide and growth factor pathways. It's widely used in the athlete community for injury recovery, but human clinical trial data remains limited.
🔬 In Plain English — GHK-Cu
GHK-Cu is a copper-binding tripeptide (three amino acids) that naturally occurs in human blood, saliva, and urine. It modulates over 4,000 human genes according to genomic research — stimulating collagen production, wound healing, antioxidant defenses, and anti-inflammatory activity. It declines sharply with age (from ~200 ng/mL at age 20 to ~80 ng/mL at age 60).
⚠ Hype Watch — Regulatory Status
As of April 2026, the FDA has not yet published its formal updated list. The reclassification is announced but pending. Category 1 status is not FDA approval — these peptides lack completed multi-phase human clinical trials. Physician prescription, PCAB-accredited pharmacy, and clinical monitoring remain mandatory requirements. Expert Opinion
Australian legal context: This US reclassification does not change Australian law. In Australia, BPC-157, TB-500, and most peptides fall under Schedule 4 (prescription only) under the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA). They can be prescribed off-label by a physician but are not TGA-approved for listed conditions. Sourcing without prescription is illegal. For Australians: the US regulatory news is directionally positive (increases legitimate supply chain quality globally) but does not create new local access pathways.

Evidence status on BPC-157: Animal data is extensive and consistently positive for musculoskeletal repair, tendon and ligament healing, and gut integrity. Human data exists as case reports and practitioner-reported outcomes — no completed RCT in humans for injury recovery. The compound is classified as Mechanistic / Practitioner Reported for human use.

⚡ Implication — Protocols
US residents: monitor for the FDA's formal updated list before ordering through a compounding pharmacy — it's not yet active. Australians: the pathway remains physician prescription through a compounding pharmacy; the US reclassification signals more mainstream acceptance of these compounds but doesn't change your access options.
05 Movement & Load
ACSM Publishes New Resistance Training Guidelines — First Update Since 2009 Meta

The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) released its 2026 Position Stand on resistance training for healthy adults in April 2026 — the first major update in 17 years. The guidelines synthesise 137 systematic reviews and more than 30,000 participants, making it the most comprehensive official document on resistance training volume and intensity to date.

🔬 In Plain English — 1RM (One-Rep Maximum)
1RM is the maximum weight you can lift for exactly one repetition with good form. Training prescriptions often use percentages of 1RM: "lift at 80% 1RM" means use a weight that's 80% of your maximum. For most people, 80% 1RM means you could do roughly 6–8 reps before technical failure.

The headline numbers from the guidelines:

  • Hypertrophy: Aim for ~10 sets per muscle group per week (minimum effective volume)
  • Strength: Lift at ≥80% 1RM for 2–3 sets per exercise
  • Power: Moderate loads (30–70% 1RM), focus on concentric speed
  • Frequency: Train all major muscle groups at least twice per week
  • Key finding: Moving from no training to any training is more important than optimising any specific variable
The ACSM calling out 10 sets/muscle group for hypertrophy aligns with the RP Strength and evidence-based fitness community's volume recommendations for the past decade. The guidelines explicitly state that advanced techniques (failure training, complex periodization) are "not strictly necessary for general health outcomes." Useful for general population framing, but experienced lifters in growth phases are likely operating at or above these thresholds already. Meta
Volume → Hypertrophy: The Dose-Response Curve Has Diminishing Returns Meta

A February 2026 meta-regression in Sports Medicine by Pelland et al. (PMID 41343037) analysed 67 trials across 2,058 participants. Findings: muscle size and strength gains increase as weekly volume increases, but both plateau — with strength showing steeper diminishing returns than hypertrophy. Frequency effects on hypertrophy were statistically "compatible with negligible effects" — meaning hitting 10 sets per muscle once per week vs. splitting across two or three sessions makes little difference for size, though strength does respond positively to higher frequency.

🔬 In Plain English — Diminishing Returns in Training Volume
Diminishing returns means each additional set you add gives you less benefit than the previous one. Going from 5 to 10 sets per muscle group per week is likely worth a lot. Going from 20 to 25 sets probably adds very little while substantially increasing recovery demand. The optimal volume range sits where benefit per set is still meaningful — broadly 10–20 direct sets per muscle per week for most trained individuals.

Separately, a Journal of Applied Physiology study (Lees, 2026) confirms that low-load and high-load training produce similar hypertrophy outcomes in trained individuals — muscle thickness increased equivalently across loading conditions. The practical implication: if you're injured, fatigued, or travelling with limited equipment, lower-load higher-rep work is not a compromise for muscle retention. Meta

Post-Training Infrared Sauna: Power Yes, Hypertrophy No Observational

A case-control study in Frontiers in Sports and Active Living tested 40 female team sport athletes using infrared sauna (IRS) 3x per week for 10 minutes at 50°C post-training over 6 weeks. The result: no significant hypertrophy advantage over training alone. But the IRS group showed improved power production in loaded countermovement jumps — statistically significant improvements in jump height and peak power. Observational

🔬 In Plain English — Countermovement Jump (CMJ)
A countermovement jump (CMJ) is a standard test where you squat down then jump as high as possible. It measures explosive lower-body power — a combination of strength, rate of force development, and neuromuscular coordination. It's one of the most validated measures of athletic performance and training adaptation.

The parameters in this study matter: 50°C infrared is significantly lower temperature than traditional Finnish sauna (80–100°C), and 10 minutes is a short exposure. Traditional sauna literature uses 15–30 minute sessions at 80°C+. The power production finding is interesting but shouldn't be extrapolated beyond this specific protocol. Don't expect this protocol to replace deload or active recovery.

If you have access to infrared sauna and want to experiment post-training: 10 min at moderate heat is unlikely to blunt adaptation and may improve power expression over time. But if the choice is between sauna and an extra 20 minutes of sleep, sleep wins — directly, via the GH mechanism covered in Section 02.
This Cycle's Training Verdict
✅ What to Change
  • Audit your weekly sets per muscle group — if below 10, add volume before worrying about technique optimisation
  • If you're doing PPL 3x per week, you're likely hitting 12–15 sets per muscle on push and pull — which is solid
  • Confirm you're training each muscle group at least twice per week (6-day PPL does this)
  • If recovering poorly: consider reducing sets before reducing frequency
🚫 What NOT to Change
  • Don't switch to low-load training for hypertrophy thinking you need heavy loads — evidence is load-agnostic for muscle size
  • Don't add frequency chasing strength gains if recovery is already compromised
  • Don't over-invest in post-training sauna expecting hypertrophy gains — there aren't any above training alone
  • Don't chase ACSM "10 set minimum" as a ceiling — it's a floor for trained individuals
⚡ Implication — Movement
If you're on a PPL split, you're likely already above the ACSM's 10-set/muscle/week threshold. The volume question is whether you're at maximum recoverable volume — signs you've crossed it: joint tenderness, stalling strength, poor sleep despite good habits, persistent soreness beyond 72h.
