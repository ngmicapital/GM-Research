The Experiment That Never Happened

12 April 2026 · Sunday

The Stanford Prison Experiment — psychology's most famous study — was not an experiment at all: guards were coached, conclusions were written before the data was collected, and the lead researcher was playing a character. The mechanism that made it stick was not science but narrative — a story so clean and disturbing that textbooks, films, and policy-makers repeated it for fifty years without reading the original notes.

TL;DR

In August 1971, Philip Zimbardo staged a two-week prison simulation in Stanford's basement, halted it after six days, and told the world that ordinary people, given institutional power, will spontaneously become monsters. It became the most cited experiment in social psychology. Then a French researcher spent years reading the original field notes — and found that the guards were coached, the prisoners were denied their legal right to leave, one "breakdown" was admitted to be faked, and Zimbardo himself was directing the cruelty from the start. The real lesson is not about prisons. It is about how science becomes myth.

Section 01

The Story: A Dungeon Beneath Jordan Hall

In the summer of 1971, a 26-year-old psychology professor named Philip Zimbardo converted the basement of Stanford University's Jordan Hall into a mock prison. He recruited 24 male undergraduates — healthy, psychologically normal, coin-flip assigned to be either "guards" or "prisoners." The prisoners were arrested at home by real Palo Alto police, processed, stripped, deloused, and given smocks and stocking caps. Guards wore khaki uniforms and mirrored sunglasses. The study was supposed to run two weeks.

What happened next became legend. Within days, guards began brutalising prisoners — waking them at 2am for counts, making them do push-ups, humiliating them with naked "games," spraying them with fire extinguishers. Prisoners broke down. One began screaming and had to be removed. Zimbardo — playing the role of prison superintendent — watched and did not intervene. His girlfriend, Christina Maslach, visited on Day 5 and was so horrified she threatened to leave him if it continued. He stopped the experiment on Day 6.

The study's conclusion was thunderous: situational forces, not individual character, produce evil. Put ordinary people in institutional roles and the role will consume them. Guards become sadists. Prisoners become helpless. The prison makes the prisoner. This finding exploded into the mainstream — into textbooks, documentaries, the Abu Ghraib defence ("systemic forces made those soldiers do it"), and the 2015 Hollywood film starring Billy Crudup as Zimbardo.

Zimbardo spent the next five decades building a career around the findings. He testified before the US Senate, gave TED talks, wrote a bestselling book, and watched the experiment become one of the most assigned readings in psychology courses globally. By 2018, when a French researcher named Thibault Le Texier filed a request for the original field notes from Stanford's archive, the SPE was effectively scripture.

Section 02

The Mechanism: A Demonstration, Not an Experiment

Le Texier's 2018 book, Histoire d'un Mensonge (published in English as Investigating the Stanford Prison Experiment: History of a Lie in 2024), is one of the most systematic debunkings in the history of science. He spent years reading every document in the archive — interview transcripts, field notes, audio recordings, observer logs — and what he found inverted the official story at almost every point.

"The SPE functioned as a demonstration created to depict the evils of incarceration rather than as a rigorous scientific study of the effects of role playing in a simulated prison." — Thibault Le Texier, American Psychologist, 2019

The experiment had none of the basic features of a scientific study: no pre-registered hypothesis, no validated measurement instruments, no significance tests, no control group. Zimbardo had no expertise in criminology — his training was in laboratory rat psychology. The conclusions, Le Texier found evidence to suggest, were written before the data was collected. This was not science that found a result. It was a demonstration that was staged to produce one.

Here is the mechanism in detail:

DAY 0 — GUARD ORIENTATION
Before the experiment began, guards attended a "Day 0" orientation session run by Zimbardo and his research assistant. They were handed a set of rules and a daily schedule for prisoners, told that their job was to make inmates feel "powerless," and coached on how to create a psychologically hostile environment. Several guards were subsequently reprimanded for not being dominant enough and urged by graduate students to be tougher.
THE TEMPLATE
Le Texier discovered that the rules handed to guards were nearly identical to rules written months earlier by David Jaffe, a student of Zimbardo's who had run a nearly identical simulation as a course assignment. Zimbardo obscured Jaffe's foundational contribution for 40 years. The "spontaneous" brutality of the guards was modelled on a script that predated the study.
THE DRAMA MAJOR
The most notorious guard — nicknamed "John Wayne" for his cruelty, now identified — was a drama major who later admitted he was performing a character. He adopted a fake Southern accent and consciously modelled his performance on film guards he had seen. When audio recordings surfaced, Le Texier found this guard being praised and encouraged by the research team for his behaviour.
THE FAKED BREAKDOWN
Prisoner #8612 — Douglas Korpi — had what appeared to be a catastrophic psychological breakdown on Day 2, screaming and raging until Zimbardo released him. In 2017, Korpi told journalist Ben Blum that the breakdown was deliberately faked: he had discovered mid-experiment that he could not simply quit, panicked about an upcoming exam, and performed distress until he was let out. Zimbardo disputed Korpi's account; Korpi maintained it.
ZIMBARDO AS SUPERINTENDENT
Zimbardo did not observe the experiment from a neutral position. He played the "prison superintendent" as a character, wore the uniform, and actively reinforced authoritarian behaviour. When guards were insufficiently aggressive, he or his staff corrected them. The "situational forces" that produced the cruelty were, in significant part, Zimbardo himself.

The 2024 National Geographic three-part documentary series The Stanford Prison Experiment: Unlocking the Truth interviewed surviving participants, most of whom confirmed Le Texier's account. Retraction Watch published a guest post in March 2025 formally asking whether the original 1973 paper should be retracted. As of 2026, Stanford has not retracted it.

Section 03

The Connections: Why a Fake Study Had Real Consequences

The SPE is not an isolated fraud. It sits at the centre of a cluster of social psychology studies that defined the field's public identity — and have since failed to survive scrutiny.

The Milgram Obedience Studies (1961–62) — the electric shock experiments where subjects appeared to harm strangers on instruction — have also been extensively criticised. Gina Perry's 2012 book Behind the Shock Machine found that Milgram's experimenters deviated from protocol, coerced participants who tried to stop, and that fewer subjects actually continued to the highest shock level than the famous "65%" figure suggests. Like Zimbardo, Milgram was a storyteller first.

The Bystander Effect — the finding that people are less likely to help in emergencies when others are present, drawn from studies after Kitty Genovese's 1964 murder — has failed replication in large-scale natural experiments. A 2019 study of real CCTV footage across three countries found bystanders intervened in 91% of street conflicts. The original premise was also built on a misreported news story: the New York Times account of Genovese's murder significantly exaggerated the indifference of witnesses.

These are not coincidences. The 2010s replication crisis — in which roughly 50-60% of classic social psychology findings failed to replicate — exposed a systematic problem: the field had been producing dramatic, press-release-friendly findings by running underpowered studies, selectively reporting results, and telling stories that confirmed what people already wanted to believe about human nature.

"The most dangerous kind of bad science is the kind that tells us something true-sounding about ourselves." — Observer's note on the replication crisis, approximate

The SPE survived because its narrative was perfect. It confirmed the fears of prison reformers, satisfied those who believed institutions corrupt people, and gave anyone who had ever behaved badly in a group a convenient explanation. It was not science. It was a very effective story about science. And for fifty years, that was enough.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

📖

The Replication Crisis in Full

The 2015 Reproducibility Project attempted to replicate 100 psychology experiments. Only 36-39% produced similar results. Stuart Ritchie's book Science Fictions (2020) is the clearest account of how this happened and what it means.

⚡

Milgram's Hidden Tape Recordings

Yale's archive contains audio of Milgram's experimenters threatening and pressuring participants who tried to stop. Gina Perry's investigation in Behind the Shock Machine reveals what the published papers omitted. The coercion was the variable.

🏛️

Abu Ghraib and the SPE Defence

Zimbardo testified in the court martial of Abu Ghraib guard Ivan "Chip" Frederick, arguing situational forces explained the abuse. The defence failed. But it raised a genuine question: if the SPE was fabricated, what does that mean for how we understand institutional abuse?

🧠

What Actually Determines Behaviour in Institutions

The real psychology of institutional cruelty points not to "roles" but to leader modelling, explicit permission, and dehumanisation of targets — factors that are all present at Abu Ghraib and all absent from controlled studies of role-playing alone.

🔬

Pre-Registration: The Actual Fix

Modern psychology's response to the replication crisis is pre-registration: researchers file their hypotheses, methods, and analysis plans before collecting data. This makes it impossible to quietly reframe a null result as a positive finding. It is now mandatory for many journals.
