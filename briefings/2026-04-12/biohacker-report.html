Section 01 — Longevity & Biomarkers

Aging as Coordination Failure — Not a Single Clock Running Down

[Observational]

Targeting Longevity World Congress 2026: The Coordination Hypothesis

The April 8–9 Targeting Longevity World Congress in Berlin (organised by the World Mitochondria Society and International Society of Microbiota) marked a meaningful shift in how the field frames the problem of aging. The dominant new framing: aging is not a clock winding down but a progressive loss of coordination between biological systems — mitochondria, microbiota, immune, hormonal, and metabolic systems gradually falling out of sync with each other.

This reframing matters practically. If aging is a single mechanism (e.g., telomere shortening, DNA damage accumulation), you look for a single intervention. If it is a coordination failure, you target multiple systems simultaneously — which aligns with what the actual data on longevity interventions shows: exercise, sleep, nutrition, and stress management working together outperform any single-compound approach by a wide margin.

🔬 In Plain English
Mitochondrial biogenesis = your cells making new energy-producing machinery. When mitochondria age or accumulate damage, cellular energy production falls and inflammation rises. Exercise (especially Zone 2 cardio) is the strongest known stimulus for making new, healthy mitochondria.
Analysis
For dc: the "coordination" model vindicates doing all the basics well rather than chasing one longevity compound. The stack that wins is multi-modal — Zone 2, resistance training, sleep optimisation, creatine, good protein — not a single peptide.
[Observational]

UK Biobank Study: Small Habits, Measurable Lifespan Gain

Published in eClinicalMedicine, a study of almost 60,000 UK Biobank participants followed for an average of eight years found that consistent small improvements across diet, exercise, and sleep could add one or more years to lifespan. No single intervention dominated — the benefit was in the combination.

For a 20–40 year old male, this is not the most urgent finding (the payoffs compound over decades). But it reinforces one principle: the highest-leverage longevity investment is habit consistency, not supplementation optimisation. Sort your sleep before you add another compound to the stack.

Analysis
This is an observational study — it cannot prove causation. But 60,000 participants over 8 years with consistent directional findings is strong evidence. The effect size is modest but real.
⚡ Implication
Optimise the fundamentals stack first (Zone 2, resistance training, sleep, creatine, protein) — the coordination model suggests this beats any single longevity compound.
Section 02 — Recovery & Sleep

Daily Movement Is the Most Underrated Sleep Intervention

[RCT]

UT Austin Study: Even 10 Minutes of Daily Exercise Improves Sleep Quality

New research from The University of Texas at Austin confirms that exercising more frequently — ideally every day — measurably improves sleep quality, particularly the proportion of deep restorative sleep (slow-wave sleep) and subjective mood the following day. The threshold is low: as little as 10 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity was enough to produce measurable improvements in the young adults studied.

The mechanism: exercise increases adenosine build-up (the "sleep pressure" molecule), lowers core body temperature post-exercise, and reduces evening cortisol — all of which improve sleep onset and depth. The implication for people doing PPL is that rest days with zero movement are suboptimal for sleep. Active recovery — a 20-minute walk, light mobility — preserves the sleep benefit without adding training load.

🔬 In Plain English
Adenosine = a chemical that builds up in your brain the longer you are awake, creating "sleep pressure." Coffee works by blocking adenosine receptors — temporarily. Exercise accelerates adenosine production, making your body want sleep earlier and sleep more deeply. Caffeine after 2pm blunts this mechanism.
🔬 In Plain English
Slow-wave sleep (SWS) = the deepest stage of sleep, also called N3. This is when the brain clears metabolic waste (via the glymphatic system), muscle repair hormones peak, and memories consolidate. More SWS = better recovery from training and better cognitive performance the next day.
[Observational]

Bedroom Environment Research: Temperature and Air Quality Are Not Noise

Studies published in Environmental Research and Sleep Medicine Reviews confirm that bedroom temperature, humidity, and air quality produce measurable effects on sleep duration, fragmentation, and time in deep sleep stages. The optimal sleep temperature range is 16–19°C (60–67°F) — cooler than most people keep their rooms. Even modest deviations (a bedroom at 22°C vs 17°C) reduce slow-wave and REM sleep measurably.

Analysis
This is actionable tonight. If you are not sleeping in a cool room, you are paying a recovery tax every night. A fan, cracked window, or air con set to 17–19°C is the cheapest sleep intervention available.
Quick Reference — Sleep Environment Stack
  • Room temperature: 16–19°C — use AC, fan, or window; err cooler
  • Blackout curtains or sleep mask — darkness is more important than most people think
  • No screens 30+ min before bed (or blue-light glasses if unavoidable)
  • Magnesium glycinate 300–400mg before bed — reduces cortisol, improves sleep depth
  • No caffeine after 2pm — half-life is 5–7 hours, so a 3pm coffee is still 50% active at 8–10pm
⚡ Implication
On PPL rest days, add 20 min of walking rather than going fully sedentary — preserves sleep quality without adding training load.
Section 03 — Nutrition & Fuel

Creatine Is Now a Cognitive and Longevity Supplement, Not Just a Gym Tool

[Meta]

Creatine and Cognition in Aging: 5/6 Studies Show Positive Effect

A systematic review published in Nutrition Reviews (Oxford Academic) found that five of six eligible studies reported a positive relationship between creatine supplementation and cognition in older adults, particularly in the domains of memory and attention. Emerging data from a forthcoming study indicates that 10 grams per day — regardless of exercise — leads to improvements in muscle mass, body composition, and cognition in elderly subjects.

🔬 In Plain English
Creatine = a molecule your body uses to regenerate ATP (the cellular energy currency) rapidly. Your muscles store it for short explosive bursts; your brain also uses it heavily during cognitively demanding tasks. Supplementing creatine tops up both reservoirs. In the brain, higher creatine availability = better short-term cognitive performance and potentially slower cognitive decline with age.
[RCT]

Combined Creatine + Carbs + Protein: Synergistic Sprint Performance

A study published in Nature Scientific Reports (April 2026) found that combined supplementation of creatine, carbohydrates, and protein before consecutive Wingate Anaerobic Tests produced superior mean power output and mitigated fatigue more effectively than any single supplement or placebo. The synergy appears to come from creatine replenishing phosphocreatine stores while protein and carbohydrates support recovery between efforts.

For dc doing PPL: this means a pre-training shake combining whey protein + fast carbs + creatine (rather than creatine alone or protein alone) is likely the highest-leverage peri-workout stack.

Practical Protocol
Current evidence-based creatine dosing: 3–5g/day for performance and muscle; emerging evidence suggests 5–10g/day for cognitive and longevity benefits in aging. Creatine monohydrate remains the only form with strong evidence — no need for buffered, ethyl ester, or other variants.
Quick Reference — Creatine Stack
  • 5g creatine monohydrate daily (any time, with food reduces GI discomfort)
  • Pre-training: 30–40g whey protein + 30–50g fast carbs (e.g., rice cakes, banana) + 5g creatine
  • Post-training: another 30–40g protein within 1–2 hours
  • No loading phase required — steady-state is reached in 3–4 weeks at 5g/day
⚡ Implication
Consider increasing creatine to 5–10g/day if cognitive performance is a priority — the longevity and brain data now justifies dosing above the traditional 3–5g gym recommendation.
Section 04 — Protocols

Sauna: The Longevity Protocol Backed by the Strongest Population Data

Of all the biohacking protocols that have accumulated serious research behind them, regular sauna use stands out as having the most consistent population-level longevity and cardiovascular data. This is not supplement company marketing. The primary data comes from Finnish epidemiological studies tracking tens of thousands of sauna users across decades.

[Observational]

The Cardiovascular Case for Sauna

Finnish cohort studies (most prominently from Professor Jari Laukkanen at the University of Eastern Finland) found that men who used sauna 4–7 times per week had a 65% lower risk of Alzheimer's and dementia, 50% lower risk of cardiovascular disease, and 40% lower all-cause mortality compared to once-weekly users. Even 2–3 sessions per week produced significant benefit over once-weekly.

Hype Watch
These are observational correlations — people who sauna regularly in Finland also tend to have healthier lifestyles overall. The effect sizes are unusually large for observational data, which is why the field takes them seriously, but causation is not proven.
🔬 In Plain English
Heat shock proteins (HSPs) = protective molecules your cells produce when exposed to heat stress. They act like molecular chaperones — refolding damaged proteins before they accumulate and cause cellular dysfunction. Higher HSP levels are associated with better cellular repair, reduced inflammation, and longer lifespan in animal models.
[Expert Opinion]

Evidence-Based Sauna Protocol

Based on Laukkanen's data and Andrew Huberman's protocol synthesis [Expert Opinion — Huberman Lab], the current evidence-supported sauna protocol for longevity and cardiovascular benefit:

Protocol Parameters
  • Temperature: 80–100°C (175–212°F)
  • Duration: 15–20 min per session
  • Frequency: 2–3x per week minimum
  • Timing: Post-training or evening (not pre-training)
  • Hydration: 500–750ml water during and after
Mechanisms Active
  • Heat shock protein induction
  • Plasma volume expansion (cardiovascular benefit)
  • Growth hormone pulse (fasted sauna amplifies this)
  • Cortisol reduction (post-session)
  • BDNF increase (brain-derived neurotrophic factor)
🔬 In Plain English
BDNF (Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor) = a protein your brain produces that stimulates growth and maintenance of neurons. Higher BDNF is associated with better memory, learning, and resilience to depression. Sauna, cold exposure, and Zone 2 exercise are the three strongest non-pharmacological BDNF stimulators.
Australian Context
Most commercial gyms in Sydney with premium memberships (e.g., Fitness First Platinum, Goodlife, boutique gyms) have saunas. Using the gym sauna post-training 3x/week adds ~20 minutes to a session and costs nothing extra if already a member. If no gym access, portable infrared sauna tents (~$300–600 AUD) produce HSP benefits at lower temperatures (50–60°C) with longer sessions (30–45 min).
⚡ Implication
If not already doing it, add 15–20 min post-training sauna 2–3x per week — it is one of the few protocols with genuine population-level longevity data, not just mechanism studies.
Section 05 — Movement & Load

ACSM 2026 Guidelines Rewrite Strength Training Science — 10 Sets/Week Is the Number

[Meta]

ACSM 2026 Position Stand: First Update Since 2009

The American College of Sports Medicine published its first major resistance training guidelines update since 2009 — a Position Stand synthesising 137 systematic reviews representing 30,000+ participants. The key findings for someone doing PPL:

Key Finding #1 — Hypertrophy
~10 sets per muscle group per week is the primary driver of hypertrophy. Higher volume (beyond 10 sets/week) provides marginal additional benefit for most people. Total weekly volume matters more than how the sets are distributed across training days.
Key Finding #2 — Load Range
Load range (light vs heavy) does not significantly affect hypertrophy when effort is equated. 3 sets of 20 reps to near-failure produces similar muscle growth to 3 sets of 8 reps to near-failure. Load range matters more for strength: 80%+ of 1RM in 2–3 sets per exercise is still the recommendation for pure strength gains.
Key Finding #3 — Myths Debunked
Training to momentary failure, specific equipment, exercise variety, blood flow restriction, and specific periodisation schemes did not consistently impact outcomes when total volume was controlled. The old "you must train to failure every set" advice is not supported.
🔬 In Plain English
1RM (One-Repetition Maximum) = the heaviest weight you can lift for exactly one full rep with good form. Training at 80% of your 1RM means using a weight you could lift ~8 times before failure. For strength (neurological adaptation), the research still favours heavier loads and lower reps. For size (hypertrophy, muscle protein synthesis), effort level matters more than the specific weight used.
🔬 In Plain English
RIR (Reps in Reserve) = how many more reps you could have done before muscular failure. A set done to 1–2 RIR is near-failure. Research shows most hypertrophy benefit comes from sets done to 1–3 RIR — you do NOT need to go to zero every set, and doing so increases fatigue without proportional extra growth.
What to Change (If Needed)
  • Audit weekly sets per muscle — aim for 10+ per group
  • Stop training every set to failure — 1–3 RIR on most sets
  • Add load variation across rep ranges (8–12 AND 15–20)
  • Count total volume, not session volume
  • Eccentric overload 1–2 sets per session is worth adding
What NOT to Change
  • Progressive overload — still the primary driver
  • Compound lifts as the foundation (squat, RDL, press, row)
  • PPL structure — frequency and variety remain effective
  • Protein intake — need at least 1.6–2.2g/kg/day regardless
  • Sleep and recovery — no amount of volume fixes poor sleep
⚡ Implication
If your weekly sets per muscle group are below 10, add volume before adding complexity — the 2026 guidelines confirm that fundamentals outperform programming sophistication.
