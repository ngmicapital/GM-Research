Driving Thesis

Three research advances reshape the longevity and performance model this cycle. On inflammaging, a six-year NIH-funded study from the University at Buffalo (Aging and Disease, January 2026) establishes that Tristetraprolin (TTP) — an RNA-binding protein that degrades pro-inflammatory messenger RNA signals — declines with age and its loss drives the chronic low-grade inflammation that accelerates physical and immune aging. Elderly mice with genetically boosted TTP levels were measurably stronger, had healthier bones, and displayed more youthful immune profiles than untreated controls; the finding reframes inflammaging not as an inevitable consequence of aging but as a programmable regulatory failure with a specific molecular target now under active drug-screening investigation. In training science, a systematic review and meta-analysis published in the Journal of Gerontology: Series A (Ryall & Denham, University of Southern Queensland, June 2025, 434 participants across 7 studies) resolves a critical VO2max question: being in the top 30% of cardiorespiratory fitness for age predicts significantly longer telomeres versus the unfit — but being in the top 10% confers no additional telomere benefit over the top 30%. Extreme training volume is not required for cellular anti-aging; sustainable aerobic fitness in the achievable moderate range is the target. On supplementation, a 100-participant double-blind RCT published in Frontiers in Nutrition (Lopresti & Smith, January 2026) shows that 2 g/day magnesium L-threonate for six weeks improved estimated cognitive age by 7.5 years and enhanced working memory and reaction time via NIH Toolbox — compounding mounting evidence that magnesium is one of the most consequential and widely under-optimized nutritional levers available, with an estimated 48–68% of U.S. adults failing to meet adequate intake.

Section 01
Longevity & Performance Science

The TTP/inflammaging finding is mechanistically significant because it identifies a specific molecular regulator — not a general anti-inflammatory drug, but a post-transcriptional control protein — whose age-dependent decline is causally sufficient to produce the hallmarks of inflammaging. TTP works by binding to AU-rich elements in pro-inflammatory mRNA (including TNF-α and IL-6 transcripts) and marking them for rapid degradation; when TTP expression falls with age, those inflammatory signals persist longer than they should, creating the chronic low-level inflammatory tone associated with accelerated cellular and tissue aging. The mouse data is strong — the effect on bone density, immune profile, and physical function is dose-responsive and consistent — but TTP enhancement in humans remains a drug-discovery challenge. No oral compound has yet demonstrated reliable TTP upregulation; the researchers are in early screening mode. The practical angle is indirect but real: the interventions already known to sustain TTP expression include regular exercise (AMPK activation sustains TTP transcription), omega-3 fatty acids (which compete with arachidonic acid substrates that drive inflammatory mRNA production), and caloric moderation (chronic energy surplus suppresses TTP via mTOR). In other words, the TTP biology provides a molecular framework for why the anti-inflammatory lifestyle interventions already endorsed in this report work at the cellular level.

The University of Sydney four-week diet study is more modest in its claims but represents a clean signal: three of four dietary arms achieved measurable reductions in biological age markers (as assessed via 20 validated blood biomarkers including cholesterol, insulin, and CRP), with the omnivorous high-carbohydrate group showing the strongest statistical evidence. The finding challenges clean low-carbohydrate framing in the longevity space, though the biomarker composite is not an epigenetic clock — it reflects metabolic state rather than epigenetic aging pace directly.

Tristetraprolin (TTP) Protein Decline Drives Inflammaging — Boosting It in Aged Mice Restores Strength, Bone Health, and Immune Youth in a 6-Year NIH Study
[Mechanistic]

Researchers at the University at Buffalo School of Dental Medicine completed a six-year, NIH-funded ($2.1 million) investigation into the role of Tristetraprolin (TTP) in the biology of aging, published in Aging and Disease (January 2026). TTP is an RNA-binding protein that post-transcriptionally degrades pro-inflammatory messenger RNA transcripts — including those encoding TNF-α, IL-6, and GM-CSF — by binding to AU-rich elements (AREs) in their 3’ untranslated regions and flagging them for rapid cellular destruction. Without sufficient TTP, these inflammatory signals persist far longer than warranted, generating the chronic low-grade inflammatory state known as inflammaging that underlies much of the functional decline of aging.

The finding: TTP expression declines with age as a consistent biological phenomenon across tissues. Elderly mice (22 months, equivalent to approximately 65–70 human years) with genetically enhanced TTP expression showed measurably better outcomes versus age-matched untreated controls across three domains. Physical performance: higher grip strength and locomotor activity. Skeletal health: reduced bone resorption markers, denser trabecular bone, lower osteoclast activity. Immune profile: the treated mice displayed a more youthful immune cell distribution — lower proportion of senescent T-cells and inflammatory macrophage phenotypes, with an NK cell profile closer to younger animals. The effect was stronger in male mice than female mice, likely reflecting sex hormone interactions with TTP transcription that are not yet characterized. Lead investigator Dr. Keith Sullivan noted: “These mice had healthier bones and reduced bone breakdown. They exhibited a more youthful-looking immune profile.”

The mechanism is precise: TTP acts as a post-transcriptional rheostat on the inflammatory response. Its decline with age is not random noise — it is structurally driven. mTOR signaling (elevated in nutrient excess and reduced in caloric restriction) suppresses TTP transcription. Chronic oxidative stress damages TTP mRNA itself. Epigenetic silencing of the ZFP36 gene locus (which encodes TTP) accumulates with age. Collectively, these inputs create a pro-inflammatory bias that TTP can no longer counteract, and the resulting systemic inflammation accelerates sarcopenia, immune senescence, and bone loss through shared downstream pathways.

Human translation remains distant. Early drug-screening efforts at Buffalo have not yet identified a reliable small-molecule TTP inducer with acceptable pharmacokinetics. The indirect approach — supporting the upstream conditions that sustain TTP — is the actionable angle for now: resistance and aerobic exercise activate AMPK and reduce mTOR tone; omega-3 fatty acids reduce substrate competition for inflammatory eicosanoid production; caloric moderation reduces the mTOR-mediated TTP suppression. These mechanisms are now mechanistically grounded in the TTP biology in a way they were not before this study.

Four Weeks of Diet Change Measurably Reduces Biological Age Markers in Adults Aged 65–75 — University of Sydney, Four Randomized Arms, Aging Cell 2026
[RCT]

Researchers at the University of Sydney enrolled 104 adults aged 65–75 in a four-week randomized dietary trial comparing four arms: omnivorous high-fat/low-carbohydrate; omnivorous high-carbohydrate/low-fat; semi-vegetarian high-fat/low-carbohydrate; and semi-vegetarian high-carbohydrate/low-fat. All diets were held at 14% energy from protein to isolate carbohydrate-fat ratio and protein source as the independent variables. Biological age was assessed via a validated 20-biomarker composite including fasting insulin, LDL cholesterol, C-reactive protein, triglycerides, and glucose — a metabolic state index rather than an epigenetic clock. Published in Aging Cell (Volume 25, Issue 5, 2026).

Results: three of four dietary arms showed significant reductions in biological age estimates after four weeks. The omnivorous high-carbohydrate/low-fat group showed the strongest and most statistically consistent signal, driven primarily by improvements in insulin sensitivity, CRP, and triglyceride markers. The semi-vegetarian arms showed smaller but still significant effects; the high-fat/low-carbohydrate omnivore arm showed the least improvement in this metabolic composite. The authors note that the high-carbohydrate advantage here is likely driven by dietary fiber intake — the high-carbohydrate groups were consuming substantially more plant-based fiber, which drives the glucose-triglyceride and inflammatory improvements measured.

Three methodological caveats are important for interpretation. First, four weeks is a short observation window: changes in metabolic biomarkers are reversible and may not reflect durable shifts in epigenetic aging pace. Second, the biomarker composite — while validated — is not a direct measure of biological aging rate (DunedinPACE, PCGrimAge); it captures a metabolic phenotype that correlates with aging but is not equivalent to measuring aging speed itself. Third, the protein source manipulation (omnivore vs. semi-vegetarian) was not large enough to isolate a clear protein-source signal independently of the carbohydrate-fat ratio. The study is best read as evidence that short-term dietary quality improvements produce measurable biological age marker changes rapidly in older adults — supporting the responsiveness of the system, not a prescription for specific macronutrient ratios.

Source: ScienceDaily · May 11, 2026 · Aging Cell 2026 (Volume 25, Issue 5) · University of Sydney · 104 adults aged 65–75 · 4-week RCT
⚡ Implication: The TTP biology provides molecular grounding for why the anti-inflammatory lifestyle stack — resistance training, omega-3s, caloric moderation — works at the cellular level: each intervention directly supports TTP expression or reduces its suppression. The drug-discovery angle is premature for human use but the regulatory pathway is now mapped. The Sydney diet finding is best used as an argument for dietary quality responsiveness in older adults — four weeks of dietary intervention moves measurable biology. Longer follow-up will determine whether this translates to epigenetic aging pace.
Section 02
Training & Performance

The VO2max/telomere meta-analysis from the Journal of Gerontology is important not just as a longevity signal but as a dosing insight. The finding that the top 30% fitness threshold (70th percentile VO2max for age/sex) delivers equivalent telomere maintenance to the top 10% (90th+ percentile) means that the cellular anti-aging return on fitness follows a diminishing returns curve after a moderate fitness level is achieved. For PPL lifters who do not do significant dedicated cardio training: the telomere protection target is reaching and maintaining the 70th percentile VO2max for age and sex — not elite aerobic performance. In practical terms for males aged 25–40, the 70th percentile VO2max is approximately 48–52 mL/kg/min; for females in the same bracket, approximately 40–44 mL/kg/min. These are very achievable targets with 2–3 moderate-to-vigorous cardio sessions per week alongside a PPL program.

The mechanism linking VO2max to telomere length runs through oxidative stress management: aerobically fit individuals express higher superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase activity, reduce chronic reactive oxygen species (ROS) production per mitochondria via greater mitochondrial density, and maintain lower resting cortisol — all of which reduce the rate of telomere oxidative damage. Exercise also upregulates telomerase activity transiently, with chronic repeated bouts sustaining this at a level that partly counteracts replication-related shortening. The key point from the meta-analysis: beyond the 70th percentile, additional aerobic fitness does not linearly add telomere protection, suggesting that the anti-ROS and telomerase-upregulation mechanisms plateau at moderate fitness levels rather than scaling with elite performance.

Top 30% Cardiorespiratory Fitness for Age Is Sufficient for Telomere Protection — Extreme Training Adds No Extra Benefit Over Moderate Fitness (Journal of Gerontology Meta-Analysis, 434 Participants)
[Meta-Analysis]

A systematic review and meta-analysis published in The Journals of Gerontology: Series A (Volume 80, Issue 6, June 2025; DOI: 10.1093/gerona/glaf068) by Clodagh Ryall and Dr. Joshua Denham at the University of Southern Queensland, Australia, pooled data from 7 studies with 8 comparisons and a total of 434 individuals to assess the relationship between cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2max) and telomere length across all tissues. An additional 11 eligible studies could not provide the necessary data for inclusion.

The main findings, comparing individuals classified by fitness percentile for their age and sex: individuals at or above the 70th percentile of VO2max for their age/sex group possessed significantly longer telomeres than unfit individuals (standardized mean difference: 0.36 [95% CI: 0.14–0.59], p = 0.002). Individuals at or above the 90th percentile also possessed significantly longer telomeres versus unfit controls (SMD: 0.28 [0.03–0.53], p = 0.03). The critical finding: no statistically significant difference emerged between the 70th–90th percentile group and the above-90th percentile group (SMD: −0.08 [−0.40–0.24], p = 0.62). Extreme endurance training volume confers no additional telomere benefit over moderate fitness levels.

The authors conclude: “These translatable findings offer achievable cardiorespiratory fitness targets associated with telomere maintenance and healthy biological aging.” The directionality of the effect is consistent with prior research: aerobically fit individuals express higher antioxidant enzyme activity (superoxide dismutase, catalase), maintain greater mitochondrial density (reducing ROS production per mitochondrion), and show transient telomerase upregulation following aerobic exercise bouts. The plateau beyond 70th percentile fitness suggests these mechanisms saturate at moderate fitness rather than scaling linearly with training volume.

Practical context: in the USA-based FRIEND Registry norms, the 70th percentile VO2max for males aged 30–39 is approximately 49–51 mL/kg/min; for females in the same bracket, approximately 42–44 mL/kg/min. These are achievable with 2–3 moderate-to-vigorous aerobic sessions per week (running, cycling, rowing at 70–80% max HR). You do not need to be a competitive endurance athlete to obtain the telomere maintenance benefit. For PPL practitioners: adding one or two higher-intensity cardio sessions per week to reach or sustain the 70th-percentile fitness level is the minimum viable aerobic investment for cellular longevity — additional volume beyond that point is not compounding on the cellular aging return.

Source: The Journals of Gerontology: Series A, Volume 80, Issue 6, glaf068 (June 2025) · Ryall C & Denham J · University of Southern Queensland · 7 studies, 434 participants · Meta-analysis · PMC12104809
Integrate Now
  • Target the 70th-percentile VO2max for your age/sex as the fitness threshold for telomere maintenance — this is the cellular anti-aging target, not elite performance
  • 2–3 sessions per week reaching 70–80% max HR (running, cycling, rowing) is sufficient to reach and sustain this threshold alongside PPL training
  • Use a smartwatch VO2max estimate (validated at ±3–5 mL/kg/min accuracy) to track progress toward and within the 70th-percentile range; annual re-assessment is sufficient
What to Watch
  • The meta-analysis included only 7 studies and 434 total participants — a relatively small base. The plateau finding is directionally consistent with prior HIIT/exercise biology but needs larger samples to be definitive
  • Telomere length is a biomarker of cellular aging, not a direct mortality predictor — longer telomeres reduce senescent cell accumulation but are not the only longevity pathway
  • The plateau finding does not mean more exercise is harmful — it means additional telomere protection doesn’t accrue; cardiovascular fitness, VO2max itself, and metabolic adaptations continue to improve with higher training volumes
⚡ Implication: The 70th-percentile VO2max target is achievable with 2–3 moderate-high-intensity cardio sessions per week — the cellular longevity return does not require elite aerobic training. For PPL practitioners: the aerobic supplement to resistance training needs to be consistent and reach a meaningful intensity (70–80% max HR), but does not need to be high volume. Going from unfit to moderately fit is where all the telomere protection value is captured.
Section 03
Supplements & Nutrition

The magnesium L-threonate RCT (Lopresti & Smith, January 2026) adds a new dimension to the magnesium story: the blood-brain-barrier-crossing form of magnesium specifically improves cognitive age estimates and working memory in a 100-person double-blind placebo-controlled trial. The 7.5-year cognitive age improvement is measured via a function-based assessment (not an epigenetic clock), which measures how the brain performs relative to age norms — so this is a performance signal, not a structural aging measure. The mechanism is well-established: magnesium is a co-factor in NMDA receptor function, synaptic plasticity, and ATP-dependent neural processes; L-threonate's ability to cross the blood-brain barrier allows systemic supplementation to actually elevate brain tissue magnesium, unlike standard forms (magnesium glycinate, citrate, oxide) that raise serum magnesium without proportionally raising brain magnesium. The sleep improvement signal is secondary but consistent with magnesium's role in GABA receptor activation and hypothalamic thermoregulation.

The broader magnesium picture is now crystallized by the Magnesium Depletion Score (MDS) scoping review (PMC12566843, 48 studies), which formalizes a composite clinical risk tool for identifying magnesium-deficient individuals. The MDS assigns points for five depletion factors — diuretic use, proton pump inhibitor use, heavy alcohol consumption, mildly reduced kidney function, and chronic kidney disease — with an MDS >2 predicting deficiency. The conditions associated with high MDS span almost every chronic disease category: CVD, T2D, hypertension, osteoporosis, Parkinson’s, sleep disorders, and depression. The dose-dependent association with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality is the most consistently replicated finding across the 48 included studies. For people on common medications (PPIs, diuretics) who also drink alcohol regularly and have early kidney decline (increasingly common in the 40+ age group), the magnesium deficit may be severe and persistent without active dietary or supplemental correction.

Magnesium L-Threonate (2 g/day, 6 Weeks) Improves Cognitive Age Estimate by 7.5 Years and Enhances Working Memory — Double-Blind RCT, 100 Adults, Frontiers in Nutrition 2026
[RCT]

A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial by Lopresti and Smith published in Frontiers in Nutrition (DOI: 10.3389/fnut.2025.1729164; accepted December 2025, published January 2026) enrolled 100 adults aged 18–45 with self-reported sleep dissatisfaction and randomized them to 2,000 mg/day of magnesium L-threonate (Magtein®) in capsule form or placebo for six weeks. Primary outcomes included the NIH Toolbox Cognition Battery, a function-based cognitive age estimate, and objective/subjective sleep measures. This was not an older-adult-only study — the relatively young cohort strengthens the interpretation that the cognitive improvements are not simply correcting severe baseline deficiency but represent a genuine performance enhancement.

Cognitive results: the magnesium L-threonate group showed a 7.5-year improvement in function-based cognitive age estimates versus placebo. On the NIH Toolbox Cognition Battery, the treatment group showed significantly greater improvements in overall total cognition composite score (p = 0.043), with the largest individual effects concentrated in working memory and episodic memory. Reaction time also improved. Secondary cognitive measures (processing speed, attention) trended positive but did not reach significance.

Sleep results: the treatment group showed improvements in subjective sleep quality scores (p < 0.05) but not objective polysomnographic sleep measures, suggesting the sleep benefit is experienced qualitatively but does not produce detectable changes in sleep architecture at six weeks. Heart rate variability (HRV) improved in the treatment group, consistent with magnesium’s role in autonomic nervous system regulation.

The mechanistic basis: magnesium L-threonate crosses the blood-brain barrier more effectively than standard magnesium forms (glycinate, citrate, oxide), raising brain tissue magnesium concentrations rather than just serum magnesium. Brain magnesium is a critical co-factor in NMDA receptor function — the receptor class central to synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory consolidation. Low brain magnesium impairs long-term potentiation (LTP) — the synaptic strengthening mechanism that underlies memory formation — which is why correcting brain magnesium specifically, rather than serum magnesium, produces cognitive effects that standard supplementation does not reliably achieve. Standard dosing: 2 g/day in divided doses; onset of cognitive effect appears at 4–6 weeks. One caveat: this study was partly funded by Magtein®/AIDP, the ingredient manufacturer, which is relevant context for interpreting the effect sizes. The trial design (double-blind, placebo-controlled, registered) reduces but does not eliminate funding-bias risk.

Source: Frontiers in Nutrition · DOI: 10.3389/fnut.2025.1729164 · Lopresti & Smith · January 2026 · 100 adults aged 18–45 · 6-week double-blind RCT
Magnesium Depletion Score (MDS) — A Clinical Risk Tool Linking Five Depletion Factors to CVD, T2D, Parkinson’s, and Mortality Across 48 Studies
[Review]

A scoping review published in Nutrients (PMC12566843, 2025) systematically assessed the utility of the Magnesium Depletion Score (MDS) as a clinical predictor of magnesium deficiency risk and associated health outcomes. The MDS is a composite risk tool scored from 0–6 based on five depletion factors: diuretic medication use (+1), proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use (+1), heavy alcohol consumption (>1–2 drinks/day by sex, +1), mildly reduced kidney function (eGFR 60–89 mL/min/1.73m², +1), and chronic kidney disease (eGFR <60, +2). An MDS >2 identifies individuals at high deficiency risk and is inversely correlated with dietary magnesium intake — those with higher pharmacological/lifestyle depletion loads require substantially more dietary magnesium to maintain adequate serum and tissue concentrations.

Across 48 studies included in the review, higher MDS was consistently associated with significantly increased risk for: cardiovascular disease, hypertension, stroke, and congestive heart failure; type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome; Parkinson’s disease; osteoporosis and bone fracture; sleep disorders, depression, and gout. The most consistently replicated endpoint across studies was all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, with dose-dependent associations between MDS and mortality risk. The review notes that 48–68% of U.S. adults fail to meet adequate magnesium intake from diet alone — and this failure is compounded substantially for the significant portion of the population on diuretics or PPIs (both among the most prescribed drug classes globally).

The actionable implication of the MDS framework: magnesium deficiency is not primarily a dietary problem in isolation — it is a drug-diet-lifestyle interaction. Someone on omeprazole (PPI) and a diuretic who drinks daily and has early kidney decline has an MDS of 4–5 and is almost certainly severely magnesium-depleted regardless of dietary intake. For this population, supplementation at 200–400 mg/day of an absorbable form (glycinate, malate, L-threonate) is not optional. Even without pharmacological depletion: the Western dietary pattern, which is calorie-sufficient but magnesium-poor (ultra-processed foods contain virtually no magnesium; refining removes 80–95% of magnesium from grains), means baseline population magnesium status is substantially suboptimal even in healthy adults.

Source: Nutrients · PMC12566843 · Magnesium Depletion Score scoping review · 48 studies · 2025
Supplement Protocol
  • Magnesium L-threonate (Magtein®) 2 g/day for cognitive benefits — specifically targets brain tissue magnesium via blood-brain-barrier crossing. Take in divided doses, 6-week onset for cognitive effects
  • Magnesium glycinate 200–400 mg/day as a cost-effective general magnesium repletion form (high absorbability, low GI side effects) — suitable for addressing MDS-driven deficiency and sleep/anxiety symptoms
  • Calculate your MDS: if you score >2 (PPI + diuretic, or PPI + regular alcohol, etc.), supplementation is essential rather than optional — dietary correction alone is insufficient under high depletion conditions
Caveats
  • The Magtein RCT has partial industry funding — the double-blind design is solid but effect sizes may be modestly overstated; the cognitive benefit direction is likely real, magnitude less certain
  • High-dose magnesium supplementation causes GI side effects (loose stools, diarrhoea) at doses >500 mg/day elemental magnesium — titrate up slowly and stay within tolerable doses
  • Magnesium interacts with some antibiotics (quinolones, tetracyclines) and bisphosphonates — space doses 2+ hours from these medications if applicable
⚠ Hype Watch

Taurine as an anti-aging supplement: A landmark 2023 paper in Science (Singh et al.) showed that taurine declines with age and that supplementation extended lifespan and healthspan in mice and worms, triggering enormous commercial interest and widespread supplementation. A 2025 study published in Science (with NIH involvement) then found that in multiple human cohort analyses, taurine levels did not reliably decline with age — and in some older populations, taurine levels actually increased. The NIH concluded that taurine is unlikely to be a reliable aging biomarker. This does not mean taurine is harmful — the energy drink and endurance community data on moderate doses is generally benign. But the anti-aging rationale (supplement taurine to restore declining levels and thereby slow aging) rests on a premise that human data does not support. The animal findings remain interesting mechanistically; the human translation is not validated. If you’re taking taurine specifically for longevity, the evidence base has materially weakened. The ongoing clinical trial (NCT06607068) assessing taurine in older women may provide more clarity.

⚡ Implication: Magnesium is the most under-optimized mineral in the Western population. Calculate your MDS — if you score >2, correct it actively via supplementation. For cognitive targeting specifically, magnesium L-threonate’s blood-brain-barrier penetration justifies the cost premium. For general magnesium repletion, glycinate is the cost-effective default. Taurine anti-aging supplementation lacks a validated human rationale — deprioritize until clinical trial data arrives.

Watchlist

New
Tristetraprolin (TTP) Drug Discovery — University at Buffalo Anti-Inflammaging Drug Screening Program
The University at Buffalo team (Sullivan lab) has confirmed the TTP/inflammaging mechanistic story in elderly mice and is now in active early-stage drug screening for small molecules that can reliably upregulate TTP expression. No compound has yet reached preclinical development — current work is in early biochemical screening mode. The drug-discovery challenge is that TTP is a post-transcriptional regulator (not a simple enzyme or receptor), making traditional small-molecule activation difficult. Watching for: identification of a lead candidate TTP-upregulating compound and first in-vivo efficacy data in aged rodents. Timeline: likely 3–5 years to IND-enabling studies if a candidate emerges.
2
Carried Forward
POLYCAD Trial — Spermidine in Coronary Artery Disease, Cardiovascular Endpoint Data
The POLYCAD trial (spermidine supplementation vs. placebo in CAD patients) completed recruitment in August 2025 and expects final patient visits in August 2026. Primary endpoints: cardiac remodeling, exercise capacity, muscle mass, systemic inflammation markers. This will be the first adequately powered cardiovascular endpoint trial for polyamine supplementation. The result will either validate the spermidine longevity biology in humans at a clinically meaningful scale or reveal that animal model findings don’t translate. Watching for: top-line cardiac remodeling and inflammatory marker data, expected late 2026.
3
Carried Forward
COSMOS Multivitamin — Long-Term Disease Outcome Follow-Up
The COSMOS trial showed approximately four months of slowed epigenetic aging over two years with daily multivitamin supplementation (Nature Medicine 2026). The outstanding question is whether this translates to reduced incidence of age-related disease (cardiovascular, cancer, cognitive decline) over longer follow-up, and whether the benefit requires continuous supplementation or persists after cessation. The COSMOS-Mind cognitive substudy is ongoing. Watching for: disease incidence endpoints from extended follow-up and post-trial cessation data.
4
Carried Forward
HMW-HA / Naked Mole Rat Longevity — Pre-Clinical Human Translation
University of Rochester researchers (Gorbunova/Seluanov lab) transferred the Has2 gene from naked mole rats into mice, producing 4.4% lifespan extension, reduced cancer rates, lower multi-tissue inflammation, and improved gut health (Nature, 2023). The team is now testing molecules that slow hyaluronan degradation in pre-clinical trials. Watching for: IND filing for first human trial of an HMW-HA modulator; Phase 1 safety data on hyaluronan-preserving compounds.
5
Carried Forward
Rapamycin / PEARL Trial — Phase 2 Efficacy Data
The PEARL trial (rapamycin for longevity in healthy adults, Phase 2) results remain in the pipeline. No new data this cycle. mTOR inhibition remains the most clinically advanced pharmacological longevity approach, but the human efficacy question in healthy (non-diseased) populations is still open. Watching for: top-line biomarker outcome data, particularly on immune function and metabolic markers in the healthy adult population.
