Time Crystals: The Phase of Matter That Repeats Forever Without Burning Energy

A normal crystal repeats in space — atoms arranged in a pattern that tiles out in three dimensions. In 2012, Nobel laureate Frank Wilczek asked a question physicists found almost offensive: what if a structure could repeat in time instead? What if matter could tick — oscillating between states indefinitely, without any energy input, without ever running down? It was declared mathematically impossible in 2014. Then the rules were changed just enough to make it real. Google built one inside a quantum computer. A team in Colorado made the first one you can see with the naked eye. And a May 2026 breakthrough shows you can engineer exotic quantum states — including time-crystal behaviour — by simply changing how a material is driven through time.

In 2012, Nobel laureate Frank Wilczek proposed a phase of matter that oscillates between states forever without consuming energy — like a clock that never needs winding. TL;DR

A crystal is defined by broken symmetry: its atoms don't fill space uniformly, they repeat in a pattern — the same arrangement, tiling out in every direction. In 2012, Frank Wilczek asked whether symmetry could be broken in time rather than space: could matter settle into a state that repeats at regular intervals, forever, without any energy input to sustain it? The answer, initially, was no. Not just practically impossible, but mathematically forbidden. Then physicists found a loophole. If you drive the system periodically — supply a rhythmic push from the outside — a system can lock into a rhythm that is not the rhythm of the driving force, repeating at a different rate indefinitely. This is a discrete time crystal. It is not a perpetual motion machine, because you do supply energy. But the behaviour it exhibits — coherent oscillation that does not decay, that resists disruption, that maintains its phase against noise — has no counterpart in ordinary physics. And as of 2026, it has moved from mathematical curiosity to something you can build, see, and potentially put inside a quantum computer.

Section 01

The Story

The concept of a crystal begins with symmetry breaking. In liquid water, molecules are arranged randomly — the substance looks the same in every direction. When water freezes, its molecules lock into a lattice: a pattern that breaks the continuous rotational symmetry of liquid and replaces it with the discrete symmetry of ice. The crystal does not fill space uniformly; it tiles it. What makes it a crystal is precisely that it breaks space's translational symmetry, preferring specific positions over the continuous blur of the liquid.

In 2012, while teaching a class about ordinary crystals, Nobel laureate Frank Wilczek began wondering about time. Space and time are treated symmetrically in the equations of physics: if a system can break spatial symmetry by forming a repeating pattern in three dimensions, Wilczek asked, why can't a system break time-translation symmetry by forming a repeating pattern in time? Could matter settle into a lowest-energy state that oscillates rhythmically — returning to its initial configuration at regular intervals, ticking like a clock, indefinitely and without any energy supply?

The original proposal was almost offensive to physicists. A system in its lowest-energy ground state that spontaneously moves — forever, for free — sounds exactly like perpetual motion.

Wilczek published his proposal in Physical Review Letters in 2012. The physics community reacted with a mixture of fascination and scepticism. Two years later, in 2014, physicists Haruki Watanabe and Masaki Oshikawa published a proof: Wilczek's original formulation was impossible. A system in true thermodynamic equilibrium — in its absolute ground state — cannot break time-translation symmetry. The mathematics were clear. Wilczek's time crystal, as originally conceived, could not exist.

What happened next was characteristic of how physics actually progresses: rather than abandoning the concept, researchers found a loophole. If a system is not in equilibrium — if it is being periodically driven by an external force — the proof does not apply. Physicist Shivaji Sondhi and collaborators showed in 2016 that a periodically kicked quantum system can spontaneously lock into an oscillation at a fraction of the driving frequency, and maintain that oscillation indefinitely without decaying. This is the discrete time crystal: not a system that oscillates for free, but one that oscillates in a way that is not directly imposed by the driver — that chooses its own rhythm, and holds it.

The same year, two experimental groups — one at Harvard using nitrogen-vacancy centres in diamond, one at the University of Maryland using trapped ytterbium ions — both claimed to have observed discrete time crystals in the laboratory. The field had moved from mathematical proof-of-impossibility to experimental demonstration in under two years.

Section 02

The Mechanism

To understand why a driven quantum system can behave in ways a static one cannot, it helps to think about what "driving" means quantum mechanically. In ordinary (static) physics, a system settles into its lowest energy state and stays there. Any oscillation in the ground state would mean the system continuously moves between two configurations, which would require energy — and a truly lowest-energy system has none to give. This is why Wilczek's original proposal failed.

But a periodically driven system — one that receives a regular external push, like light oscillating at a set frequency, or a magnetic field that flips on a timer — is not in equilibrium. It lives in what physicists call a Floquet state: a quantum state defined by a periodic drive rather than a static Hamiltonian. Floquet systems obey different rules. Their energy levels form bands that depend on the drive frequency, not just the material's intrinsic structure. And crucially, these bands can contain phases of matter with no static counterpart.

The Floquet Loophole Explained

Think of a child on a playground swing. At rest, the swing hangs straight down — its ground state. You can't make it oscillate without supplying energy. But if you push it rhythmically at the right frequency, it swings higher and higher — and a sufficiently clever quantum system can lock into an oscillation at half the frequency of your pushing, even though you never pushed at that frequency. The system is choosing its own rhythm from within the energy you supply. That subharmonic response — the spontaneous halving (or quartering, or thirding) of the drive frequency — is the signature of a discrete time crystal. Critically, the oscillation is rigid: perturb the system slightly and it snaps back to its locked frequency, rather than drifting. This robustness is what makes it physically interesting.

The Floquet framework applies not just to time crystals but to a whole zoo of exotic quantum phases. By carefully timing an external drive — a laser pulse, a magnetic field oscillation, a sequence of microwave pulses — physicists can engineer quantum states that the material's equilibrium chemistry would never produce. This is Floquet engineering, and a May 2026 paper from Cal Poly researchers Ian Powell and Louis Buchalter showed that something as simple as a periodically flipping magnetic field can generate quantum phases with topological properties — states where the quantum information is protected against local disruption because it is encoded in global, geometric features of the wavefunction rather than in any individual particle.

The key insight from Powell and Buchalter's "Flux-Switching Floquet Engineering" paper, published in Physical Review B, is that useful quantum properties can depend not just on what a material is made of, but on how it is manipulated in time. A material that is a mundane insulator under static conditions can become a topological superconductor under the right periodic drive. The possibility space for quantum matter is dramatically larger than what equilibrium chemistry can access — and time is the key that unlocks it.

Section 03

The Connections

Google's quantum computer time crystal (2021). In November 2021, a collaboration between Stanford University, Google Quantum AI, the Max Planck Institute for Physics of Complex Systems, and Oxford University created a discrete time crystal inside Google's Sycamore quantum processor. Using 20 of the processor's 53 qubits, the team demonstrated that the system oscillated at exactly half the drive frequency, and maintained this oscillation for the duration of the experiment without decay. This was significant not just as proof-of-concept but because it demonstrated time-crystalline behaviour in a programmable, controllable quantum system. The processor was not being used to compute anything — it was being used as a laboratory for exotic quantum matter, a role that quantum computers are increasingly being designed to play.

The first time crystal you can actually see (2025). In September 2025, physicists at the University of Colorado Boulder created a time crystal from liquid crystals — the same class of materials used in phone and laptop displays. After a single laser pulse, the material spontaneously formed repeating patterns that researchers described as "psychedelic tiger stripes": oscillating between two distinct configurations at a regular period, without any further energy input, for hours. This was the first time crystal observable by human eye under a microscope, and it used materials sufficiently ordinary that the discovery suggested time-crystalline behaviour might be far more common than the field had assumed. The implications for sensing technology — where a material that maintains phase coherence for hours after a single pulse could detect extraordinarily small perturbations — are significant.

Why This Is Not a Perpetual Motion Machine

It's the question everyone asks. The answer is that a discrete time crystal requires an ongoing periodic drive to maintain its non-equilibrium state — it is not self-sustaining in the way Wilczek's original proposal imagined. What is extraordinary is not that it runs forever without energy (it doesn't), but that its response to that energy is anomalously coherent and rigid. Most driven systems eventually heat up, become disordered, and lose their structure. A time crystal resists this. The energy flowing through it organises it rather than randomising it. That resistance to thermal decay, sustained over long timescales, is the physically remarkable property — and the one with practical applications.

Linked time crystals and quantum networking (2025). In October 2025, a team at Aalto University in Finland achieved something the field had been working toward: coupling two time crystals together while maintaining their perpetual oscillations. Published in Nature Communications, the experiment demonstrated that two separate time-crystalline systems — each oscillating independently — could be entangled such that a measurement on one would instantaneously affect the other. This is a necessary step toward any time-crystal-based quantum memory or quantum communication device, because isolated quantum systems are useless unless they can interact with each other in controlled ways.

What Floquet engineering means for quantum hardware. The practical implication of Floquet engineering, and of the 2026 Cal Poly paper specifically, is that quantum hardware designers may not need to synthesise entirely new materials to access exotic quantum phases — they may simply need to drive existing materials in the right way. A material that is cheap, stable, and easy to fabricate at room temperature might access the same topological quantum states as a rare, exotic compound, if you pulse it correctly. This dramatically changes the economics of quantum materials research and potentially of quantum computing hardware.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

⌚
The Original Proof of Impossibility
Watanabe and Oshikawa's 2014 proof that no equilibrium system can break continuous time-translation symmetry was mathematically airtight. What makes the subsequent history interesting is that their proof did not kill the concept — it precisely defined the loophole. By proving what was impossible in equilibrium, they implicitly defined what might be possible out of equilibrium. The Floquet time crystal concept emerged directly from trying to find what their proof did not forbid.
🔬
Many-Body Localisation: The Enabler
Time crystals in driven systems rely on a phenomenon called many-body localisation (MBL): in strongly disordered quantum systems, energy does not spread and thermalise the way it normally does. Without MBL, a periodically driven system would simply heat up and eventually randomise. With MBL, the system can maintain coherent oscillations indefinitely. MBL itself is a major open problem in condensed matter physics — it was only rigorously established theoretically in 2006, and its full conditions are still being worked out experimentally.
🧪
The Spruce Pine Parallel
Exotic quantum matter research faces the same supply-chain problem as semiconductor manufacturing: it often requires materials of extraordinary purity or specificity. Nitrogen-vacancy centres in diamond, ytterbium ions in traps, and superconducting qubits each require different extraordinary inputs. The Cal Poly Floquet engineering result — that you can generate exotic phases in ordinary materials just by driving them correctly — is potentially a solution to this problem analogous to finding that ordinary sand could be polished into chip-grade silicon by the right process.
📡
Time Crystals as Sensors
The phase rigidity of a time crystal — its resistance to perturbation — makes it a natural candidate for precision sensing. A system that locks into a stable oscillation and holds it against noise can detect tiny deviations in magnetic fields, gravitational fields, or electric fields, because even microscopic perturbations shift the phase of the oscillation. Proposals for time-crystal-based magnetometers, accelerometers, and gravitational wave detectors have been published, though none have yet reached practical deployment. The CU Boulder visible time crystal's hours-long coherence time makes these applications significantly more plausible.
🧠
Quantum Memory That Doesn't Forget
The central problem in quantum computing is decoherence: quantum states collapse when they interact with their environment, losing their information. Time-crystalline states are anomalously resistant to this collapse. A time crystal "remembers" its phase — the starting configuration — because the whole system locks into that phase coherently. This is why researchers have proposed using time-crystalline phases as quantum memory: not for computational purposes, but as storage that maintains quantum states far longer than conventional qubit approaches. The Aalto University entanglement experiment in 2025 is the first demonstration that such a memory could also be read out.
