Alpha — Orderbook Depth & Spread

Friday · 8 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #15 11-min read
TL;DR

Orderbook depth and the bid-ask spread are the two static measurements of a market's capacity to absorb size — depth is how much resting limit-order volume sits at each price level, spread is the cost of crossing from buyer to seller right now. Every dynamic concept on this curriculum — sweeps, absorption, spoofing, liquidity voids — is something happening to the depth map. Learn to read the static map first; the dynamic events become readable as deviations from it. The practical payoff: when spread widens and top-of-book depth thins simultaneously, the market has lost confidence in the current price and is repricing risk in real time.

§01
What It Is
The static map · resting size at each level + the cost of crossing
Depth is the cumulative resting limit-order volume at each price level on both sides of the book; spread is the gap between the best bid and the best ask.

Every centralised exchange runs a limit order book (LOB) — a price-time-priority queue of unmatched limit orders waiting to be filled. The book has two sides: bids (buyers willing to wait at or below a price) and asks (sellers willing to wait at or above a price). The highest bid and lowest ask define the inside market; everything else is queued behind them at progressively worse prices. The vertical stack of resting size at each price level is the depth. The horizontal distance between best bid and best ask is the spread.

These are the two atomic primitives of microstructure. Larry Harris, in the canonical reference Trading and Exchanges, defines them as orthogonal axes of liquidity: spread measures tightness (how cheap it is to round-trip a small order), depth measures resilience (how much you can transact before the price moves against you). A market can be tight but shallow (penny spread, but only 1 BTC available at top of book) or wide but deep (10-tick spread, but every level holds 50 BTC). Both measurements are needed to characterise a market — neither is sufficient alone.

Crypto traders typically encounter depth in three forms: the raw DOM (depth of market) ladder showing levels-x-size, the depth chart (cumulative bid/ask volume plotted as a curve away from mid-price), and the liquidity heatmap (a time-x-price grid that animates depth changes — Bookmap, Coinglass, Hyblock). Each is the same data viewed differently. The DOM is the literal queue; the depth chart is a snapshot of cumulative liquidity at any distance from mid; the heatmap is the time series.

The reason this matters for everything else on the curriculum: order flow only has meaning relative to the depth it consumes. A 100 BTC market buy means very different things if it eats 0.5% of the visible asks (routine) versus 80% of them (sweep). A widening spread on flat depth means makers are stepping back. Both are reads off the book. Learn the static map first; the dynamic events become readable as deviations from it.

§02
Mechanics
How matching engines walk the book · slippage math · why spread = adverse-selection rent
A market order walks the book until it's filled, paying progressively worse prices at each level — depth determines slippage; spread is what makers charge to compensate for the risk of trading against informed flow.

When an aggressor sends a market buy, the matching engine fills it against resting asks in price-time priority: cheapest ask first, then the next-cheapest, then the next, until the order's full size is consumed. Each level it eats is a price level higher than the last. The effective fill price is a size-weighted average of every level it touched.

Slippage on a market buy of size Q: Σ(qᵢ × pᵢ) for i = 1..n where Σqᵢ = Q Effective fill = Σ(qᵢ × pᵢ) / Q Slippage = Effective fill − Best ask at submit Top-of-book depth imbalance: DI = (Bid₁ size − Ask₁ size) / (Bid₁ size + Ask₁ size) DI > 0 → bid-heavy DI < 0 → ask-heavy DI ∈ [−1, +1] Cumulative depth ratio at ±X bps from mid: CDR(X) = Σ bid size within X bps / Σ ask size within X bps CDR > 1 → buy-side dominant within that band CDR < 1 → sell-side dominant Effective spread (Roll, 1984 — practitioner version): Spread_eff = 2 × |Trade_price − Mid_at_trade|
Note: most exchange-published spreads are quoted spreads (Ask₁ − Bid₁). The realised spread that traders actually pay can differ — large orders eat through multiple levels (slippage component); aggressive flow against fast-moving makers can fill through the quoted spread (price-improvement, rare in crypto). Use Roll's measure for what you actually paid; use the quoted measure for what's posted.

Spread is not arbitrary. It's the rent market makers charge to compensate for two costs they can't avoid: inventory risk (holding the asset while it moves against them) and adverse selection (the certainty that some of their counterparties know more than they do). When uncertainty rises — major data print, geopolitical shock, exchange outage — both costs rise, and makers widen the spread to stay solvent. As the academic microstructure literature puts it: "a market maker who fails to widen their spread during high-volatility regimes essentially subsidises informed traders." Spread widening is therefore a leading-indicator stress signal — it's the first thing to move when makers lose confidence in fair value.

Crucially, depth-of-book is an unreliable measure of intent. Resting orders can be cancelled instantly. The CME's own market-quality research ("Reassessing Liquidity: Beyond Order Book Depth", 2025) concedes that visible depth overstates true willingness to transact — what matters more is depth that holds as price approaches it. Bookmap calls this distinction "real liquidity vs fake liquidity": real liquidity remains in the book and executes; fake liquidity (spoofing, layering, optical-illusion size) is pulled before fill. The depth you trust is the depth that has survived previous tests at the same level.

§04
In Practice
The four heuristics · sizing to the book · worked example
The one rule: never size a position to the visible depth — size it to the depth that has held through previous tests at the level.
1

Read Spread First, Depth Second

Spread is the leading indicator; depth is the lagging confirmation. Makers widen spread before they pull depth. If the BTC/USDT.P spread on Binance widens from $5 to $40 ahead of a CPI print, makers are stepping back — your depth read from 30 seconds ago is already stale. Always check the spread first; if it's compressed, the depth chart is meaningful. If it's blown out, the depth chart is decoration.

2

Trust Depth That Has Survived a Test

The 28 BTC wall at $82,100 means nothing on its first appearance — it could be a 30-second spoof. But if price taps $82,100 three times over an hour and the wall absorbs the prints without flinching, that depth has earned its weight. Bookmap's heuristic is the cleanest: "Real liquidity remains in the order book and executes; fake liquidity appears temporarily and is removed before execution." Track the wall's behaviour through tests, not its raw size.

3

Size Trades to the 1% Depth Bracket, Not the Inside

The number worth memorising for any market you trade: how much resting liquidity sits within ±1% of mid? On BTC/USDT.P that's typically 200–400 BTC across all venues. Your sizing should not exceed 5–10% of that bracket on a single market order — beyond that you're paying meaningful slippage and signalling your size to algos that detect order-book impact. For larger size, work the order via TWAP or iceberg, not single market sweeps.

4

Watch the Depth Imbalance Around Key Levels

At a structural level (prior swing high, VWAP, 200-day MA), the depth-imbalance ratio (DI) is the cleanest single read. DI strongly positive into a level = bid-stacking, level expected to hold or be reclaimed. DI strongly negative into a level = ask-stacking, breakout into resistance is being faded. DI flipping sign during the test = makers changing their mind in real time — that's the inflection.

§07
Common Traps
Five ways traders misread depth and spread
The single biggest mistake: trusting visible depth as a measure of real intent — most of what's visible is conditional, and most of what matters is hidden.
1
Treating Visible Depth as Committed Capital

Resting limit orders can be cancelled instantly. A 50 BTC bid wall at $80,000 is meaningful only if it survives a test — until then it's an option that costs the maker nothing to walk away from. The CME's own market-quality research (2025) concedes that visible depth systematically overstates true willingness to transact. Track wall behaviour through tests, not raw size.

2
Ignoring Iceberg and Hidden Orders

Sophisticated participants on BTC/ETH execute via iceberg orders (only a fraction of true size visible) and hidden orders (zero visible). What you see in the DOM is a lower bound, not the truth. If an apparent 5 BTC ask absorbs 30 BTC of buying without printing the next price level, you just hit an iceberg — the real depth was 6× what the screen showed. Bookmap's iceberg-detector is the production tool; the heuristic without it is "size that absorbs more than it shows is hidden size."

3
Trading the Quoted Spread When the Realised Spread Is Wider

The exchange-published spread (Ask₁ − Bid₁) is the cost of a 1-tick round-trip in zero size. The realised spread on a 20 BTC market order can be 5×–20× wider once you walk through multiple ask levels. If you size off the quoted spread and execute against a thin book, slippage will eat your edge before you've even taken risk. Always estimate effective fill price using cumulative depth — never the inside.

4
Mistaking Depth Imbalance for a Directional Signal

A strongly positive depth imbalance (lots of bid size vs ask size) does not mean price will rise — it often means the book is set up for a sweep through that imbalance. Algorithmic flow specifically targets visible imbalance because resting size is a known source of fills. Use DI as a context input alongside aggressive flow direction (CVD, tape pace), not as a standalone bias indicator.

5
Thinking Depth-Reading Alone Is Edge

Honest read: depth and spread are the foundational language of microstructure, but in 2026 they are widely watched. Every serious crypto desk reads Coinglass, Bookmap, or Hyblock; HFT firms see far more granular versions in nanoseconds. Trading off raw depth signals as a retail discretionary trader, against algos with sub-millisecond cancellation latency, is not a fair fight. Use depth reading as execution intelligence (where to place orders, how to size, when to step aside) and as confluence with structure and flow — not as a standalone alpha generator.
