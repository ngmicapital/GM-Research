Alpha — Large Prints & Block Trades

Friday · 8 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #15 11-min read
TL;DR

Large prints and block trades are the visible footprints of size — single executions big enough that they could only have been placed by an institution, a market maker, or a fund unwilling to break the order down further. The edge isn't seeing the print itself; everyone sees it. The edge is reading where on the tape it landed, how the book responded, and whether price kept moving against it or stalled — that distinguishes a real positioning move from a hedge, a sweep from a settlement, and a tell from a trap.

§01
What It Is
Size on the tape · screen prints vs negotiated blocks
A large print is any single executed trade big enough to stand out from the surrounding tape; a block trade is a pre-negotiated print routed off-book and reported back to the exchange after the fact.

Two related but distinct things are usually lumped together. Large prints are individual executions visible on the public tape — a single 200 BTC market buy clearing the ask, a 5,000 ETH sell that walks the bid down 80 ticks. They happened on-screen, in the order book, and you can replay them tick-by-tick. Block trades are different: they are negotiated privately between two parties (or through an RFQ system like Paradigm or Deribit's Block RFQ), executed away from the lit book, and printed publicly only after the fact. The tape sees the size; it never saw the orderbook activity that would have moved price.

Both matter for the same reason: they are the visible signature of someone with size who could not, or chose not to, hide. A real $20M institutional order rarely walks across the screen as one print — it gets sliced into iceberg fragments, TWAP'd over an hour, or routed off-book through a market maker. So when you do see size hit the tape, it usually means one of three things: someone wanted urgency over stealth (a hedge fund cutting risk, a CEX user liquidating), someone wanted to signal presence (a mark-up move designed to be seen), or someone routed through a venue that auto-prints the entire fill (futures sweeps on Binance, CME closing prints).

On Deribit, the formal definition is precise: a block trade is "a trade agreed privately between two parties" with a minimum size of 25 BTC or equivalent in ETH per the exchange's own block-trading rules, "to safeguard order book liquidity." On CME, BTC futures blocks have a 5-contract minimum (25 BTC). Off-exchange, in spot, there is no formal threshold — "large" is whatever stands out from the local tape's average trade size.

§02
Mechanics
Detection thresholds · screen vs block reporting · aggressor classification
A trade qualifies as a "large print" when its size exceeds a rolling z-score threshold of recent tape; a block trade is identified by a venue-specific block flag in the exchange's trade record.

There is no universal numeric threshold. Practitioners use a rolling-window approach: pull the last N trades on the venue, compute mean and standard deviation of trade size, and flag anything over 3σ (or whatever cutoff the desk's risk model prefers) as a large print. The threshold floats with regime — in 2024 a "large" BTC print on Binance spot was ~50 BTC; on a thin Sunday tape today, 20 BTC stands out. Adapting to local volatility matters more than the absolute number.

Large-print z-score test: z = (size − μ_window) / σ_window where window = last N trades flag if z > 3 → LARGE_PRINT Aggressor classification (per trade): if exec_price ≥ best_ask: side = BUY (taker bought, hit ask) if exec_price ≤ best_bid: side = SELL (taker sold, hit bid) if bid < exec_price < ask: side = NEUTRAL (mid-fill, often block) Block-trade flag (Deribit / CME): trade.liquidity_type == "BLOCK" OR trade.size ≥ exchange_block_minimum AND reported_after_negotiation
Note: most retail dashboards (Coinglass "Large Order", Whale Alert) use fixed dollar thresholds (>$1M) rather than rolling-z. That works for headlines but misses the structural prints — a 40 BTC sell into a thin Sunday book is more informative than a $5M Tuesday sweep. Build your own filter if you can pull the trade stream.

The screen-print vs block-print distinction is structurally important. Screen prints reveal aggression: someone crossed the spread and accepted slippage, which usually signals urgency or conviction. Block prints reveal positioning: the size was already agreed at a fixed price between two counterparties, the exchange just publishes the receipt. A 500 BTC block at $80,200 tells you positioning shifted; it tells you nothing about whether the buyer or seller was the one driving terms.

For derivatives, the decorated trades approach (Amberdata, Velo, Greeks.live) is the analytical upgrade: every block print is annotated with the orderbook snapshot just before and just after, plus implied vol if it's an option. That tells you which direction the maker had to hedge into the screen — i.e., who actually moved the underlying. Without that decoration, a raw block print is just a number.

§04
In Practice
Confluence: print + reaction + level · screen vs block reads
The print itself is noise; the reaction to the print is the signal — watch what the next 20–60 seconds of book and tape look like, not the print number itself.

The single most useful question to ask after a large print isn't "what does this mean?" — it's "did price go in the direction of the print, or against it?" A 50 BTC market buy that pushes price up 30 ticks and the bid stays bid is a tell: the buyer was strong enough to move the level and the floor held. A 50 BTC market buy that pushes price up 30 ticks and the bid immediately gets refreshed cheaper while sellers reload is a trap: the buyer was the dumbest money in the room and the smarter side was waiting to fade them.

1

Read the reaction, not the print

Time the next 60 seconds. Did the level the print cleared act as new support? Did the book reload behind the move or into it? The print is the question; the next minute is the answer.

2

Cluster prints, then act

One large print is noise. Three large prints in the same direction within a 5-minute window, into the same level, with CVD confirming, is positioning. Wait for the cluster.

3

Distinguish blocks from sweeps explicitly

A block print at mid-price is positioning, often hedged. A screen sweep through five levels is urgency. Same notional, completely different signal — never treat them as the same datum.

4

Pair with structure, not just size

A 100 BTC sweep into the middle of a range is forgettable. The same sweep through the upper edge of a 3-day accumulation, into the daily VWAP, with funding still negative — that's a tradable confluence.

Worked example — sweep + level + reaction
Setup:BTC consolidating $79.6K–$80.1K for 36h. Spot CVD flat, perp CVD modestly positive, funding 0.002%.

§07
Common Traps
Why "follow the whales" fails more often than it works
The single biggest mistake is treating a print as direction. The print is a fact; the direction is an inference, and the inference is wrong more than half the time without the book context.
1
Treating "Whale Alert" notifications as actionable

Whale Alert flags exchange transfers, not market trades. A 1,000 BTC inflow to Binance can be a sell signal, a hot-wallet rebalance, or a new prime-broker custody arrangement. Without the on-tape execution that follows, it's a curiosity, not a trade.

2
Reading a block print as directional

Block trades are negotiated. The "side" you see in the dashboard is often the maker's reporting convention, not the aggressor. A 500 BTC block at mid-price tells you positioning shifted; it does not tell you who was forcing terms. Treat blocks as exposure data, not signal.

3
Front-running a single print

A lone large print is the noisiest data point on your screen — it could be a hedge, a settlement, a forced cut, or a real position. Without confirmation (cluster, level, reaction), entering off one print is gambling. Wait for two or three.

4
Confusing volume with size

A candle with 8,000 BTC of volume made up of 4,000 small prints is structurally different from one with 8,000 BTC of volume composed of three 2,500 BTC blocks. Same number, completely different operators. Always check the trade-size distribution, not just the total.

5
"Smart money" theatre — overhyped callout

A whole content economy has been built on watching screen prints and labelling them "smart money," "institutional flow," or "whale buys." Most of it is reverse-engineered narrative. Desks running real institutional flow specifically engineer their executions to not stand out — slicing, iceberging, blocking. By construction, the prints you see on free dashboards are mostly the orders that weren't hidden, which is a biased sample. The signal exists; it's just not where the marketing points.
