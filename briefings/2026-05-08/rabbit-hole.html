The Earth Built a Nuclear Reactor — and Ran It for 150,000 Years

How a uranium deposit in Gabon spontaneously went critical 1.7 billion years ago, self-regulated like a modern plant, and held its waste for longer than life has existed on land

In 1972, French scientists found uranium ore with missing isotopes — evidence that 1.7 billion years ago, nature had assembled a working nuclear fission reactor complete with self-regulating safety systems. It pulsed on and off every three hours for roughly 150,000 years, and nobody knew it existed until the 20th century.

Section 01

The Story

In May 1972, a chemist at the Tricastin uranium enrichment plant in Pierrelatte, France, ran a routine quality control test on a shipment of uranium hexafluoride gas from a mine in Gabon, West Africa. The test measured isotope ratios — specifically, the proportion of uranium-235 to uranium-238. In all natural uranium, anywhere on Earth, this ratio is a constant: 0.7202 percent U-235. It had been measured in meteorites, in ancient geological samples, in ore from every uranium mine ever tested. The ratio does not vary. It is, by assumption, a law of nature.

The Gabonese sample came back at 0.60 percent. The Oklo mine's uranium was missing about a sixth of its expected U-235.

The French Atomic Energy Commission (CEA) initially suspected contamination, or instrument error, or fraud — perhaps someone had extracted the fissile U-235 for weapons purposes and was trying to smuggle depleted ore. But the depletion pattern was far too complex and localized to be the result of simple extraction. After months of analysis, on September 25, 1972, the CEA made an announcement that scientists found almost impossible to accept: the missing uranium-235 had been consumed by a sustained, self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction. Approximately 1.7 billion years ago, in what is now the Oklo region of Gabon, the Earth had spontaneously built and operated a nuclear fission reactor.

Subsequent investigation found not one reactor zone but at least sixteen, spread across the Oklo and nearby Bangombé sites. Each was a compact zone of uranium ore — some just a meter across, others several meters — where the conditions had aligned precisely enough to sustain criticality. The reactors had collectively consumed about five to six tonnes of uranium-235 and produced roughly two tonnes of plutonium, which has since decayed away. The power output was modest — probably never exceeding 100 kilowatts thermal, roughly the output of a small diesel generator — but the duration was staggering: the reactions continued, off and on, for somewhere between 100,000 and 300,000 years.

Every uranium mine ever tested produced the same isotope ratio. The Oklo sample was different — and the only explanation was that something had burned the fuel nearly two billion years before humans learned fire.

The Oklo discovery forced a complete rethinking of what "natural" and "artificial" mean in nuclear physics. Before 1972, nuclear fission reactors were considered an exclusively human invention. The 1942 Chicago Pile-1 — a squash-court-sized stack of uranium and graphite assembled under the University of Chicago bleachers — was understood as the first reactor in the history of the planet. The Oklo revelation pushed that date back by 1.7 billion years, and the architect was groundwater.

Section 02

The Mechanism

Understanding why Oklo happened requires understanding what makes a nuclear chain reaction possible — and why the conditions exist nowhere on Earth today.

Nuclear fission works by firing a neutron at a heavy nucleus like uranium-235, which splits and releases energy plus more neutrons, which in turn split more nuclei. To sustain a chain reaction, you need enough fissile material close enough together that the neutrons released keep hitting more uranium-235 before escaping the mass. You also need a "moderator" — something to slow the neutrons down to the right speed. Fast neutrons tend to bounce off U-235 without triggering fission; slow ("thermal") neutrons are far more likely to cause a split. This is why modern nuclear plants use water: the hydrogen in H₂O slows neutrons efficiently without absorbing too many of them.

The problem with building a natural reactor is that U-235 is rare. Today it constitutes only 0.72 percent of natural uranium. That is nowhere near enough to sustain a chain reaction in an unmoderated pile of rock. Modern nuclear plants require uranium enriched to 3 to 5 percent U-235 — artificially concentrated from natural levels. You cannot accidentally hit criticality with today's ore, no matter how much water you add.

The Timing Window

U-235 and U-238 both decay radioactively, but at very different rates. U-238's half-life is 4.5 billion years; U-235's is only 700 million years. This means U-235 was proportionally far more abundant in the past — making up about 25% of all uranium when Earth formed 4.6 billion years ago. By 1.7 billion years ago, it had decayed down to roughly 3.1% — just enough to support a natural chain reaction in a rich ore deposit with a water moderator. Today it has fallen to 0.72%, well below the critical threshold. No new natural reactors can form on Earth. The window closed, and it will never reopen.

At Oklo 1.7 billion years ago, the uranium was rich enough. But the mechanism that actually triggered and sustained the reaction was water — specifically, groundwater percolating through porous sandstone into the uranium ore body. The water slowed neutrons to fission-friendly speeds, and criticality was achieved. The reaction began generating heat. And then something elegant happened.

As the reaction heated up, the groundwater boiled. Steam expanded and eventually the water evaporated from the ore zone. Without the moderating water, the neutrons moved too fast to sustain fission. The reaction slowed and stopped. The rock cooled. Groundwater seeped back in. The reaction restarted. Researchers modeling the Oklo pulse cycle estimate that active fission periods lasted roughly 30 minutes, followed by dormant intervals of about two and a half hours as the system cooled and refilled. A full cycle took approximately three hours, sustained hundreds of thousands of times over the reactor's operational lifetime.

This is not an approximation of modern reactor safety design. It is the exact same principle. The "negative void coefficient of reactivity" — the property that makes a reactor self-limiting when its coolant boils away — is considered a key safety feature of light-water reactors. Engineers spent decades designing for it. The Oklo reactors had it by accident of geology, and it worked flawlessly for over 150,000 years.

Section 03

The Connections

The nuclear waste problem, solved by geology. One of the most intractable issues in nuclear energy policy is what to do with the radioactive byproducts of fission — highly toxic material that remains dangerous for tens of thousands of years. The Oklo site offers the longest-running experiment in nuclear waste containment ever observed: approximately two tonnes of fission products and transuranic elements were generated by the natural reactors, and despite the high temperatures, intense radiation, and circulating groundwater, the vast majority of these radioactive products stayed geologically confined for 1.7 billion years. Nuclear engineers and waste regulators have studied Oklo specifically for what it tells us about deep geological storage — the idea that burying waste in stable rock formations can isolate it safely. The stalled Yucca Mountain repository in Nevada, proposed to store U.S. civilian nuclear waste for 10,000 years, is built on the same logic that Oklo demonstrated empirically across 170 times that timescale. Nature already ran the long-term experiment; we just had to find where.

Isotope ratios as a universal clock. The Oklo discovery was made not by finding the reactor directly but by noticing a discrepancy in a number that was assumed to be constant. This reveals something profound about how isotope forensics works: the ratios of radioactive isotopes in any sample are a record of everything that has happened to that material. The specific pattern of depletion at Oklo — which isotopes were consumed, in what proportions, with what fission products remaining — could only have been produced by one thing: a sustained neutron chain reaction. No other process in nature consumes U-235 while leaving behind cesium-133, neodymium-142, and the particular suite of daughter products found at the site. The isotope pattern was a fingerprint that could only be read one way. This same logic underpins nuclear nonproliferation monitoring today: the isotopic signature of uranium from a clandestine enrichment program is detectable even in trace atmospheric samples, because the ratios cannot lie.

The startup that named itself after the reactor. In 2013, a company called Oklo Inc. was founded in California, directly invoking the Gabonese phenomenon. Oklo is developing compact fast fission reactors — so-called "microreactors" — designed for remote locations and defense applications. The reference is apt in more than one sense: like the geological original, Oklo's designed reactors are intended to be small, self-regulating, and capable of operating without active human management. There is an odd circularity to the story. A natural reactor ran itself autonomously for 150,000 years, was discovered by humans in 1972, was studied for half a century, and is now serving as inspiration for machines designed to replicate its autonomous operation. The Earth invented the technology. We are reverse-engineering it.

A hard limit on natural nuclear physics. One implication of the Oklo timing window — that 3% U-235 is the rough criticality threshold for an unmoderated ore deposit — is that the window is permanently closed for Earth. As U-235 continues to decay toward zero, no future geology can recreate the Oklo conditions, regardless of how the ore concentrates or how much water is present. The planet had a narrow era of about 500 million years during which self-sustaining natural fission was possible in principle, and Oklo is the only known place on Earth where it actually happened. If there are other natural reactors to be found, they will be on other planets with different geological histories — or they will be in the ancient rock record, waiting for a chemist to notice that a number which is supposed to be constant is not.

The Chicago Pile-1 (1942)
Enrico Fermi's reactor under the University of Chicago bleachers — the first "artificial" nuclear reactor, now understood to have been preceded by nature by 1.7 billion years. The control rods were literally wooden boards wrapped in cadmium.

Yucca Mountain
The proposed U.S. nuclear waste repository, stalled since 2010. Its design premise — geological isolation is sufficient for long-term containment — is exactly what Oklo proved, at a timescale 170,000 times longer than required.
⚛️
Oklo Inc.
The California startup building compact fast-spectrum microreactors, named after the Gabonese phenomenon. Listed on the NYSE in 2024; designed to operate autonomously without active human control — like the original.
🔬
Isotope Forensics
The field that identified Oklo by reading the "fingerprint" left in depleted uranium. The same technique is used today to detect clandestine nuclear enrichment programs anywhere on Earth — isotope ratios cannot be falsified.

The Great Oxidation Event
Around 2.4 billion years ago — 700 million years before Oklo — Earth's atmosphere first filled with oxygen. The Oklo reactors existed in a world with breathable air, but no complex life. The planet was already ancient when the chain reactions began.
