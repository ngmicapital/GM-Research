HKMA Grants World's First Stablecoin Issuer Licences to HSBC and Anchorpoint Financial — 6% Approval Rate From 36 Applications

"The granting of stablecoin issuer licences is an important milestone for the development of digital assets in Hong Kong." — Eddie Yue, Chief Executive, HKMA, 10 April 2026

On 10 April 2026, the Hong Kong Monetary Authority issued the world's first stablecoin issuer licences under the Stablecoins Ordinance — granting authorisation to The Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation Limited (HSBC) and Anchorpoint Financial Limited, a Standard Chartered–led consortium that includes Animoca Brands. Only two of 36 applications were approved — a 6% acceptance rate — signalling that the HKMA intends its regime to be a quality-controlled framework, not a registration gateway. Both licences are effective immediately.

HSBC intends to launch a Hong Kong dollar (HKD)–denominated stablecoin in the second half of 2026. Anchorpoint plans a B2B2C model through authorised distribution partners, with the product branded HKDAP (HKD At Par). The HKMA warned the public to verify licensees through its official Register of Licensed Stablecoin Issuers and to acquire stablecoins only through regulated channels.

📎 Historical Precedent: MAS (Singapore) was first to grant stablecoin authorisations under its Payment Services Act in 2024, but the Stablecoins Ordinance is the first standalone statutory stablecoin licensing regime in the world to generate issued licences — a structural distinction that matters for cross-border mutual recognition negotiations.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The 6% approval rate is a deliberate policy signal: both approved entities are regulated under existing statutory frameworks — HSBC under the Banking Ordinance, Anchorpoint through its Standard Chartered anchor — meaning the HKMA has effectively required a pre-existing supervisory relationship as an informal prerequisite. Fintech-native applicants without incumbent banking partners should model a multi-year compliance runway before reapplication. The HKD stablecoin's most immediate legal implication is its interaction with HKMA's RTGS linkage arrangements with the People's Bank of China: a SWIFT-equivalent HKD on-chain settlement instrument creates a new CNH settlement pathway that the PBoC will need to respond to.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The "world's first" framing overstates novelty — the US OCC has authorised bank-held stablecoin activities since 2020, and MiCA authorises e-money token issuance. What Hong Kong has actually done is create a dedicated stablecoin-issuer licence category rather than shoehorning stablecoins into banking or payment frameworks. Whether that structural distinction generates a competitive advantage over MiCA-authorised Euro stablecoin issuers remains unproven — Tether's dominance suggests regulatory prestige correlates poorly with adoption at retail scale.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Tether and Circle face no immediate competitive threat — HKD stablecoins serve a distinct settlement corridor. However, Animoca Brands' involvement in Anchorpoint is strategically significant: it gives a gaming/NFT-native firm a regulated stablecoin issuer anchor for in-game HKD settlement across Animoca's 400+ portfolio companies. The 34 rejected applicants — which likely include crypto-native fintechs and regional banks — face a binary choice: pursue a partnership with HSBC or Anchorpoint for distribution, or pivot to MAS or MiCA authorisation as an alternative path into APAC institutional markets.

📊 Portfolio Impact

Bullish [HKD stablecoin settlement infrastructure, Animoca-linked tokens]: Regulatory legitimacy for HKD on-chain settlement opens institutional tokenisation pipelines in APAC.

Bearish [Smaller crypto-native stablecoin applicants seeking Hong Kong access]: 6% approval rate effectively closes the market to non-incumbent applicants until the next application round.

Neutral [BTC, ETH]: No direct price impact; positive for broader institutional sentiment in APAC heading into H2 2026.

Japan's Cabinet Approves FIEA Amendment — 105 Cryptoassets Reclassified as Financial Instruments, Penalties Trebled, 20% Flat CGT Proposed

"The reforms aim to promote market fairness, transparency, and the supply of growth funds amid evolving capital markets." — Finance Minister Katsuyuki Katayama [paraphrased], 10 April 2026

Japan's cabinet approved an amendment to the Financial Instruments and Exchange Act (FIEA) on 10 April 2026, reclassifying approximately 105 crypto assets — including BTC and ETH — from the Payment Services Act (PSA) framework to the same legal category as stocks and bonds. Oversight shifts from the FSA's payments division to its securities regulatory framework, with the Securities and Exchange Surveillance Commission (SESC) gaining strengthened enforcement authority over crypto markets. The bill now goes to the Diet; if passed, it takes effect in fiscal year 2027 (April 2027). NFTs and certain stablecoins remain under the existing PSA framework.

Key provisions: a ban on insider trading based on non-public material information; mandatory annual disclosures from issuers covering technology, volatility, and governance; and sharply increased penalties — maximum prison terms rise from 3 to 10 years for unregistered sales, fines from ¥3 million to ¥10 million. A companion measure proposes a flat 20% capital gains tax aligned with equities, plus a three-year loss carry-forward mechanism — replacing the current miscellaneous income treatment that carries a maximum 55% marginal rate.

📎 Historical Precedent: Japan reclassified crypto from "property" to "currency" under the 2017 amended PSA after the Mt Gox collapse; this FIEA upgrade is the first major jurisdictional reclassification since and mirrors South Korea's Digital Asset Basic Act trajectory now advancing in parallel.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

FIEA reclassification means Japan's 7 million retail crypto account holders gain the same market abuse protections as equity investors, while exchanges face broker-dealer–equivalent disclosure and market surveillance obligations. The annual disclosure requirement is the most consequential provision for issuers: projects listed on Japanese platforms must produce governance and technology disclosure documents annually or face SESC enforcement — a standard that will effectively delist most sub-scale tokens. The 20% flat CGT is a structural positive: the current 55% marginal rate on miscellaneous income has demonstrably suppressed realised gains and retail re-engagement, and its removal could trigger a retail inflow cycle comparable to 2020–2021.

🔄 Contrarian Take

FIEA reclassification opens crypto to institutional equities infrastructure but also invites Japan's notoriously slow IPO-style listing approval process. The FSA's conservative approach under the PSA already produced a 30-exchange ecosystem with limited token access; applying FIEA listing standards to 105+ tokens could paradoxically shrink Japanese retail access by delisting tokens that cannot meet disclosure requirements — repeating the post-2018 Coincheck crackdown dynamic where stricter rules reduced rather than expanded the market.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Bitflyer, Coincheck, and GMO Coin face a 12–24 month compliance buildout to meet FIEA-equivalent market surveillance, AML monitoring, and annual disclosure obligations. Binance and OKX — which lack Japanese FIEA licenses — are effectively barred from the post-amendment market. Custody service providers operating under banking licences (MUFG, SMBC Digital Assets) gain a material advantage as the only entities already equipped for FIEA-level operations. For global asset managers running Japan-domiciled crypto funds, the FIEA framework creates a clear custodian selection matrix: only FIEA-licensed custodians will qualify from FY2027.

📊 Portfolio Impact

Bullish [FSA-licensed Japanese exchanges, BTC/ETH, retail-accessible major tokens]: FIEA reclassification + flat 20% CGT is a net positive for Japanese retail participation and institutional product development from FY2027.

Bearish [Long-tail tokens lacking annual disclosure capacity, Binance/OKX Japan revenue]: Disclosure requirements will delist most sub-scale projects; unlicensed exchanges lose market access.

Neutral [Global DeFi protocols]: FIEA primarily affects CEX-listed assets; DeFi is indirectly affected by changed retail flow patterns but not directly in scope.

HIGH U.S. Treasury / Federal Register [PRIMARY] · STABLECOIN · US FEDERAL
🟢 High Confidence — Federal Register Vol. 91, No. 64, 3 April 2026; Treasury press release sb0428

Treasury Opens Comment Period on GENIUS Act State Stablecoin Framework — $10B Threshold, OCC-Anchored Reserves, June 2 Deadline

"Treasury welcomes comments on the proposal from all interested stakeholders." — U.S. Department of the Treasury, GENIUS Act Broad-Based Principles NPRM, 3 April 2026

On 3 April 2026, Treasury published its first GENIUS Act notice of proposed rulemaking in the Federal Register (Docket 2026-06489), establishing the "broad-based principles" test for determining whether a state-level regulatory regime is "substantially similar" to the federal framework. The NPRM governs state qualified payment stablecoin issuers (SQPSIs) — state-chartered, non-bank issuers with up to $10 billion in outstanding stablecoins who can elect to operate primarily under state, not federal, oversight. Comments are due 2 June 2026.

Treasury's proposed principles require state regimes to anchor capital requirements in common equity tier 1 and additional tier 1 definitions (mirroring federal bank capital rules), restrict permissible reserve assets to only those the OCC has approved as "similarly liquid Federal Government-issued assets," and mandate a fair, transparent, and viable licensing application process. States may be more conservative than OCC standards but not more permissive — creating a federal floor with upward-only state variation. Until Treasury finalises this rule, no state can be certified as a valid alternative to federal PPSI oversight.

📎 Historical Precedent: The GENIUS Act's $10B state/federal threshold mirrors the Dodd-Frank–era insurance regulatory model (Collins Amendment, 12 U.S.C. §1831o), under which states supervised smaller insurers while federal rules set a minimum floor — a framework that took five years of litigation before stable jurisdictional lines were established.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

This is a gateway rulemaking: without a finalised Treasury "substantially similar" determination, no state stablecoin regime can be certified, meaning all issuers — regardless of size — must operate under federal PPSI supervision or halt issuance during the gap. Wyoming, New York, and Texas are closest to qualifying based on existing SPDI, BitLicense, and virtual currency frameworks respectively, but all three require legislative amendments to meet the CET1/AT1 capital anchoring and OCC reserve-asset restrictions. Treasury's reserve-asset constraint is the most consequential and contestable provision: it forecloses state experimentation with broader reserve compositions — directly overriding Wyoming's earlier SPDI reserve proposals and limiting the state pathway to issuers who mirror federal conservatism.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The $10B threshold means the entire state pathway is designed for community bank–affiliated stablecoin programs and regional fintechs — a market segment that does not yet exist at scale. Circle ($60B USDC), Tether ($143B), and even Ripple's RLUSD ($1.25B, growing) are all in the federal lane regardless of state certification. This NPRM is primarily an anti-race-to-the-bottom mechanism, not a meaningful state competition framework — and the compliance cost of qualifying a state regime likely exceeds the benefit for most state regulators.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Wyoming SPDI banks (Custodia Bank, Kraken Financial) are the most obvious intended beneficiaries of state certification, but will need legislative support to meet the CET1 anchoring requirement. New York's NYDFS will need to amend the BitLicense framework — reserve requirements alone require legislative action in Albany, not just regulatory rulemaking. Circle and Paxos, both pursuing OCC charters and firmly in the federal PPSI lane, are unaffected by this NPRM but will monitor state certification as a competitive market-entry signal for regional challengers.

📊 Portfolio Impact

Bullish [Wyoming SPDI banks, regional bank tokenised deposit programs]: State certification pathway creates a compliant sub-$10B stablecoin issuance route for community banks that can meet the federal capital floor.

Bearish [Non-standard state stablecoin experiments]: OCC-anchored reserve standard forecloses non-Treasury reserve compositions that several states had proposed, limiting state-level innovation.

Neutral [USDC, USDT, RLUSD]: All major issuers are in the federal PPSI lane regardless of state certification; this NPRM does not affect their competitive position.

UPDATE: CLARITY Act — Senate Returns From Easter Recess, Two-Week Markup Window Now Open With Yield Standoff Still Unresolved

"The prohibition is not ambiguous. It is the commercial model." — unnamed Senate Banking Committee staffer on the stablecoin yield language, FinTech Weekly, April 2026

The Senate returned from Easter recess on 13 April, opening the two-week window in which Senator Cynthia Lummis has confirmed the Banking Committee will attempt a CLARITY Act markup. The weeks of 13 and 20 April are the only remaining Senate sessions before Senator Bernie Moreno's self-imposed May deadline, after which he has warned the bill could stall until post-midterms. Executive pressure is now substantial: Treasury Secretary Bessent publicly urged passage on 8 April; PCAST members Marc Andreessen and Fred Ehrsam — both Trump-aligned — endorsed the bill on 11 April despite accepting the yield restrictions that Coinbase has rejected.

The central sticking point remains unchanged: the 23 March draft bans passive yield on stablecoin balances and permits only narrowly defined activity-based rewards. Coinbase, which generated $1.35 billion in stablecoin revenue in 2025 (approximately 19.6% of net revenue), has privately told Senate staff it cannot accept the current text. A #BoycottCoinbase movement from XRP holders — frustrated that Coinbase's opposition is delaying a bill that would confirm XRP's commodity status — has introduced unusual public pressure on a traditionally behind-closed-doors lobbying standoff.

📎 Historical Precedent: The 2010 Durbin Amendment (Dodd-Frank §1075) banned debit interchange arrangements over intense banking-industry opposition and passed in a narrow lame-duck session — demonstrating that revenue-threatening provisions can advance when executive branch pressure and bipartisan momentum align, even against well-funded opposition.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The structural dynamic has shifted: White House + Treasury + PCAST vs. Coinbase on a single provision. Senate Banking markup rules require Chairman Tim Scott to schedule the session; the absence of a confirmed date as of 14 April is the key indicator to watch. If no markup date is set by the end of the week of 13 April, the probability of pre-May passage drops materially. If the bill advances with current yield language, Coinbase faces approximately $270 million in annual revenue impact at current USDC rates — a figure material enough to sustain lobbying resistance but not large enough to be existential for a firm with $6.9 billion in 2025 net revenue.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The "Coinbase is blocking this" narrative misdiagnoses the structural issue: the yield ban also adversely affects Stripe, PayPal, and any fintech offering stablecoin-linked rewards programs. More importantly, if the yield ban is enacted and later challenged on First Amendment or dormant Commerce Clause grounds, it could be struck down before implementation — creating a worse outcome than the status quo. The longer-term risk is a two-tier stablecoin market: US-issued stablecoins (no yield) versus MiCA or MAS-authorised equivalents (yield potentially permissible), driving institutional demand offshore.

🏗️ Industry Implications

A successful markup in the week of 13–20 April would be the most significant US crypto legislative milestone since the GENIUS Act's July 2025 enactment. For Ripple (RLUSD), a payments-only model is structurally unaffected by the yield ban and directly benefits from CLARITY Act commodity confirmation of XRP. Circle — which dropped 20% in March on the yield draft leak — faces a post-enactment recovery scenario as institutional investors reprice its payments-and-settlement business absent the yield product line. Coinbase's decision to support the bill publicly (CEO Armstrong endorsed it on 10 April) while staff privately oppose the yield language creates an internal contradiction that Senate staff will exploit in markup negotiations.

📊 Portfolio Impact

Bullish [XRP/Ripple, RWA tokenisation platforms, payment-model stablecoin issuers]: Commodity classification for BTC/ETH/SOL/XRP confirmed; CLARITY Act market structure clarity accelerates institutional product launches.

Bearish [Coinbase stock (COIN), yield-dependent stablecoin programs]: Passage with current yield language costs Coinbase ~$270M annual revenue; Circle's stablecoin yield product is constrained.

Neutral [BTC, ETH as commodities]: Both confirmed as CFTC-jurisdictional commodities under CLARITY Act; no incremental regulatory burden on holders.

EU AI Act Sandbox Deficit: 16 of 27 Member States Silent, Harmonised Standards Missed, With August 2 Full-Application Deadline 110 Days Away

"With fewer than five months remaining until 2 August 2026 — the date when the majority of the Act's provisions enter force — the infrastructure required to actually enforce the law remains, in significant parts, unbuilt." — European Parliament Think Tank, 1 April 2026

A 1 April European Parliament Think Tank analysis found that only 5 of 27 EU member states have functional AI regulatory sandboxes — the controlled testing environments required under Article 57 of the EU AI Act, where companies trial high-risk AI systems under regulatory supervision before market launch. Four states have declared intent; 16 have not communicated any plans. All 27 member states are required to have at least one national sandbox operational by 2 August 2026. Spain's AESIA authority is the most advanced, having opened its sandbox in 2025 and hosted 12 high-risk AI systems. Only 8 of 27 states have designated national AI enforcement contacts.

The compliance deficit is compounded by a standards failure: CEN and CENELEC missed their 2025 deadline for harmonised technical standards that companies need to demonstrate AI Act conformity for high-risk systems. The bodies are now targeting end of 2026 — six months after the enforcement date — meaning firms with high-risk AI systems deployed in the EU will face an August 2 enforcement date with no certified conformity pathway available through harmonised standards.

📎 Historical Precedent: GDPR's Article 57 supervisory authority designation deadline (May 2018) was missed by multiple member states without triggering formal Commission infringement proceedings — enforcement gaps closed organically by 2019, but the two-year implementation delay allowed non-compliant data practices to persist across the EU single market.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The sandbox deficit creates asymmetric compliance costs: companies in Spain, Germany, and the Netherlands — the three most advanced sandbox jurisdictions — have a lower-friction path to pre-certifying high-risk AI systems than companies based in the 16 non-communicating states. For firms using AI in fintech, credit scoring, or AML — all high-risk AI categories under Annex III — the practical compliance route is documented internal risk assessment plus sandbox testing where available, which shifts legal liability from the regulatory infrastructure back to the developer. Commission enforcement against member states for sandbox non-compliance is legally possible under Article 258 TFEU but historically slow; a more immediate risk is that national competent authorities in non-sandbox states begin improvised enforcement after August 2, creating fragmented obligations across the single market.

🏗️ Industry Implications

For AI firms in financial services — credit risk models, fraud detection, AML screening — the absence of harmonised standards means there is no official pre-clearance route in most EU member states before the August 2 enforcement date. KPMG, EY, and PwC are already marketing AI Act compliance gap assessments as a bridging service. US-headquartered AI firms operating EU subsidiaries face the highest exposure: they must comply with the AI Act regardless of where their development teams are based, and their non-EU parent structures are unlikely to provide any legal separation from liability under the Act's extraterritorial scope provisions.
