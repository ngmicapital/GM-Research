Alpha — Order Flow: The Foundation

Thursday · 29 May 2026 · Sydney · Issue #35 · 8 min read
TL;DR

Order flow is the pulse of the market: every price move is caused by market orders hitting limit orders, and the direction of that aggression tells you who is in control right now. Understanding the difference between passive (limit) and aggressive (market) orders — who initiates, who absorbs, who pays the spread — is the bedrock beneath every advanced orderflow concept: CVD, footprints, imbalance, absorption. You can’t read any of them correctly without this mental model locked in.

§01
What It Is
The definition that makes everything else make sense
Order flow is the real-time record of who is aggressively initiating trades and in which direction — it is the only direct evidence of actual buying or selling pressure hitting the market.

Order flow is not price. Price is the location where the last trade printed. Order flow is the sequence of trades that produced that price — and specifically, which side of each trade was aggressive. A market order to buy hits the best available offer; that trade is classified as a buyer-initiated, or aggressive, buy. A market order to sell hits the best available bid; that is an aggressive sell. The limit orders sitting on the book providing those prices are passive — they do not initiate anything. They wait.

Price moves when aggressive demand exhausts passive supply at a given level, or vice versa. If buyers keep firing market orders at the offer, eventually all the limit sells at that price are filled and the exchange has to go to the next offer level up. Price ticks up. Nothing else causes price to move in a continuous auction market. No news, no chart pattern, no sentiment — these only matter to the extent they cause someone to pick up a phone and send a market order.

Market OrderAggressive — executes immediately at best available price; pays the spread; initiates the trade
Limit OrderPassive — rests on the book; waits to be filled; provides liquidity; earns the spread
AggressorThe side that sends the market order; classified as buyer or seller by whether they hit the bid or lift the offer
Trade ClassificationAssigning each trade as buyer-initiated or seller-initiated; the input to CVD, footprints, and imbalance metrics
SpreadThe gap between best bid and best offer; aggressors pay it, passive orders earn it
If you only remember one thing Price is the scoreboard. Order flow is the game. The scoreboard only tells you who’s winning; order flow tells you who is pressing and who is giving up ground.
§02
Mechanics
How trades are classified and how aggression produces price movement
Every trade is stamped as buyer-initiated or seller-initiated using the tick rule or Lee-Ready algorithm; the running net total of that classification is CVD — and CVD diverging from price is the signal.

Exchanges broadcast a real-time feed of every executed trade: price, size, timestamp, and whether the aggressor was a buyer or seller. On liquid exchanges like Binance and OKX, this is the “trades” websocket — raw tape. The classification is done server-side by comparing the trade price to the prevailing bid/ask at the moment of execution.

For exchanges or data feeds that don’t provide side classification directly, two algorithms dominate:

AlgorithmMethodBest Used When
Tick RuleIf trade price is higher than last trade: buyer-initiated. If lower: seller-initiated. If equal: same as previous.High-frequency data; any liquid market; fast to compute
Lee-ReadyCompare trade price to midpoint of prevailing bid/ask. Above mid = buyer; below mid = seller; at mid = tick rule fallback.Lower-frequency data where bid/ask is available; more accurate for illiquid instruments

The Cumulative Volume Delta (CVD) is simply the running sum of buyer-initiated volume minus seller-initiated volume across all trades:

CVD(t) = ∑ [Volbuy(i) − Volsell(i)] for i = 1 to t

Delta per candle = Volbuy in candle − Volsell in candle

Positive delta = aggressive buyers dominated that period. Negative delta = aggressive sellers dominated. Divergence between CVD direction and price direction = the signal.

The critical concept: price can move up while CVD is falling (passive absorption — large limit buyers absorbing aggressive sells while price holds up), or price can hold flat while CVD spikes (aggressive buyers are being absorbed — large limit sellers defending a level). These divergences are the foundation of absorption analysis, which this brief’s covered concepts will layer on top of.

A key mechanical nuance: most retail platforms show you tick-level CVD, not order-level CVD. One large institutional market order that sweeps multiple price levels will show up as many ticks in the feed, all buyer-initiated. The trade count overstates the number of independent decisions being made. What matters is the dollar volume of aggression, not the number of ticks.

§04
In Practice
How to actually use order flow — not just what it is
The heuristic that matters most: before entering any trade, know which side is currently aggressive — and whether the other side is absorbing or retreating.
1
Read the tape before reading the chart
Open the Time & Sales and watch for one minute before touching a level. Is print size growing? Are buyers or sellers printing larger? Are there consistent 10–50 BTC market orders hitting one side? This is the ground truth. The chart is a historical summary of it.
2
Use CVD divergence to identify absorption
If CVD is falling (aggressive sellers active) but price is not falling — or is rising — there is a large passive buyer absorbing the sells. That is not a short setup. That is a compression spring. Wait for the aggressive sellers to exhaust and price to break upward from the absorbed level.
3
Size your reads to the timeframe
On a 1-minute timeframe, a 50 BTC aggressive buy is significant. On a 4-hour timeframe, the same 50 BTC is noise. Calibrate what counts as “large” to the period you are trading. Intraday scalpers watch absolute dollar volume per trade; swing traders watch daily delta cumulative flows.
4
Look for aggression into a key level, not away from it
The most reliable order flow entries come when aggressive buyers accelerate into a level from below (they want in at any price) or when aggressive sellers pile into a rejection. Aggression away from a level after the fact is confirmation, not signal. The signal is the aggression that precedes the move.

Worked example — BTC long setup via absorption:

BTC at $107,200. CVD falling steadily for 40 minutes: −3,200 BTC cumulative aggressive selling. Price: flat at $107,150–$107,250. The aggressive sellers are being fully absorbed by passive buyers at the level. Entry: wait for CVD to flatten or tick positive for the first time in the session. Market buy at $107,280 when CVD flips. Stop: $106,900 (below the absorbed zone, $350 risk). Target: $108,400 (R/R 3.2:1). Rationale: the absorption confirmed that passive buyers were committed to this level; the CVD flip signalled aggressive sellers exhausted, removed the supply pressure, and set up the path of least resistance upward.

Timeframe fit: Order flow aggression reads are most accurate on 1m–15m for execution timing; 1h–4h delta cumulative trends for directional bias. Don’t apply 1m tape reads to daily-chart positions.

§07
Common Traps
What kills traders who half-understand order flow
The biggest mistake: confusing high volume with high aggression — large volume can be entirely passive, telling you nothing about directional pressure.
TRAP 01
Treating volume as order flow
High volume means a lot of trades happened. It does not tell you the direction of aggression. A 50 BTC candle with balanced delta (+0 net) is meaningless for directional bias. Always break volume into aggressive buy volume and aggressive sell volume. Raw volume with no delta decomposition is chart noise dressed as information.
TRAP 02
Assuming aggressive buying = price going up
Aggressive buyers can be absorbed by large passive sellers who never let price move. This is the entire basis of how institutional sellers distribute into a retail rally. CVD can rise strongly while price fails to break higher. The missing component is the passive side. Always read delta in the context of what price is doing in response.
TRAP 03
Using CVD on the wrong timeframe
A 1-minute CVD spike of +200 BTC looks significant. Zoom out to 1-hour and it is a single candle in a choppy session-level mean reversion. CVD has no meaning without the context of the timeframe you are trading. Reset your CVD at the start of the relevant session (Asia, London, NY), not from some arbitrary historical point.
TRAP 04
Over-reading low-liquidity periods
During the Asian overnight session or Sunday pre-market, a 20 BTC market order moves price 5x further than during London open with the same nominal delta. Order flow signals during thin periods have completely different signal-to-noise ratios. What looks like institutional aggression at 03:00 AEST is often a single mid-sized retail stop hunt. Weight your reads by liquidity condition.
NOTE — NOT AS USEFUL AS MARKETED
Real-time order flow without execution speed
Many retail tools sell “live order flow” data. At the timeframes they display (1-second to 1-minute bars), the information is genuine but the execution infrastructure to act on it is missing. The edge from reading aggressive flow at the 1-second level requires sub-100ms execution. Without colocation or DMA, this class of order flow is interesting for context and positioning reads, not for timing individual entries to the second.

Further Reading
Where to build the next layer
Start with Dalton for the conceptual framework, then Steidlmayer for the mechanics, then go to live tape.
Book
Mind Over Markets — James Dalton
The foundational text on auction market theory and order flow as a framework. Dalton articulated the idea that markets are auctions seeking fair value and that order flow is the mechanism by which that value is discovered. Chapter 5 on volume and trade facilitation is the entry point for understanding aggressive vs passive flow.
Book
Markets in Profile — James Dalton & Eric Jones
The update to Mind Over Markets, covering electronic markets. The section on “market-generated information” builds directly on order flow concepts and explains how to read auction behaviour in real-time tape rather than as a post-hoc chart analysis.
Article
Lee, C.M.C. & Ready, M.J. — “Inferring Trade Direction from Intraday Data”
Journal of Finance, 1991. The seminal academic paper on the Lee-Ready algorithm for trade classification. Not light reading, but understanding the mechanics of how trades are classified as buyer- or seller-initiated is essential for knowing where CVD data comes from and when it can be wrong.
Thread
@credo__v — Order Flow Primer Thread
A pinned thread on X that walks through the practical mechanics of reading order flow on crypto perpetuals: trade classification, CVD construction, delta divergence setups, and how to use Bookmap alongside raw tape. Crypto-native framing rather than equities-era terminology.
Guide
Exocharts / Velo Data Documentation
The platforms most actively used for crypto order flow analysis. Exocharts’ documentation covers cumulative delta, footprint charts, and bid-ask imbalance construction in detail. Velo covers institutional-grade CVD aggregated across spot and perpetuals simultaneously — the multi-venue view that catches cross-exchange flow.

Sources: Lee & Ready (1991) Journal of Finance; Dalton, Mind Over Markets (1993); Dalton & Jones, Markets in Profile (2007); Exocharts platform documentation; Velo data platform documentation; @credo__v order flow primer thread (X, 2024)
