Physics · Thermodynamics · Unsolved Problems

A Tanzanian Schoolboy Told His Physics Teacher That Hot Ice Cream Froze Faster. Science Has Been Arguing About It for 60 Years.

In 1963, a secondary school student named Erasto Mpemba in Iringa, Tanzania, was making ice cream when he noticed something that contradicted everything he had been taught about heat. He told his teacher. His teacher laughed at him. He told a visiting university physicist who was sufficiently irritated by the question that he actually tested it. The result was a paper in Physics Education in 1969 that introduced one of the most persistently contested, stubbornly unresolved anomalies in thermodynamics — a phenomenon so counterintuitive that the standard approaches to explaining it keep quietly failing.

The Mpemba effect — the counterintuitive observation that hot water can freeze faster than cold water under certain conditions — was named after a Tanzanian student who noticed it while making ice cream in 1963 and was dismissed by his teacher before a university physicist took the question seriously enough to test it; over 60 years later, the scientific literature contains multiple incompatible proposed explanations and an ongoing dispute about whether the effect is even consistently reproducible, making it a rare case of a seemingly trivial question from a school kitchen that has resisted complete resolution through six decades of peer-reviewed physics.

TL;DR

In 1963, a Tanzanian secondary school student named Erasto Mpemba was making ice cream with his class when he noticed that the hot mix placed directly in the freezer solidified before classmates’ mixes that had been cooled first. His physics teacher dismissed the observation as impossible. A visiting physicist from the University of Dar es Salaam — Denis Osborne — was provoked enough by the certainty of the dismissal to actually run the experiment. It worked. They published a paper in 1969. The effect — now called the Mpemba effect — entered the literature, and the search for a physical explanation began in earnest. Sixty-three years later, there are at least five competing proposed mechanisms, a persistent dispute about whether the effect is even reliably reproducible, a Royal Society of Chemistry public competition in 2012 that produced hundreds of papers and no consensus winner, and a fascinating 2020 study at Simon Fraser University that may have found the actual mechanism, but whose conclusions remain contested. It is one of the most studied “simple” questions in physics, and the correct answer is still not settled.

Section 01

The Story

Erasto Mpemba was born in Tanzania in 1950. In 1963, he was a student at Mkwawa Secondary School in Iringa when his class was assigned to make ice cream. The standard procedure was to boil the milk mixture, allow it to cool to room temperature, and then put it in the freezer. Mpemba, running late and wanting a good spot in the freezer, put his mix in while it was still hot. When he checked later, his was solid. His classmates’ — cooled before freezing — were not.

He brought the observation to his physics teacher, who told him he was wrong. “This is Mpemba physics, not universal physics,” the teacher reportedly said — a dismissal that became famous. Mpemba kept asking. When Denis Osborne, a physicist from the University of Dar es Salaam, visited the school and gave a lecture, Mpemba raised his hand and asked why hot water froze faster than cold. Osborne, by his own account, told Mpemba he was confused. But the question stuck.

“I decided to test the Mpemba claim,” Osborne later wrote, “mainly to show my technician that this was ridiculous.” His technician came back and told him the hot water froze first.

Osborne ran controlled experiments. In multiple trials, water starting at higher temperatures reached the freezing point before water starting at lower temperatures, under identical conditions otherwise. They wrote up the results and submitted them to Physics Education, which published the paper in 1969 under both names: Mpemba and Osborne. The effect had a name. Mpemba was twenty-three years old.

What Osborne’s experiments could not do was explain the mechanism. Their paper catalogued the observation and noted several possible contributing factors — evaporation, dissolved gases, convection currents, insulation properties of frost — but did not identify which, if any, was dominant. That question was left for future researchers. Future researchers have been working on it ever since.

Section 02

Why It Should Be Impossible (and Why It Might Not Be)

The naive objection to the Mpemba effect is the obvious one: for hot water to freeze faster than cold water, it must at some point overtake the cold water in temperature. But that means it must pass through every temperature the cold water started at — including the cold water’s starting temperature. If the hot water can reach the cold water’s starting temperature faster than the cold water can reach 0°C, something is changing the rate of cooling. Several candidates have been proposed, none definitively confirmed as the primary mechanism in all reported cases:

Evaporation. Hot water evaporates more rapidly, losing mass during cooling. Less mass means less water to freeze. A 100°C sample loses a non-trivial fraction of its volume before approaching 0°C. Some estimates put the evaporative mass loss at 16% or more. Less water could freeze faster. But this only fully explains the Mpemba effect if the containers are open. In sealed containers, the effect has still been observed in some experiments, which means evaporation cannot be the complete story.

Dissolved gases. Cold water retains more dissolved gas than hot water; boiling drives most dissolved air out. Dissolved gases may affect the thermal conductivity of water, or interfere with the formation of ice crystals, slowing freezing. If hot water had already been boiled before the experiment, it would contain less dissolved gas regardless of its temperature at the start of the trial. Some experiments find no Mpemba effect when both samples have been degassed. This is promising but still not unanimous in the literature.

Convection and the Frost Layer Problem

Hot water placed in a freezer creates stronger convection currents as its surface cools faster than its interior. This mixing can accelerate average cooling by keeping the coldest water at the surface in contact with the cold air. Additionally, cold water placed in a frost-coated freezer may sit on an insulating layer of frost that reduces heat transfer to the freezer shelf — while hot water melts through that layer, improving contact and conduction. These effects are real but highly variable depending on container geometry and freezer conditions.

Hydrogen bonding and the 2016 NTU hypothesis. In 2016, Xi Zhang and colleagues at Nanyang Technological University proposed a molecular explanation: when water is hot, the hydrogen bonds between molecules are stretched and store energy. As water cools from a hot starting point, these bonds snap back and release that stored energy, which must be dissipated before freezing can proceed — but the release happens at lower temperatures than for water that started cold, where the bonds were never stretched in the same way. This mechanism was widely covered in science journalism as a “solution” to the Mpemba effect. But the paper described a theoretical model, not a direct experimental confirmation, and subsequent researchers noted that the hydrogen bond dynamics described are present in all water and may not produce a difference large enough to explain the magnitude of the Mpemba effect in real-world conditions.

Supercooling and nucleation. Water does not always freeze exactly at 0°C; it can supercool below 0°C before ice nucleation begins, especially in clean containers without impurities for ice to form around. Some experiments find that cold water supercools more readily than hot water before nucleation, meaning it spends more time at sub-zero temperatures before actually freezing solid. If hot water nucleates more readily — perhaps because of trace impurities concentrated during partial evaporation — it may actually begin solidifying earlier in absolute time even though its average temperature is higher. This may explain some cases, but not all reported conditions.

Section 03

The 2012 Competition, the 2020 Study, and Why It’s Still Not Resolved

In 2012, the Royal Society of Chemistry in the UK ran a public competition asking scientists to submit the best explanation of the Mpemba effect. They received over 22,000 entries from 133 countries. The winner — a Croatian scientist named Nikola Bregović — proposed that the interaction between evaporation and convection in open containers was the primary driver. The RSC awarded the prize. This was widely reported as “the Mpemba effect explained.” It was not. The prize was for the best entry received, not for a settled scientific answer, and the scientific literature did not converge on Bregović’s explanation as definitive.

The most significant development since the original paper came in 2020, when researchers at Simon Fraser University — Avinash Kumar and John Bechhoefer — published results in the journal Nature using a very different experimental approach. Rather than working with bulk water, they used microscopic glass beads in a fluid as a model system, where they could carefully control initial conditions and measure “thermal relaxation time” precisely. They found that the system showed a pronounced Mpemba effect: samples prepared at higher temperatures reached the target low temperature faster than samples prepared close to it. Crucially, they also found the “inverse” Mpemba effect: cold samples could “heat up” faster than warm ones under the right conditions.

The Kumar & Bechhoefer result suggested the Mpemba effect was not a quirk of water’s chemistry or thermodynamics, but a general property of systems far from equilibrium — which would make it a much more fundamental phenomenon than anyone had suspected.

Their result raised the possibility that the Mpemba effect is an emergent property of any system that has been driven far from equilibrium — that the “memory” of a system’s initial state influences its relaxation dynamics in ways that standard equilibrium thermodynamics does not predict. This is interesting because it would reframe the entire question: instead of asking “why does hot water freeze faster?” the question becomes “why do systems with very different histories of being driven far from equilibrium relax at different rates?”

But there is a critical caveat. The SFU study was not done with water. It was done with colloidal particles in a fluid — a physically tractable model system, not the actual phenomenon Mpemba described. Whether the mechanism they identified applies directly to bulk water in a kitchen freezer is still an open question. The paper did not claim to have explained the original Mpemba effect definitively; it claimed to have demonstrated the effect in a controlled system and proposed a general theoretical framework. Whether that framework applies to the messy reality of tap water in a household freezer remains to be experimentally verified.

The result also highlighted a broader issue that has plagued Mpemba effect research for decades: experimental reproducibility. Several attempts to replicate Osborne’s original result under carefully controlled conditions have failed to find a consistent Mpemba effect. A 2016 study from the University of Washington found no reliable Mpemba effect across hundreds of trials when they tightly controlled for dissolved gas content, container material, and freezer conditions. Their conclusion was that many reported observations of the effect may be artefacts of inconsistent experimental conditions — variations in dissolved gas, container frost, exact temperature measurement — rather than a genuine thermodynamic anomaly. This does not prove there is no Mpemba effect; it suggests the conditions under which it occurs are more specific than earlier work implied.

Section 04

The Connections

What it says about how science handles anomalies. The history of the Mpemba effect is a case study in how the scientific process responds to observations that don’t fit the textbook. The initial dismissal by Mpemba’s teacher was not unique; scepticism from physicists who considered the claim impossible was the default response well into the 1970s. The observation survived because one person — Osborne — decided to actually test it rather than dismiss it on theoretical grounds alone. The effect then entered a long middle period where it was acknowledged as real but mechanistically unexplained, which meant it attracted papers proposing mechanisms without rigorous experiments, and a culture of claiming to have “solved” it developed repeatedly. The RSC competition formalized that culture: an institution awarded a prize for the best explanation received, not the correct one, which created the impression in science journalism that the problem had been resolved when it had not.

Systems far from equilibrium. The Kumar & Bechhoefer result, if it generalizes, would make the Mpemba effect relevant to a broad class of physical systems: anywhere a system is driven far from its equilibrium state, its relaxation pathway may depend on the details of how it arrived at its initial conditions, not just what those conditions are. This is potentially important for understanding protein folding, glassy materials, colloids, and even some financial systems (where the “memory” of past states influences future dynamics). Whether the connection is substantive or merely analogical remains to be worked out.

Erasto Mpemba’s life after the paper. The original paper made Mpemba’s name famous in physics classrooms but not his circumstances visible. He did not become a physicist. He trained as a wildlife manager and spent his career working in conservation in Tanzania and later for the Food and Agriculture Organization. He knew the effect bore his name but, by his own account, was not especially engaged with the ongoing scientific debate about it. He was sometimes invited to conferences and interviewed about the original observation. He died in 2023 at the age of approximately 73, having lived long enough to see the Nature paper by Kumar & Bechhoefer and the continued argument about whether the effect he observed in a school kitchen was a genuine anomaly or a laboratory artefact. The answer, by the time of his death, was still not settled.

What “simple” questions cost science. The Mpemba effect illustrates the disproportionate difficulty of questions that appear simple. The question “can hot water freeze faster than cold water?” seems like it should have a clean yes or no and a one-paragraph mechanism. Instead, it involves interactions between evaporation, dissolved gas physics, nucleation theory, non-equilibrium thermodynamics, and experimental design challenges that have occupied hundreds of researchers for six decades. This is not unique — the smoothness of the boundary layer in fluid dynamics, the exact mechanism of general anaesthesia, the precise reason birds navigate using magnetic fields, are all “simple” questions with decades of unresolved physics behind them. The Mpemba effect is a reminder that the cost of actually answering a question is rarely proportional to how easy the question sounds.

Further Down the Hole

📖
The Original 1969 Paper
Mpemba and Osborne, “Cool?” Physics Education, Vol. 4, 1969. One of the most cited papers in the journal’s history. Remarkably readable: it describes the original ice cream observation, the controlled experiments Osborne ran, and an honest list of possible mechanisms with no claim to have identified the correct one. Its restraint is a model for how anomalous observations should be written up.
🧪
Kumar & Bechhoefer (2020)
“Exponentially faster cooling in a colloidal system,” Nature, 2020. The paper that proposed the Mpemba effect as a general property of systems far from equilibrium. Technical but important: it is the first work to demonstrate the effect in a system controlled well enough to isolate the mechanism, and also to predict and demonstrate the “inverse Mpemba effect.” Available on arXiv: 2008.08673.
🧊
Burridge & Linden (2016)
“Questioning the Mpemba effect: hot water does not cool more quickly than cold,” Scientific Reports, 2016. The most rigorous attempt to replicate the original effect under controlled conditions. Found no consistent Mpemba effect when experimental variables were tightly controlled. A useful corrective to overclaiming, and a case study in why reproducibility matters before a phenomenon is accepted as robust.
🌊
Non-equilibrium Statistical Mechanics
The theoretical framework that Kumar & Bechhoefer’s work connects the Mpemba effect to. The field studies how systems evolve when they are not in thermodynamic equilibrium — which includes almost everything interesting in the real world. Luca Peliti and Simone Pigolotti’s book Stochastic Thermodynamics (2021, Princeton University Press) is the accessible entry point for anyone wanting the physics behind relaxation dynamics in driven systems.
🏆
The 2012 RSC Competition
The Royal Society of Chemistry’s public competition attracted 22,000+ entries and was won by Nikola Bregović. The competition materials, winning entry, and shortlist are archived on the RSC website. Reading the winning entry alongside the subsequent scientific literature that did not converge on it is instructive about how prize science differs from consensus science.
