Rabbit Hole

The Actress Who
Invented WiFi

Biography 7 April 2026 · Sydney, Australia
TL;DR

Hedy Lamarr — "the most beautiful woman in the world," MGM's biggest female star — held US Patent 2,292,387, a "Secret Communication System" co-invented with avant-garde composer George Antheil in 1942. The invention described a torpedo guidance signal that continuously hopped between 88 radio frequencies (exactly the number of piano keys) in synchrony, using a mechanism derived directly from Antheil's player piano expertise. The Navy said it was too complex and classified it. The patent expired in 1959. Three years later, the Navy deployed frequency-hopping technology during the Cuban Missile Crisis. Lamarr received no royalties. The same principle now underlies Bluetooth, WiFi, and GPS. She received an EFF Pioneer Award in 1997 — 55 years after the patent — reportedly responding "Well, it's about time." She died in 2000, largely forgotten by Hollywood, while the wireless industry she helped build was worth trillions. Her technical education had come entirely from sitting at her arms-dealer husband's dinner table listening to Nazi and Fascist officials discuss radio jamming over dessert.

Section 01

The Story

Hedy Lamarr's cover story was perfect. Born Hedwig Eva Maria Kiesler in Vienna in 1914, the only child of a Jewish banking family, she had become — by age 16 — a film actress, and by 18 — a scandal. Her 1932 Czech film Ecstasy featured nude scenes and what critics called the first simulated orgasm in cinema. Pope Pius XI condemned it. Mussolini approved it for the Venice Film Festival. US Customs blocked its import for seven years.

Then she made the marriage that educated her. In 1933, aged 18, she married Friedrich "Fritz" Mandl — Austria's wealthiest arms dealer, head of the Hirtenberg Cartridge Factory, supplier to Mussolini, and an associate of Nazi Germany's procurement apparatus. Mandl's wealth was matched only by his jealousy. He attempted to buy and destroy every print of Ecstasy. He forbade her acting career. He required her attendance at his business dinners — meetings with fascist officials, military attachés, weapons engineers, and Nazi procurement officers. Lamarr sat at these tables while men discussed torpedoes, radio guidance systems, the vulnerability of remote-controlled weapons to enemy jamming, and the technical limitations of existing warhead designs.

She absorbed all of it. She was bored, caged, and learning.

After two failed escape attempts, she got out in 1937 — disguised as a maid in one version, simply walking out in another. She made her way to London, maneuvered herself onto the same ocean liner as MGM co-founder Louis B. Mayer, and negotiated her own Hollywood contract at five times his opening offer. Mayer renamed her Hedy Lamarr — a tribute to the 1920s silent star Barbara La Marr. Her debut in Algiers (1938) opposite Charles Boyer made her a sensation overnight. Publicity machines called her "the most beautiful woman in films." She became the highest-grossing female star at the most powerful studio in the world.

In her off-hours, she built things. She kept a drafting table at home. Howard Hughes — one of the century's greatest aviation engineers — called her a "genius" and, when she suggested he study fast birds and fish to improve his aircraft wing designs, assigned engineers to investigate. She sketched technical ideas in notebooks. And in 1940, at a dinner party in Hollywood, she met composer George Antheil.

The Counterintuitive Core

Hedy Lamarr's technical education came not from a university but from three years as a trophy wife at fascist dinner parties. The more completely Fritz Mandl tried to cage her, the more inadvertently he armed her. The most useful thing he ever gave her was boredom in the right rooms.

Section 02

The Mechanism

George Antheil was the "bad boy of American music" — an avant-garde composer who had lived in 1920s Paris alongside James Joyce, Ezra Pound, and Man Ray. His most notorious work, Ballet Mécanique (1923–26), was scored for 16 synchronized player pianos running in mechanical lockstep from identical punched paper rolls. His central engineering problem had been synchronization: how to make separate machines, in different physical locations, switch states at precisely the same moment and remain coordinated through an entire performance.

When Antheil and Lamarr met in 1940, the Atlantic was a graveyard. Nazi U-boats were destroying Allied shipping at scale. In September that year, a torpedo had sunk the SS City of Benares, killing 77 of 90 children being evacuated from Britain to Canada. Lamarr told Antheil what she had absorbed at Mandl's dinner tables: radio-guided torpedoes were vulnerable to jamming. An enemy who identified the guidance frequency could broadcast a stronger signal on that frequency and redirect the torpedo away from its target. The fix seemed obvious in principle — but implementing it required a solution neither had alone.

The idea: the guidance signal should not sit on a single frequency. It should move — continuously hopping between frequencies in a pattern that transmitter and receiver both knew in advance, but an enemy could not predict. Antheil knew precisely how to synchronize two independent systems around a shared, pre-encoded pattern. The player piano had already solved it.

Their invention — formally a "Secret Communication System" — worked as follows. The transmitter aboard the ship and the receiver inside the torpedo would each carry identical paper tapes, punched with the same sequence of holes — exactly as with player piano rolls. Both tapes would begin running at exactly the same moment, driven by synchronized clocks. As each hole passed through the reader, it would trigger a switch to the next radio frequency in the sequence. Because both ends carried identical tapes started at identical times, transmitter and receiver would always land on the same frequency at the same instant — even as that frequency changed dozens of times per second. To jam such a system, an enemy would need to simultaneously overwhelm all possible frequencies. The specified number of frequencies: 88. Exactly the number of keys on a standard piano.

In Plain English

Think of it as a secret language where two people agree in advance to use a different codeword every ten seconds, rotating through 88 possibilities in an order only they know. Anyone trying to intercept the conversation would need to monitor all 88 channels simultaneously — a task that in 1941 was practically impossible. The piano keys were not a metaphor. They were the engineering specification.

US Patent 2,292,387 was filed on June 10, 1941 and granted on August 11, 1942. Hedy Lamarr's name on the patent was "Hedy Kiesler Markey" — her married name at the time, from her second marriage to screenwriter Gene Markey. Antheil was listed as co-inventor. The pair donated the patent to the US government rather than attempting to commercialize it. They received no payment.

The Navy's response, as characterized by historian Richard Rhodes, was essentially: What do you want us to do — put a player piano in a torpedo? Officials claimed miniaturization was impractical, despite the inventors having already addressed the problem with a pocket-watch-sized design. The patent was classified. Lamarr and Antheil were told she could better serve the war effort through her celebrity — raising war bonds. She raised $25 million in war bonds in a single evening, roughly $340 million in today's money. She did it, and got no credit for the other thing.

The patent expired in 1959. Antheil died that same year. Three years later, in October 1962, the US Navy deployed frequency-hopping communications technology during the Cuban Missile Crisis — the most dangerous fortnight of the Cold War. No royalties were owed to anyone. The patent had been dead for three years.

Section 03

The Connections

The principle that underlies every wireless device you own. Frequency-hopping spread spectrum — the technique Lamarr and Antheil described in 1941 — became the foundational insight of modern wireless communication. Bluetooth uses FHSS directly, hopping between 79 frequencies in the 2.4 GHz band at 1,600 times per second, making connections resistant to interference and interception. Early WiFi (IEEE 802.11) used related spread spectrum techniques; later versions use Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing, a cousin that distributes data across many simultaneous subcarrier frequencies for the same reason — resilience. GPS uses direct-sequence spread spectrum for its signals. CDMA, the backbone of 3G cellular networks, applies spread spectrum to shared spectrum access. Every phone call that doesn't drop in a noisy environment, every Bluetooth headphone that holds its connection across a crowded room, every GPS lock on a city block — all of it reflects the architecture of a solution worked out on a notepad in Hollywood in 1940.

The injustice was structural, not accidental. The Lamarr-Antheil patent expired in 1959. The first commercial applications of frequency-hopping in consumer wireless products arrived in the 1980s and accelerated through the 1990s, when the FCC mandated spread spectrum as the standard for secure civilian radio. Lamarr was alive and traceable for all of this. The companies building multi-billion-dollar wireless products owed her and Antheil's estate nothing — legally — because the patent had run its 17-year term before the technology became useful. Patent law implicitly assumes inventors and industries move at the same pace. They do not.

The recognition, when it arrived, was private and small. In 1997, the Electronic Frontier Foundation awarded Lamarr and the late Antheil its Pioneer Award. Her son Anthony Loder accepted on her behalf at the ceremony. Lamarr, 82, legally blind, and almost entirely reclusive in a Florida apartment, reportedly said when informed: "Well, it's about time." She also received the Bulbie Gnass Spirit of Achievement Award — the informal "Oscars of inventing" — becoming the first woman ever honored with it. In 1998, Austria gave her the Viktor Kaplan Medal. She died on January 19, 2000. The Wi-Fi Alliance completed the 802.11b standard two years later, bringing wireless internet into hundreds of millions of homes. Her name appears on none of the standards documents.

Howard Hughes knew. During their relationship in the early 1940s, Hughes — who built aircraft for a living and could assess engineering competence — gave Lamarr access to his team of aeronautical scientists and told her she was a "genius." When she suggested he improve his plane designs by studying the cross-sections of fast fish and fast birds, he assigned engineers to test the idea. The suggestion had merit. The engineers confirmed it. This is mentioned almost nowhere. Hollywood had a category for what Lamarr was — actress, beauty, scandal — and no category for what she also was.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

Music / Engineering Ballet Mécanique and the Synchronization Problem

George Antheil's 1923 masterwork required 16 player pianos to run in perfect mechanical lockstep. The engineering challenge — making separate machines start simultaneously and maintain synchrony from identical coded tapes — was solved before he ever met Lamarr. The frequency-hopping patent applied exactly that solution at a different scale. The player piano was not a metaphor. It was prior art.

Patent Law When Being Too Early is Catastrophic

The Lamarr-Antheil patent expired in 1959. Commercial applications arrived in the 1980s. By the time the first royalty-generating wireless products reached consumers, no IP rights remained. Patent duration is calibrated around average R&D timelines — inventors who are radically ahead of industry cannot be paid by the system as designed. Their only recourse was recognition, which arrived 55 years late.

Military Technology FHSS in Modern Electronic Warfare

Today's military radios use advanced frequency-hopping as standard anti-jamming and anti-interception practice. Modern FHSS military systems can hop through hundreds of frequencies per second using cryptographic pseudo-random sequences — known only to the paired transceivers — making interception practically impossible. The core architecture Lamarr described in 1941 is now a matter of national security doctrine worldwide.

Biography The Other Things She Invented

Lamarr was not a one-patent inventor. She worked on an improved traffic light system and a carbonated drink tablet designed to dissolve in water and approximate Coca-Cola — the latter inspired by wartime shortages. She reportedly helped Howard Hughes improve the aerodynamic profile of his aircraft wings by suggesting engineers study the fastest birds and fish. None of it made her famous as an inventor. The drafting table at home was not a prop.

Technology OFDM: The Descendant Technology

Modern WiFi (802.11n, 802.11ac, 802.11ax) uses Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing, a scheme that splits data across dozens of simultaneous subcarrier frequencies. The underlying logic — distributing a signal across multiple frequencies for robustness against interference — is the same insight Lamarr and Antheil codified. OFDM also underpins 4G LTE and 5G. The principle has never stopped being useful.
