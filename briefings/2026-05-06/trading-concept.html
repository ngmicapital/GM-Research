Alpha — Auction Market Theory

Wednesday · 6 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #14 11-min read
TL;DR

Auction Market Theory (AMT) is the meta-framework underneath every orderflow concept covered in this series — markets are continuous two-way auctions where price advertises opportunity, time validates value, and volume confirms acceptance. The practical edge: once you can label whether the tape is in balance (fade the edges) or imbalance (trade with the initiative), every other tool — CVD, footprint, heatmaps, absorption — slots into a coherent decision tree instead of firing context-free signals.

§01
What It Is
Steidlmayer 1980s · the framework all orderflow lives inside
AMT is the philosophy that every market is an organised auction whose only job is to find the price at which the maximum number of transactions can occur — that price is "value", and price oscillates around it.

The framework comes from J. Peter Steidlmayer, a CBOT trader in the late 1970s and early 1980s who built the original Market Profile to organise intraday data into a statistical distribution. The thesis is simple and physical: a market doesn't move because of an indicator crossing a level — it moves because aggressive participants are willing to pay the spread to get filled, and the price travels until it finds enough resting liquidity on the other side to satisfy them. Everything you watch on a footprint, a CVD chart, or a liquidity heatmap is a measurement of that one mechanic.

AMT replaces the indicator-based mental model with a process-based one. Instead of asking "is RSI overbought?" or "did the EMA cross?", you ask three questions: where is value, are we accepting it or rejecting it, and who is the aggressor. Those three questions cover every trade decision a discretionary orderflow trader needs to make. Wyckoff's accumulation/distribution schematics, Dalton's Market Profile day-types, ICT's "liquidity runs" — all of them are dialects of the same underlying language.

The reason it matters now, in 2026, with HFT bots dominating the book and ETF flows distorting spot vs perp, is that the auction mechanic itself hasn't changed. Bots still need a price to advertise at, time to wait for the other side, and volume to confirm. AMT abstracts away from the participant identity (pit trader vs market-making algo vs spot ETF authorised participant) and stays focused on the function: where does this market believe value is, and is it changing?

If you only remember one thing Price is not value. Price advertises; value is where time and volume agree to do business. Most of trading is correctly identifying when price is leaving an old value zone for a new one.
§02
Mechanics
The three components · the four phases · balance vs imbalance
AMT decomposes every market into three measurable components — price, time, volume — and uses their interaction to classify the auction as either balanced (rotational) or imbalanced (directional).

The components are deliberately reductive. Price is the auction's advertisement: it moves up to attract sellers and down to attract buyers. Time is its validation mechanic: a price the market "accepts" gets revisited; a price it "rejects" prints once and never returns. Volume is the confirmation: lots of business done at a price means the market agrees that's fair; little business means it's not. Plot all three and you get the bell-curve shape of a Market Profile — the densest section is the value area, the tails are excess.

Value Area (VA): VA = price range containing ~70% of session volume (one std dev) VAH = upper edge · VAL = lower edge · POC = densest price Acceptance test: price stays inside VA for ≥ 2 TPO periods → ACCEPTED price prints once and reverses → REJECTED (single print / excess) Balance condition: day's VA ⊆ prior day's VA (or substantial overlap) → BALANCE two-way rotation likely; fade the extremes Imbalance condition: day's VA fully above or fully below prior VA → IMBALANCE trend day; trade with initiative, not against it
Note: the 70% figure is convention, not law — TradingView and most platforms default to 70%. Steidlmayer's original used a normal-distribution one-sigma assumption (~68.27%); the rounding doesn't matter for trade decisions.

Steidlmayer's original framing identified four phases of any auction. Phase 1 — Initiation: price probes a direction, often violently. Phase 2 — Discovery: price travels until it finds resting liquidity strong enough to push back. Phase 3 — Acceptance: participants on both sides agree the new range is fair; volume builds. Phase 4 — Reversal or continuation: the auction either rotates back inside (balance) or breaks out again to repeat the cycle. Every Wyckoff schematic Tony recognises (accumulation, distribution, re-accumulation) is a stitched-together sequence of these four phases on a longer timeframe.

Auction stateTape signatureHow to trade it
BalancePrice rotates inside prior VA; volume builds at POC; rejections at VAH/VALFade the edges back to POC; tight stops; fail-fast bias

The crucial distinction is initiative vs responsive activity. Initiative buying is aggression that pushes price out of value upward — somebody decided the prior value zone is too cheap. Responsive buying is aggression that defends a level at or below value — somebody decided this is a bargain and stepped in to absorb. Same buy print on the tape, different meaning. AMT trains you to ask which one you're seeing before you act on it.

§04
In Practice
Three rules · one heuristic · a worked example
The single rule that matters: identify the auction state first, pick a setup second. Trading a balance setup in an imbalance market (or the reverse) is the most expensive mistake in this framework.
1

Label the auction before you look for a trade

First chart open of the session: is today's developing value above, below, or overlapping yesterday's? Overlapping = balance, fade extremes back to POC. Above/below with no overlap = imbalance, trade with initiative. Don't take a single ticket until that label is on the chart.

2

Use VAH, VAL, and POC as the only structural levels that matter

Forget Fibonacci, forget round numbers, forget pivots. The only levels with mechanical relevance are the ones the market built itself: prior session VAH/VAL/POC and the developing day's. Stops go behind them, targets at the next one. Everything else is decoration.

3

Trade acceptance, not the breakout candle

The breakout candle is bait — most fail. The trade is when price holds outside the prior VA for two TPO periods and starts building a new value range. You'll miss the first 30bps, but you'll skip every false break, which is a much better deal in the long run.

4

The POC is a magnet only inside balance

In a balance day, price returns to POC like a homing pigeon — fade VAH/VAL with POC as target. In an imbalance day, the prior POC gets abandoned and the new POC develops mid-trend. Never assume a POC is a target without checking which auction state you're in.

Worked example — fading a balance VAH
Auction stateBalance — today's developing VA fully inside prior VA (overlap > 80%)
SetupPrice grinds up to prior VAH ≈ 81,200 on declining CVD; rejection wick prints; tape pace slows
EntryShort on confirmed rejection — first lower high after VAH tag, ≈ 81,150
StopAbove prior session high + ATR buffer ≈ 81,420 (270 pts risk)
Target 1Prior POC ≈ 79,500 → 1,650 pts → 6.1R
Target 2Prior VAL ≈ 78,300 → 2,850 pts → 10.5R
InvalidationAcceptance above prior VAH (price holds > VAH for 2 × 30-min TPOs on rising volume) — auction has flipped to imbalance, exit immediately regardless of stop level
Timeframe fit: 30m TPO is the canonical session profile. For execution use 5–15m. AMT scales: weekly profiles drive swing positioning, daily profiles drive intraday bias, hourly drive entries.

§07
Common Traps
Where AMT rookies and AMT veterans both bleed
The biggest mistake: applying balance-day rules in an imbalance market. Fading a trend day to POC will systematically empty an account.
1
Fading every VAH/VAL without checking auction state

Mean-reversion to POC is real only in balance. In an imbalance day, VAH gets sliced through and the "fade" becomes a face-rip short. Always classify the auction first — overlap with prior VA = balance; no overlap = imbalance.

2
Treating the breakout candle as the trade

Most VAH breakouts fail and snap back inside. The actual edge is in acceptance: two TPO periods outside without rotation back. Yes, you sacrifice the first move; you also skip every fakeout, which is the bigger statistical win.

3
Mixing volume profile and TPO profile interchangeably

They produce different POCs and different VAs. Volume profile shows where business was done; TPO shows where time was spent. In low-liquidity hours TPO POC and volume POC can be 50bps apart. Pick one and stay consistent — most crypto desks default to volume profile, which is correct for 24/7 markets.

4
Using daily profiles in 24/7 crypto without defining a session

Crypto has no closing bell. A "daily" profile is whatever 24-hour window you choose — UTC midnight, your local close, the CME futures session. Different choices give different VAs. Pick one (UTC daily is standard) and never compare across windows.

5
The "AMT-as-vibes" trap (overhyped)

Honest read: a non-trivial slice of trading-X uses AMT vocabulary — "value", "POC", "rotation", "initiative" — as decoration on what's still a vibes-based call. AMT is a measurement framework. If someone says "we're in balance" without showing you a profile with a VAH/VAL on it, they're using the words, not the framework. Be one of the people who actually puts the levels on the chart.
