The Neutral Country That Wired Itself to Explode

Section 01

The Story

In the summer of 1940, Switzerland watched France collapse in six weeks. The Wehrmacht stood on three of its four borders. Italy, under Mussolini, held the fourth. The Swiss Federal Council met in an atmosphere of barely contained panic — a landlocked nation of 4.2 million, famous for clocks, cheese, and the explicit avoidance of other people's wars, was surrounded by the most powerful military machine in European history.

Most expected Switzerland to capitulate. It was, geographically, already inside the Reich. The Nazis had a plan — Operation Tannenbaum — ready to execute. German and Italian commanders had war-gamed the invasion and estimated it would take three to six days to overrun Swiss border defences.

What happened instead is one of the stranger stories in military history. On July 25, 1940, General Henri Guisan — the 65-year-old farmer's son who commanded the Swiss Army — summoned his entire officer corps to a meadow above Lake Lucerne. The location was the Rütli, the legendary field where, in 1291, the founding cantons had sworn the oath that created the Swiss Confederation. The symbolism was deliberate. Guisan told his officers Switzerland would not negotiate, would not surrender, and would not go quietly.

We will fight in the mountains. We will make Switzerland ungovernable. The cost of holding this country will exceed any possible benefit.

What Guisan had in mind was not conventional defence. It was something stranger and more ruthless: Switzerland would systematically destroy its own country.

The plan was called the Réduit national — the National Redoubt. The Swiss Army would not attempt to hold the border (a futile endeavour against a force ten times its size). Instead, it would fight a controlled retreat into the Alps, and as it went, it would blow up everything behind it — every bridge, every tunnel, every mountain pass, every railway junction. The infrastructure that made Switzerland economically valuable — the roads through the Alps connecting northern and southern Europe, the rail lines carrying goods across the continent — would be rubble before any invader could use it.

Then the Swiss would vanish into the mountains and wait.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The engineering required to execute the Redoubt was extraordinary. Over the following years, Swiss military engineers quietly worked their way across the country pre-wiring infrastructure for demolition. By the peak of the programme, there were approximately 3,000 designated demolition points — bridges, tunnels, railway lines, mountain roads — each packed with explosives and connected to detonation systems that could be triggered at short notice.

This was not symbolic. The Säckinger Bridge, a covered wooden bridge built in 1272 and one of the longest of its kind in Europe — a national monument — was packed with TNT. Medieval history, beloved landmark: it didn't matter. It went on the list. The attitude was clinical: if the enemy can use it, we destroy it.

Engineering detail

Bridges were pre-drilled with cavities, steel plates welded over the charges. Mountain tunnels had explosive columns built into the rock at calculated intervals for maximum collapse. Artillery positions were built into cliff faces on both sides of key crossings, so that even if an invader attempted to repair a blown bridge, they'd be doing so under direct fire from positions they couldn't easily target.

Simultaneously, the Swiss carved an entirely parallel country inside the Alps. Twenty thousand military structures — bunkers, gun emplacements, artillery forts, command posts, supply depots — were built into the mountains. Three enormous fortress complexes anchored the system: Saint-Maurice in the west, controlling the Rhône valley approach; Saint-Gotthard in the centre, commanding the critical trans-Alpine pass; and Sargans in the east, sealing the Rhine corridor.

The centrepiece was Sasso da Pigna, a fortress built between 1941 and 1945 at 2,106 metres above sea level. It extended 2.4 kilometres through the mountain interior, with over 8,000 square metres of operational space — artillery galleries, command rooms, barracks, kitchens, hospitals, and enough ammunition and food to sustain a garrison indefinitely. From the outside, the mountain looked like a mountain. Inside, it was a city.

Hundreds of smaller bunkers were disguised as chalets, barns, and rocky outcrops. Farmers would work fields containing concrete gun emplacements behind false stone walls. The camouflage was meticulous — some installations remained secret from the Swiss civilian population for decades, only declassified in the 1990s.

The logic of the Redoubt was economic, not military. Switzerland's geographic value was as a transit corridor — the Alpine passes connected northern and southern Europe, and whoever controlled them controlled the flow of goods, troops, and raw materials across the continent. The Redoubt strategy said: if you invade, you get the Swiss Army shooting at you from inside a mountain, and you get rubble where the passes used to be. The invasion cost becomes infinite. The transit benefit becomes zero. The rational actor walks away.

It was deterrence through self-destruction — the same logic that would later underpin nuclear MAD, applied with explosives and concrete in 1940.

Section 03

The Connections

It worked. Operation Tannenbaum was never executed. Historians debate exactly why — German war-gaming apparently concluded that subduing Switzerland would take ten to fifteen divisions and six months, not three days, and would produce a transit network of no value. Switzerland spent the war feeding refugees, hosting Red Cross operations, and running its banks. It never fired a shot.

The strategy lives on. Switzerland still maintains the world's largest per-capita citizen militia — every male citizen serves in the military and keeps his rifle at home. The country has enough nuclear shelters to house its entire population (built during the Cold War, when the Redoubt doctrine was updated for the atomic age). "Armed neutrality" is still the official policy, and it is genuinely armed: Switzerland has fighter jets, surface-to-air missiles, and the constitutional right to blow up its own infrastructure remains on the books.

Finland copied the model. After witnessing the Redoubt's deterrent effect, several neutral and small nations developed similar pre-demolition infrastructure strategies. Finland's Cold War defence doctrine was heavily influenced by the Swiss model: fight a costly retreat, deny terrain, force a cost-benefit calculation the invader will fail.

The bunkers found a second life. After the Cold War ended and the Redoubt was decommissioned (a process that began in 1995 and cost over a billion Swiss francs), the country had to figure out what to do with thousands of underground structures. The answers were creative. Sasso San Gottardo became a museum. La Claustra, a command post inside the Gotthard Massif, was converted into a 17-room luxury hotel — guests sleep in the former barracks and eat in the old officers' mess. Other bunkers became data centres, cheese cellars, mushroom farms, and wine storage facilities. Infrastructure built to deny resources to enemies became, in peacetime, an economic asset the country had already paid for.

The Säckinger Bridge survived. The TNT was finally removed in 2014, decades after the Cold War ended. The medieval bridge still stands, now a pedestrian crossing and tourist attraction. It has no idea how close it came to being rubble.
