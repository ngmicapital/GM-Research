CFTC Proposes the First Federal Prediction-Markets Rule — a Public-Interest Test That Bars War, Terror and Assassination Contracts While Clearing Most Sports

“This proposal gives the commission a durable, transparent framework to identify the contracts Congress directed us to scrutinize while letting legitimate markets move forward.” Michael S. Selig, Chairman, U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CoinDesk, 10 Jun 2026)

On 10 June the CFTC issued its first comprehensive Notice of Proposed Rulemaking for prediction markets, amending Commission Rule 40.11 to define which event contracts a designated contract market (DCM) may self-certify and list. Published in the Federal Register on 12 June (Doc. 2026-11854) under the title Prediction Markets; Public Interest Determinations, it opens a 45-day comment window closing around 27 July. It is the rule that cleared White House OIRA review last week — now public — and it puts statutory shape on a product class that Kalshi, Polymarket and Crypto.com have scaled into a multibillion-dollar market under no-action ambiguity.

The framework erects a three-step gate: confirm the instrument is an event contract; determine whether it involves an enumerated or "special" category; and decide whether listing it would be "contrary to the public interest." That last step turns on a multi-factor analysis weighing hedging utility, price-discovery value and the risk of "perverse incentives." Contracts tied to terrorism, assassination, war, other illegal activity, and — more contentiously — "gaming" are flagged as presumptively outside the public interest. Most sports event contracts are expressly welcomed: final scores, point differentials, win-loss results, tournament advancement, and individual and team statistical performance all qualify as legitimate price-discovery instruments.

The deeper play is jurisdictional. By codifying federal criteria for permissible contracts, the CFTC sharpens its claim that anything listed on a registered DCM falls under the Commodity Exchange Act's exclusive-jurisdiction clause and therefore preempts state gambling law — the precise question now being litigated in roughly a dozen states. Days before the NPRM, Polymarket sued Minnesota over its prediction-market ban, becoming the third plaintiff after the CFTC and Kalshi; the CFTC and DOJ had already jointly sued Illinois, Arizona and Connecticut on 2 April. A finalised Rule 40.11 is the agency's strongest weapon in that fight.

📎 Historical Precedent: Echoes the CEA's exclusive-jurisdiction clause (7 U.S.C. § 2(a)(1)) and the CFTC's 2012 rejection of Intrade-style political contracts — reversed in practice only after Kalshi's 2024 D.C. Circuit win reopened election event contracts.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Milbank's NPRM client alert reads the public-interest test as a real tightening for political and "gaming"-coded contracts even as it green-lights sports, and notes the three-step inquiry imports a Commission determination step that did not previously exist. Norton Rose Fulbright ("Prediction markets at a crossroads") warns the multi-factor standard invites case-by-case discretion. The Brief's read: the contract-eligibility rules are the headline, but preemption is the prize — a final Rule 40.11 lets the CFTC argue the Supremacy Clause knocks out the Minnesota, Illinois, Arizona and Connecticut bans wholesale, converting a 50-state legal patchwork into one federal venue. Watch whether the final text hard-codes a preemption finding or leaves it to the courts.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The Brief's read: the "gaming" carve-out is a loaded gun pointed at the very business the release claims to bless. If a future Commission reads season-long or player-statistic contracts as "gaming" rather than "sports price discovery," the same multi-factor test that welcomes sports today can delist them tomorrow — discretion cuts both ways, and a rule built on a public-interest balancing standard is only as friendly as the sitting Chairman.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Direct beneficiaries are the regulated venues named as the "first line of defense" — Kalshi, Polymarket and Crypto.com — plus Robinhood, which routes event contracts through Kalshi's DCM. Listed sportsbook operators (DraftKings, Flutter/FanDuel) face an asymmetric threat: a CFTC-blessed, nationally available sports-event market that sidesteps state sports-betting licenses and taxes. State lotteries and tribal-gaming compacts are the structural losers if preemption holds. The Brief's read: capital will chase DCM access — expect more exchanges to seek registration and more equity flows into prediction-market infrastructure on the assumption the category is now federally legitimate.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish Crypto.com, Kalshi/Polymarket (private), and event-contract distribution via Robinhood (HOOD) — a federal framework de-risks the category and pressures state bans.

🔴 Bearish State-licensed sportsbooks (DKNG, FLUT.L) and state lottery/tribal-gaming interests — a preempting federal venue undercuts the licensed-betting moat and tax base.

⚪ Neutral CME, ICE — incumbents can list event contracts but inherit new public-interest compliance overhead; near-term wash.

EU Publishes Its Final Code of Practice on Labelling AI-Generated Content — Watermarks and Signed Metadata Ahead of the 2 August Transparency Deadline

“Europeans have a right to know whether what they see, hear or read has been made or altered by AI, especially when such content can shape public debate. Transparency is how we protect trust. This Code of Practice gives AI providers and deployers a clear and practical way to label AI-generated content and deepfakes in line with the AI Act's rules that will apply from 2 August.” Henna Virkkunen, Executive Vice-President for Tech Sovereignty, Security and Democracy, European Commission (10 Jun 2026)

On 10 June the European Commission published the final Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content — a voluntary playbook landing roughly seven weeks before the AI Act's Article 50 transparency obligations take effect on 2 August 2026. The Code translates the statute into operational steps: deepfakes and AI-generated or AI-manipulated text published on matters of public interest must be clearly labelled, and users must be told when they are interacting with an AI system such as a chatbot.

Technically, the Code converges on two machine-readable mechanisms — digitally-signed provenance metadata (C2PA-style content credentials) and imperceptible watermarking — nudging the ecosystem toward interoperable provenance signals rather than ad-hoc visible tags. Systems already on the market get a transitional runway to 2 December 2026, and May's Omnibus agreement separately cut the transparency grace period from six months to three. Adherence is voluntary; Article 50 is not. Signing the Code is the lowest-friction way to evidence compliance, and the Commission and AI Board will now assess whether it is adequate.

📎 Historical Precedent: Mirrors the 2025 GPAI Code of Practice — a "voluntary code shadowing a binding obligation" — and builds on industry provenance standards (C2PA / Content Credentials) already shipped by Adobe, OpenAI and Google.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Agence Europe and K&L Gates frame the Code as the de facto implementation standard for Article 50: a voluntary instrument paired with a hard statutory date functions as a presumption of conformity for signatories. The Brief's read: the strategic question for every generative-AI vendor in the EU is binary — sign and inherit the safe-harbour optics, or don't sign and still owe full Article 50 compliance while carrying the burden of proving an alternative method works. Most serious deployers will sign.

🔄 Contrarian Take

EUNews headlined the move as a "mandatory labelling Code," underscoring genuine confusion about how voluntary it really is. The Brief's read: the watermarking pillar is the weak one — imperceptible watermarks are trivially strippable by re-encoding or screenshotting, so the signed-metadata path is load-bearing, and real enforcement will hinge on platform-level cooperation (the deployers who display the content), not on the model providers alone.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Frontier labs already shipping Content Credentials — OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Microsoft, Anthropic — face only marginal cost; the squeeze falls on open-weight image-generation deployers with no provenance pipeline and on smaller EU deployers staring at 2 August with thin compliance budgets. Provenance tooling vendors (Adobe and the C2PA toolchain, Truepic-style verification) get a demand pull-forward. The Brief's read: expect "provenance-as-a-service" to become a line item in EU AI procurement over the next two quarters.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish Provenance/watermarking tooling — Adobe (ADBE) and C2PA-aligned verification vendors — on compliance-demand pull-forward.

🔴 Bearish Open-source/open-weight generative deployers and small EU SaaS firms with no provenance pipeline facing the 2 Aug clock.

⚪ Neutral Frontier labs (MSFT, GOOGL) — already shipping content credentials; compliance is incremental, not a catalyst.

📋 What To Do — Before 2 August

US Banking Regulators Finish Stripping "Reputation Risk" From Supervision — the Formal End of the Crypto-Debanking Lever

“Reputation risk is not a sound basis for supervision. Regulators and banks have too often used it as a pretext for decisions that have nothing to do with safety and soundness, financial risk, or even BSA/AML compliance.” Jonathan Gould, Comptroller of the Currency, OCC (Statement on the Reputation-Risk Final Rule, 2026)

On 2 June the OCC, FDIC and Federal Reserve jointly reissued 15 interagency guidance documents scrubbed of every reference to "reputation risk" (OCC Bulletin 2026-23), operationalising the final rule the OCC and FDIC adopted in April and a parallel February Federal Reserve proposal. It is the capstone of a campaign launched by President Trump's August 2025 executive order, "Guaranteeing Fair Banking for All Americans," which directed agencies to stop using reputational optics to push banks out of lawful customer relationships.

For crypto this is the formal dismantling of the supervisory lever behind so-called "Operation Chokepoint 2.0" — the 2023–2024 debanking that froze exchanges, stablecoin issuers and founders out of the banking system after the Silvergate, Signature and SVB failures. FDIC Chairman Travis Hill has been explicit that a focus on "reputation risk" untethered from traditional risk channels "can pressure banks into debanking law-abiding customers who are viewed unfavorably by supervisors."

The limit matters: nothing here compels a bank to take crypto clients. It removes the regulator's thumb from the scale. Banks still apply BSA/AML, credit and market-risk screens — but they can no longer be examined down for "reputational" concerns alone.

📎 Historical Precedent: The original Operation Choke Point (2013–2017 DOJ/FDIC pressure on payday lenders and firearms dealers) and the post-Silvergate/Signature debanking of crypto firms in 2023 — the conduct this rule is designed to foreclose.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The Consumer Financial Services Law Monitor (Troutman Pepper Locke) characterises the 2 June reissuance as the supervisory-guidance layer catching up to the April codification — removing the residual "reputation" hooks examiners could still cite. The Brief's read: the win for crypto is structural, not cosmetic — it converts banking access from a discretionary examiner judgment back into an objective, contestable risk decision, which is exactly what a bank's general counsel needs to say "yes" to a digital-asset client without fearing the next exam cycle.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Skeptics quoted in American Banker argue the change is partly semantic: examiners retain broad "safety and soundness" discretion and can reach the same debanking outcome under a different label. The Brief's read: there's truth here — discretion migrates rather than disappears — but stripping the explicit pretext raises the evidentiary bar for adverse action and gives debanked firms a cleaner record to challenge, which is a real, if incremental, shift in leverage.

🏗️ Industry Implications

The direct beneficiaries are crypto firms most dependent on fiat rails — Coinbase, Circle, Kraken, Gemini — and the banks (and trust-charter applicants) that want their deposits but feared supervisory blowback. Custodia Bank's long fight over Fed master-account access is the canonical grievance this addresses. The Brief's read: pair this with the OCC's contested crypto trust-charter approvals (the Warren records fight) and the through-line is clear — the banking perimeter around crypto is being deliberately reopened from multiple directions at once.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish Crypto names dependent on banking access — Coinbase (COIN), Circle (CRCL) — and crypto-friendly banks regaining cover to onboard digital-asset deposits.

🔴 Bearish Niche "crypto-only" banking workarounds whose moat was scarcity of access — that scarcity erodes as mainstream rails reopen.

⚪ Neutral Money-center banks (JPM, BAC) — gain optionality but unlikely to rush crypto deposits near-term.

Medium CFTC Press Releases 9247-26 & 9236-26 / Gibson Dunn / CNBC [Primary] Enforcement · Derivatives
🟢 High — official CFTC actions; joint court motion filed

CFTC Dismantles Its Enforcement-by-Settlement Machinery — Scraps the 1998 "No-Deny" Rule and Moves to Vacate the Gemini Consent Order

“For nearly three decades, the Commission has refused to settle cases unless the defendant promised not to publicly deny the Commission's allegations. I am pleased that we are rescinding the no-deny policy consistent with regulators throughout the government.” Michael S. Selig, Chairman, U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission (Press Release 9247-26, 3 Jun 2026)

Two moves complete the CFTC's enforcement reset. On 3 June it rescinded the 1998 "no-deny" policy that had forced settling defendants to stay silent about the agency's allegations — mirroring the SEC's 19 May reversal of its own 1972 rule. Days earlier, on 27 May, the Commission took the rarer step of joining Gemini Trust Company LLC in a motion for relief from judgment, asking the Southern District of New York to vacate the prospective provisions of their January 2025 consent order in CFTC v. Gemini Trust Company LLC (filed June 2022).

The CFTC's own review concluded the complaint — over alleged misstatements during Gemini's 2017 bitcoin-futures self-certification — "should never have been filed," resting on testimony from a whistleblower later deemed not credible, and that Gemini was itself a fraud victim. The already-paid $5 million penalty stays; only the forward-looking injunctive terms would be lifted. A former agency chair told CNBC the move was "very unusual." Together, the rescission and the vacatur signal a CFTC that will actively unwind prior crypto enforcement, not merely decline new cases.

📎 Historical Precedent: The SEC's parallel no-deny rescission (19 May 2026) and its 2025 dismissals of Biden-era crypto suits against Coinbase, Kraken and Consensys — the template this CFTC action now extends from "drop" to "actively vacate."
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Gibson Dunn's derivatives weekly update and Katten note the no-deny reversal hands defendants real leverage — they can now settle while publicly contesting the allegations, eroding the reputational deterrent settlements once carried. The Brief's read: the Gemini vacatur is the louder signal. Re-opening a closed consent order to lift an injunction is extraordinary, and it tells every firm carrying a Biden-era CFTC order that the door to relief is open — expect more motions for relief from judgment citing this as precedent.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Gemini (the Winklevoss exchange, post-IPO) sheds an injunctive overhang at a useful moment; Coinbase and Kraken, already past their SEC dismissals, gain a CFTC analogue to point to. The Brief's read: the practical effect is a lower litigation-risk discount on US-domiciled crypto exchanges broadly, as the derivatives regulator joins the securities regulator in retiring the enforcement-first posture.
