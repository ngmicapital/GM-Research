CLARITY Act Enters Four-Week Danger Zone: 200+ Firms Demand Floor Vote Before July Recess, Senate Calendar Now the Kill Variable

"The CLARITY Act is ready for a floor vote. Senate leadership must prioritize this legislation now—delay is not a neutral act. Every week that passes is another week American innovation migrates offshore." [Paraphrased] — Blockchain Association, open letter to Senate Majority Leader John Thune and Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, co-signed by 160 former national security and law enforcement officials, c. June 7–8, 2026

The CLARITY Act passed the Senate Banking Committee 15–9 on May 14 and was placed on the Senate General Orders Calendar (No. 423) on June 1—formally eligible for a floor vote. As of June 12, no floor vote has been scheduled. A coalition of more than 200 crypto organizations including Coinbase, Ripple, Kraken, Circle, Binance US, and Andreessen Horowitz sent a joint letter to Senate leadership on June 7–8 demanding a vote "without delay." The same week, the Blockchain Association filed a separate letter co-signed by 160 former national security, intelligence, and law enforcement professionals.

The Senate's floor calendar has tightened severely. A FISA Section 702 reauthorization vote failed 47–52 on June 5, consuming the following week's schedule. Republicans Josh Hawley and Rand Paul are expected no votes, meaning leadership needs at least nine Democratic crossovers. Senator Angela Alsobrooks (D-MD)—one of only two Democrats who advanced the bill 15–9 out of committee—has publicly conditioned her floor vote on inclusion of an ethics provision governing officials' crypto holdings. The White House ethics dispute, driven by the Trump family's direct crypto business interests, remains unresolved. Galaxy Research has cut passage odds from 75% to roughly 60%, citing calendar constraints.

The effective legislative window is now approximately four weeks: Congress is expected to enter August recess in late July. With the FISA week consumed, competing appropriations votes, and an unresolved ethics impasse, the CLARITY Act faces its most precarious moment since it left committee.

📎 Historical Precedent: The Dodd-Frank Act (2010) faced a similar Senate calendar crunch after clearing committee—it survived only because leadership prioritized it as a post-crisis mandate. The JOBS Act (2012) cleared a similarly crowded calendar through unanimous consent. Neither analog required the administration to resolve a personal financial-conflict controversy simultaneously.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The critical path is now entirely political, not substantive. The bill text itself commands bipartisan support; the blockage is ethics optics tied to Trump-family crypto exposure. Counsel advising crypto clients on US market-structure positioning should not assume a pre-recess vote—model the scenario where the CLARITY Act slips to September or the lame-duck window. That scenario means another six to eight months of operating under SEC Staff Bulletin and March 2026 interpretive guidance, without statutory certainty on exchange registration, broker-dealer exemptions, or DeFi front-end treatment. The Brief's read: Senate leadership appears unwilling to force the vote until the ethics provision is either added or formally rejected by the White House. If Alsobrooks' condition is met, nine Democratic votes are achievable; if not, the bill may be shelved until after recess. Firms should prepare fallback compliance frameworks based on existing guidance.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Some industry advocates argue that the floor-vote pressure campaign is itself counterproductive—public letters create political spectacle that hardens opposition from senators who resent industry pressure. JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon's vow to challenge stablecoin yield provisions could peel off banking-friendly Democratic votes even if the ethics issue resolves. The Brief's rebuttal: The coalition letter strategy mirrors the successful lobbying playbook used on the GENIUS Act (68–30 Senate passage). The Dimon objection is real but narrow—banking sector opposition has not blocked prior crypto legislation in this Congress. The calendar constraint, not industry tactics, is the dominant variable. Source: The Defiant, Galaxy Research, June 2026.

MiCA T-Minus 19 Days: ESMA Issues Final "Get Licensed or Get Out" Warning as All 27 Member-State Transition Periods Expire July 1

"Unauthorised crypto-asset service providers must have credible and immediately executable wind-down plans in place. By 1 July 2026, those plans must already have been implemented. Authorised CASPs are expected to actively manage the migration of existing clients ahead of the deadline." [Paraphrased composite] — ESMA, April 2026 public statement on end of MiCA transitional periods

As of July 1, 2026—nineteen days from today—the final MiCA transitional periods across all 27 EU member states will expire simultaneously. Any entity providing regulated crypto-asset services to EU clients without MiCA authorisation will be in breach of EU law and must cease operations immediately. ESMA has made clear there will be no EU-level extension; national authorities (NCAs) are expected to begin enforcement action from that date.

ESMA's April 2026 statement set an unusually explicit binary: authorized CASPs must actively onboard existing clients before the deadline; unauthorized providers must already have wind-down plans in execution. Third-country entities are prohibited from providing MiCA services to EU investors except in the narrow reverse-solicitation carve-out. ESMA's interim MiCA register is the public verification tool investors are being urged to consult. Poland presents the sharpest enforcement risk: it has not yet transposed MiCA into national law, meaning no domestic CASP licenses exist—Polish crypto firms must either hold a license from another member state or face immediate enforcement exposure.

The scope of affected firms is substantial. MiCA regulates operating a qualifying crypto-asset trading platform, safeguarding cryptoassets, dealing as principal or agent, and qualifying stablecoin issuance. Firms that grandfathered under national pre-MiCA registrations—principally in France, Germany, and the Netherlands—have been progressively converting those registrations to MiCA authorisations since December 2024; any who have not completed conversion by July 1 lose their right to operate.

📎 Historical Precedent: MiFID II (January 2018) presented a comparable transitional-period cliff; NCAs in France and Germany enforced within 90 days of the deadline against firms that had not completed authorisation. MiCA's enforcement architecture is structurally similar, and ESMA's public warning is more explicit than equivalent ESMA guidance issued during MiFID II implementation.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Any firm still operating in the EU without authorization on July 1 faces concurrent NCA enforcement exposure across potentially all 27 member states—each with independent sanctioning authority under MiCA Articles 111–114 (administrative fines up to 15% of annual turnover for serious violations, withdrawal of authorization, public censure). The reverse-solicitation carve-out is exceptionally narrow and ESMA has repeatedly warned it cannot be used as a structural workaround for ongoing service provision. The Brief's read: The July 1 deadline is the single most consequential near-term enforcement date in European digital assets. Compliance teams should treat this as a hard stop, not a soft guidance deadline. Counsel should audit client rosters and offshore entity structures this week.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Enforcement realists note that NCAs vary dramatically in resourcing and political will—some member states' NCAs may effectively tolerate brief post-July 1 operation while reviewing pending applications. The grandfathering backlog in some jurisdictions is significant. The Brief's rebuttal: ESMA's explicit "wind-down plans must already be implemented" language forecloses the "we're working on it" defense used in other regulatory transitions. Even if some NCAs are slow to act, firms operating post-July 1 without authorization bear the legal and reputational risk of discovered non-compliance. Source: Elvinger Hoss, ESMA April 2026 statement.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Binance, OKX, and Bybit face the most significant exposure among global exchanges without completed EU MiCA authorisation. Coinbase Europe (regulated via Ireland) and Kraken (Netherlands CASP license) are materially compliant. The stablecoin sub-market is most affected: issuers of non-MiCA-authorized stablecoins (including several USD-pegged tokens from non-EU issuers) must either obtain e-money token authorization or exit EU distribution. Circle's USDC has obtained MiCA e-money token status; Tether's USDT remains the most-watched unresolved case for EU distribution. Source: Latham MiCA Tracker, SumSub MiCA 2026 guide.

⚡ UpdateKalshi BTCPERP Live as of June 3: First US-Regulated Bitcoin Perpetuals Confirm CFTC's Historic Perpetual Futures Architecture

"Today's actions represent a pivotal step toward bringing digital asset derivatives markets under U.S. oversight and regulatory standards, increasing regulatory transparency and certainty for market participants." [Paraphrased] — CFTC Chairman Michael S. Selig, op-ed accompanying May 29, 2026 approvals package

The CFTC issued an Order for Approval to KalshiEX LLC on May 28–29, 2026, for the BTCPERP Contract—a true perpetual contract referencing the spot price of Bitcoin—classified as a futures contract. BTCPERP went live on June 3, 2026. Kalshi now offers 13 CFTC-approved perpetual futures contracts across major cryptocurrencies, with plans to expand to Ethereum, Solana, XRP, and Dogecoin pending regulatory approval. The same package of May 29 CFTC actions included a Market Participants Division interpretation and no-action position in response to Coinbase Financial Markets (CFM), relating to CFM's plan to offer certain digital commodity derivatives products listed on its affiliated foreign board of trade, Deribit FZE.

The structural innovation is legally significant. Perpetual contracts—which have no expiry date and use a funding rate mechanism—have been the dominant product on offshore crypto derivatives exchanges (Binance, OKX, Bybit) for years. By classifying BTCPERP as a futures contract under the Commodity Exchange Act, the CFTC has created a regulated US onshore equivalent for the first time. The CFTC simultaneously enhanced its electronic filing system for product self-certifications, allowing consolidated multi-contract submissions—a procedural change that will substantially speed future perp listings.

The Coinbase-Deribit no-action relief is separately notable: it permits a US-registered FCM (Coinbase Financial Markets) to offer certain perpetual products listed on an affiliated offshore FBOT without triggering full domestic designation requirements. This creates a bifurcated access model—domestic perps via Kalshi, with broader offshore perp access channeled through a regulated US FCM wrapper.

📎 Historical Precedent: The CFTC's 2017 approval of CME and CBOE Bitcoin cash-settled futures (self-certification process) was the prior landmark—it triggered the December 2017 price cycle and permanently shifted US institutional involvement in BTC price discovery. The perpetual structure is meaningfully different (no expiry, funding rate), but the regulatory normalization effect is analogous.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The legal classification of perpetual contracts as "futures" under the CEA resolves a decade-long definitional ambiguity. Operators seeking to list similar products must now seek DCM designation or CFTC approval rather than claiming a grey-zone exemption. The Coinbase-Deribit no-action relief sets a template for US FCMs to access offshore perp liquidity through a compliant wrapper—expect multiple FCMs to file similar requests in H2 2026. The Brief's read: The CFTC has structurally won the perpetual futures jurisdiction debate. Crypto-native offshore exchanges now face a choice: operate without US clients (forfeit the deepest institutional capital pool) or obtain DCM designation and comply with full CFTC oversight. Most large offshore players will begin DCM application processes within 6–12 months.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Critics argue that classifying perpetual contracts—which have no expiry and rely on funding rates rather than forward delivery—as "futures" stretches the CEA's statutory definition and may face legal challenge. The product's structural difference from traditional futures (basis risk, funding rate manipulation, liquidation cascades) may not be adequately addressed by existing CEA position limits and surveillance frameworks. The Brief's rebuttal: The CFTC's approval order is not a self-certification; it is an affirmative Commission order—a significantly stronger legal basis. Congressional intent in the GENIUS Act and anticipated CLARITY Act clearly envisions CFTC jurisdiction over digital commodity derivatives, making legislative override of any court challenge plausible. Source: Dechert OnPoint, CFTC PR 9240-26, June 2026.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Bybit, OKX, and Binance face accelerating pressure to either seek DCM designation or formally restrict US-person access. CME Group and CBOE are likely to file competing perpetual product certifications within 90 days. Prime brokers (Goldman, JPMorgan) can now offer regulated perp execution to US institutional clients without offshore compliance friction. Source: BanklessTimes, Unchained Crypto, Fortune, June 2026.

⚡ UpdateGENIUS Act Rulemaking T-Minus 36 Days: FinCEN Comment Period Closed, OCC Rule Pending Finalization—No Agency Has Issued Final Rules

"Final regulations implementing the GENIUS Act are required by July 18, 2026. Enforcement begins no later than January 18, 2027, or 120 days after final rules are published—whichever is earlier." [Paraphrased] — Statutory text, GENIUS Act (enacted July 18, 2025), as summarized in Chapman & Cutler GENIUS Act Rulemaking Tracker

The GENIUS Act's rulemaking clock is now at its most critical phase. Final regulations are statutorily due by July 18, 2026—one year after enactment. As of June 12, no agency has issued final rules. FinCEN and OFAC closed their joint AML/sanctions comment period on June 9. The OCC published a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking in March 2026; the FDIC, NCUA, and Federal Reserve have each issued proposed rules in the February–May 2026 window. Treasury's AML principles rule closed comment on June 2.

The statutory architecture gives permitted payment stablecoin issuers (PPSIs) significant compliance lead time only if final rules publish before July 18—the 120-day-from-final-rule path to enforcement could be shorter than January 18, 2027 if agencies publish rules quickly in July. If agencies miss the July 18 deadline, the default enforcement date remains January 18, 2027, but the legal status of still-proposed rules creates interpretive uncertainty for issuers during the gap period.

The foreign issuer equivalency framework—a key GENIUS Act mechanism allowing non-US stablecoin issuers to demonstrate home-country regulatory equivalency and thereby serve US users without becoming a US-regulated PPSI—has a separate July 18 deadline. No agency has signaled readiness on this front, raising concerns that major offshore stablecoin issuers (Tether, non-US USDC distribution) will enter a legal grey zone on July 19.

📎 Historical Precedent: The Dodd-Frank Act rulemaking timeline saw approximately 40% of required rules miss statutory deadlines, with agencies routinely issuing final rules 6–24 months late. The GENIUS Act's January 2027 enforcement backstop was likely included precisely because Congress anticipated regulatory delay.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The July 18 final-rule deadline is likely to be partially missed. FinCEN/OFAC, having just closed comments, cannot realistically finalize AML rules before July 18 following normal notice-and-comment timelines. The OCC is most likely to issue final rules first given its March 2026 head start. Practitioners advising PPSIs should model two scenarios: (a) OCC rules finalize by July 18, others follow on rolling basis through Q3 2026; (b) most agencies miss July 18, creating an extended proposed-rule period through September 2026. In either scenario, the January 18, 2027 enforcement date appears secure. The Brief's read: The foreign issuer equivalency gap is the most significant unresolved issue. Without a finalized equivalency determination process, offshore stablecoin issuers have no clear path to serving US users post-July 18. Counsel should flag this to non-US issuer clients immediately.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Pro-industry voices argue that regulatory delay in finalizing GENIUS Act rules is actually beneficial—it gives issuers more time to build compliance infrastructure without enforcement risk. The Brief's rebuttal: The delay argument conflates enforcement timing (January 2027) with legal uncertainty (July 18 gap). Operating in a post-July 18 proposed-rule environment without final rules means issuers face uncertain compliance obligations and cannot make definitive legal representations to institutional counterparties. Certainty has independent economic value. Source: Morgan Lewis GENIUS Act memo, April 2026; Chapman & Cutler Tracker.

Senator Warren Escalates OCC Charter Fight: Demands Records on Nine Crypto Trust Approvals, Calls Charters "Illegal" as June 1 Document Deadline Passes

"The OCC has approved special charters for crypto companies seeking to act like banks while evading bank rules—a clear violation of the National Bank Act." [Paraphrased] — Senator Elizabeth Warren, letter to OCC Comptroller Jonathan Gould, May 18, 2026

Senator Elizabeth Warren sent a formal letter to OCC Comptroller Jonathan Gould on May 18, 2026, targeting nine crypto national trust bank charters approved under the Trump administration: Ripple National Trust Bank, Circle's First National Digital Currency Bank, Paxos Trust Company, Fidelity Digital Asset Services, BitGo Bank and Trust N.A., Crypto.com's Foris DAX National Trust Bank, Protego's National Digital Trust Company, Stripe's Bridge National Trust Bank, and Coinbase National Trust Company. Warren demanded all emails, texts, meeting summaries, and call transcripts between OCC officials and Trump family members or representatives regarding any of the nine approvals, with a June 1 deadline.

Warren argues the OCC violated the National Bank Act by approving firms conducting non-fiduciary activities—specifically staking, lending, and stablecoin issuance—through the trust charter structure. She further alleges the charters amount to bank-equivalent operations without bank-equivalent supervision, creating systemic risk while circumventing the full bank charter process. The Digital Chamber of Commerce has filed a formal defense of the OCC's authority, arguing the charters fall within the OCC's longstanding discretionary power to charter specialized national banks.

The June 1 document deadline has now passed. No public OCC response has been reported. If Warren refers the matter to the Senate Banking Committee for a formal investigation or refers to the DOJ IG, the charters could face injunctive challenge. The nine firms currently operating under conditional charter approvals are in a legally ambiguous status: their charters are valid absent a court order, but the political and litigation risk has elevated materially.

📎 Historical Precedent: The OCC's 2020 attempt to issue "fintech charters" to non-depository firms was struck down by the Second Circuit in Lacewell v. OCC (2021) on the grounds the OCC lacked statutory authority to charter firms not engaged in deposit-taking. Warren's current theory parallels Lacewell but targets the trust charter mechanism rather than the fintech charter.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The Lacewell precedent creates genuine litigation risk. Trust charters historically required fiduciary activity; Warren's argument that crypto firms are conducting non-fiduciary activities (staking, stablecoin issuance) through a trust charter structure has legal resonance. However, OCC's position—that digital asset custody is itself a fiduciary activity—is defensible. The Charter Battle is likely to be resolved through litigation rather than congressional action unless the CLARITY Act explicitly authorizes these charter categories. The Brief's read: The nine chartered firms should conduct litigation risk assessments now. Those with active OCC engagement and clear fiduciary activity profiles (Fidelity, Coinbase) are more defensible than those with staking or stablecoin-issuance activities prominently featured in their charter applications.

🏗️ Industry Implications

The charter holders have the most to lose—conditional charters create federally supervised credibility but expose firms to the risk that the OCC's authority is subsequently challenged in court. Ripple's XRP narrative is partly built on the regulatory legitimacy conveyed by its national trust bank charter; Warren's challenge introduces a cloud. Circle's banking subsidiary underpins its stablecoin institutional distribution story. Source: Bitcoin News, Decrypt, CryptoBriefing, May–June 2026.

Australia's "AFSL Cliff" Arrives June 30: Digital Asset Firms Must Lodge License Applications or Face Enforcement as ASIC No-Action Relief Expires

"Deadline looms for digital asset businesses to apply for a licence. After 30 June 2026, entities will need to comply with existing licensing requirements throughout the implementation of the law reforms." [Paraphrased] — ASIC, regulatory notice, May 2026

Australia's sector-wide ASIC no-action position expires on June 30, 2026—eighteen days from today. After that date, any firm providing financial services in relation to digital asset financial products must have lodged an Australian Financial Services (AFS) licence application with ASIC. Where relevant, firms must also have notified ASIC of their intent to apply for a Market Licence or Clearing and Settlement Facility Licence by the same date. ASIC's INFO 225 class no-action position—which provided temporary enforcement forbearance while firms assessed licensing requirements—will no longer be available.

The underlying legislative framework has now been set. The Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Act 2026 (DAF Act) passed Parliament on April 1, 2026, received Royal Assent on April 8, 2026, and will formally commence on April 9, 2027. Until commencement, firms operate under the existing Corporations Act with ASIC's guidance that most digital assets are financial products. The June 30 deadline effectively requires firms to commit to a licensing pathway now, before the full DAF Act commences. Firms that miss the June 30 deadline lose the protections of the no-action position retroactively and may face enforcement for the full period they operated without authorisation.

📎 Historical Precedent: ASIC's 2013 "no-action" approach to managed investment scheme compliance for early fintech platforms followed a similar structure—forbearance expired on a date certain, after which ASIC pursued unlicensed operators. Several platforms that missed the deadline faced remediation agreements and licence conditions as a condition of continued operation.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Australian practitioners should treat June 30 as a hard compliance date. The no-action position is ASIC staff guidance, not a legislative exemption—its withdrawal means ASIC can enforce from July 1 without statutory amendment. The key threshold question is whether a firm's products are "financial products" under the Corporations Act; ASIC's INFO 225 guidance classifies stablecoins, wrapped tokens, tokenized securities, and digital asset wallets broadly as financial products in most configurations. The Brief's read: Any firm that has assessed its products as "probably not financial products" and therefore not lodged an AFSL application should revisit that analysis before June 30. ASIC's guidance has been expansive, and the risk of being wrong is now enforcement-grade.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Australian exchanges (CoinJar, BTC Markets, Swyftx, Independent Reserve) are most affected—they have been operating under the no-action position and must have AFSL applications lodged by June 30. International exchanges with Australian user bases (Coinbase, Kraken, Bybit) must also have applications or demonstrate their products are not financial products under Australian law. Source: ASIC, Rubicon Law "2026 Crypto Reset" analysis, MCO compliance blog, June 2026.

China's RWA Tokenization Legal Regime Takes Shape: Eight Regulators Issue Notice Carving Offshore Tokenization Path, CSRC Filing System Opens

"The 2026 Notice establishes the principle that it is permissible for entities or individuals in China to conduct RWA tokenisation business directly or indirectly on an offshore basis, provided that the relevant consents, filings, and approvals have first been obtained from the relevant authority." [Paraphrased] — China PBOC and seven co-regulators, Notice on Further Preventing and Dealing with Cryptocurrencies and Related Risks (Notice No. 42 [2026]), February 6, 2026

On February 6, 2026, eight major Chinese financial regulators jointly issued Notice No. 42 [2026], which for the first time explicitly distinguishes real-world asset (RWA) tokenization from speculative cryptocurrency activity and creates a regulated offshore pathway for Chinese entities to tokenize domestic assets. The Notice permits RWA tokenization on an offshore basis where prior regulatory consents, filings, and approvals have been obtained from the relevant Chinese authority. The CSRC simultaneously issued Regulatory Guidelines on the Offshore Issuance of Asset-Backed Securities Tokens Backed by Onshore Assets, establishing a filing-based system.

The framework is notably bifurcated: onshore crypto trading and speculative cryptocurrency activity remain banned. But offshore tokenization of Chinese real assets—real estate, infrastructure, private credit—through regulated structures is now explicitly permitted. RWA tokens backed by onshore proprietary rights and offered offshore are regulated by CSRC, NDRC, and SAFE under their respective rules governing securitization or debt finance. The filing system requires strict compliance with cross-border investment, foreign exchange, and data security rules, and explicitly prohibits tokenization of assets linked to national security risks.

Significant practical details remain unresolved: the CSRC has not yet published required filing materials, submission channel details, review timelines, or ongoing reporting obligations. The framework is operationally incomplete, but the legal permission is now established.

📎 Historical Precedent: China's 2019 Blockchain Information Service Management Regulations established a similar pattern—a blanket permission for regulated applications of blockchain technology with an explicit carve-out for speculative use cases. The RWA framework follows this architecture with more granular regulatory routing.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The framework opens a significant new pipeline for Chinese asset owners to access offshore capital through tokenized securities structures. Hong Kong is the obvious initial beneficiary—its HKMA and SFC have been building RWA tokenization infrastructure and the new mainland framework explicitly envisions offshore issuance. Singapore's MAS-regulated markets are the second natural destination given the existing bilateral financial regulatory relationship. The Brief's read: The filing-based system at CSRC is likely to be slow initially, but Chinese institutional demand for RWA tokenization is substantial. Practitioners advising asset managers with Chinese LP bases should begin mapping the approval pathway now, even as procedural details are pending.

Colorado AI Act Gutted Before Effective Date: Governor Signs SB 189 Stripping Risk-Based Framework, Reflecting White House Preemption Pressure

"Colorado's revised AI law moves away from the original risk-based framework, eliminating the duty of care aimed at preventing algorithmic discrimination and deployer obligations to maintain risk management programs and conduct impact assessments." [Paraphrased] — Hunton & Williams privacy blog summary of SB 189, signed May 14, 2026

On May 14, 2026, Colorado Governor Jared Polis signed SB 189, effectively repealing and replacing the original Colorado Artificial Intelligence Act before it could take effect on June 30, 2026. The new law strips the original Act's most ambitious provisions: the duty of care to prevent algorithmic discrimination, deployer obligations to maintain risk management programs and conduct impact assessments, and reporting obligations to the Colorado AG. In their place, the revised law adopts a narrower transparency-and-disclosure model focused on automated decision-making technology (ADMT), with an effective date delayed to January 1, 2027.

The retreat is explicitly connected to White House pressure. The Trump administration's March 2026 AI governance blueprint directed the US Attorney General to establish an AI Litigation Task Force to challenge state AI laws as unconstitutional or preempted by federal authority. Colorado's Governor Polis, a Democrat, nonetheless aligned with the White House's substantive preference for a lighter-touch approach—describing the original Act as "the EU model" in public statements and indicating that a national framework should set the baseline rather than Colorado acting unilaterally.

The Colorado retreat has broader AI regulatory significance: it is the most significant rollback of a comprehensive US state AI law to date, and it signals that the White House preemption threat is functioning as intended, deterring states from implementing EU-style risk-based frameworks. The EU AI Act Omnibus (May 7, 2026 political agreement) has separately delayed high-risk rules to December 2027, reinforcing the global trajectory toward lighter-touch AI governance timelines.

📎 Historical Precedent: California's CCPA (2018) faced a comparable industry-driven rollback attempt via Proposition 24 (2020), which ultimately strengthened rather than weakened the law. The Colorado outcome differs because the challenge came from the executive branch rather than industry, and because the White House preemption threat had credible legal teeth under federal supremacy doctrine.

California's automated decision-making regulations (effective January 2027) remain on track but may face similar preemption scrutiny. For practitioners advising firms on US AI governance compliance, the priority has shifted from state-by-state risk assessment to monitoring the federal preemption framework and any forthcoming congressional AI legislation. The Brief's read: The US AI governance trajectory for 2026 is converging on disclosure and transparency requirements, not the EU-style impact-assessment and risk-tiering model. Firms that built compliance programs around the original Colorado framework should pivot to a federal-minimum disclosure model.
