Alpha — Iceberg Orders

Friday, 12 June 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #47 11 min read
TL;DR

An iceberg order is a large limit order that shows only a tiny visible tip while the bulk of the size sits hidden, auto-refilling at the same price each time the tip is eaten; it is the machinery behind absorption at a defended level. The signal that matters is not the size you can see — it is a price that refuses to move while far more volume transacts there than the book ever displayed.

§01
What It Is
The tip and the iceberg
An iceberg is a large hidden limit order that displays only a small slice at a time and silently refills at the same price until the whole thing is filled.

Treat the visible order book as a partial lie. The most important resting orders in any liquid market are the ones you cannot see — and an iceberg order is the cleanest example. The trader sets two numbers: a total size they actually want done, and a display size — the tiny fraction the exchange shows publicly. When that visible tip is filled, the matching engine instantly replenishes it from the hidden reserve, at the same price, until the full quantity is exhausted.

The point is concealment. A whale who flashed a 500-BTC bid would move the market against itself the instant it printed — front-runners stack ahead of it, sellers pull. So they show 6 BTC, refill it eighty times, and let aggressive sellers walk into a wall that never seems to thin out. This is not exotic: every serious venue offers it. On crypto exchanges it lives under names like iceberg, hidden, or reserve order; on CME and equities it is the same primitive.

Why a Wyckoff trader should care: an iceberg is the order-type expression of absorption. When a composite operator quietly accumulates in the lower half of a range, they are doing exactly this — taking the supply onto themselves without advertising demand. The iceberg is the mechanism; absorption is what you observe on the tape; a held range low is the result.

§02
Mechanics
Display size, refresh, fill-to-display
You detect an iceberg by the arithmetic: when volume transacted at one price dwarfs the size ever shown there, and the level holds, hidden reserve is doing the work.

Walk the cycle. (1) An aggressive seller market-sells into the bid and clears the visible 6 BTC. (2) The price level would normally drop a tick as the bid empties — but milliseconds later the same 6 BTC reappears at the same price. (3) More sells come; the tip is eaten and refills again. (4) Repeat until the reserve is empty. To anyone watching the tape, hundreds of BTC just transacted at one price while the displayed depth barely flickered.

The forensic signature is the fill-to-display ratio. If a level only ever shows 6 BTC but absorbs 480 BTC of aggressive selling without breaking, the ratio is 80:1 — no ordinary limit order behaves that way. Academic detection (Zotikov & Antonov on CME data) formalises this: track the discrepancy between displayed and executed volume, watch for refills arriving on a tight, regular cadence, then estimate the hidden total.

Fill-to-Display Ratio (FDR) = volume_executed_at_price ÷ average_displayed_size
FDR ≈ 1  →  ordinary limit order, fully visible
FDR ≫ 1 (e.g. 20–100×)  →  hidden reserve refilling — iceberg likely
Confirmation needs the level to hold: high FDR + stable price = genuine absorption. High FDR while price slides = the iceberg is being consumed, not defending.

One tell separates real hidden size from a fragile wall: depletion shape. Genuine displayed liquidity drains non-linearly — it accelerates as the level cracks and others pull. An iceberg drains linearly: steady, mechanical refills at a constant clip until the reserve is gone. That regularity is the fingerprint of an algorithm working a parent order, not a crowd.

§04
In Practice
Trading the defended level
Never trade the iceberg until the refill has proven itself twice and price has reclaimed — and put your stop where the reserve is exhausted, not at an arbitrary tick.
1

Read the volume, not the wall

A fat displayed bid is not an iceberg. The tell is a small tip absorbing many times its size and refilling. Watch fill-to-display, not depth — depth lies, transacted volume doesn't.

2

Wait for the second refill

One refill is noise. Two or three refills holding the same price while sells keep coming is a defended level. Enter with the hidden side after a reclaim, not on the first touch.

3

Respect the flip

The reserve is finite and the operator can stop refilling. When refills cease and price slices through, the level inverts into a vacuum — flip your bias immediately. The vanished bid is now overhead supply.

4

Stop beyond the reserve

Your invalidation is "the defence failed," not a fixed percentage. Place the stop a tick past where the iceberg was last seen holding — if price closes through it on volume, the thesis is dead.

Worked example — long off a defended base (illustrative)
SetupBTC re-accumulation, range $60K–$65K. A bid at $62.2K (S2) repeatedly absorbs aggressive selling and refills — high FDR, price holds.
TriggerTwo clean refills hold, then a 5m reclaim of $62.8K on rising delta. Enter long $62,800.
Stop$61,600 — a tick below where the iceberg last defended. Risk = $1,200 (≈1.9%).
Target 1$63,900 (range resistance / short-squeeze trigger). +$1,100 ≈ 0.9R — scale half.
Target 2$65,000 (range top). +$2,200 ≈ 1.8R — runner.
InvalidationRefills stop and $61.6K closes below on volume → the base is gone, exit and consider the flip.
Timeframe fit: detection is a seconds-to-minutes read off L2 / footprint data; the trade it informs is 5m–1h execution against a 4h+ range bias. Without order-by-order data you infer the iceberg from price holding + heavy volume, not from seeing it directly.

§07
Common Traps
Where iceberg-hunting goes wrong
The biggest mistake is treating a big visible wall as hidden size — an iceberg is a small tip that keeps coming back, not a fat order just sitting there.
1
Mistaking a static wall for an iceberg

A large displayed bid that just sits is not hidden size — and it's often the easiest thing to spoof. Hidden size is the small tip that refills as it's eaten. Judge by volume transacted versus displayed, never by displayed depth alone.

2
Front-running the first touch

One fill-and-refill proves nothing; algos refill once and pull. Demand at least two refills holding the level with sells still hitting before you commit. Entering on first contact is how you get run into the stop pool.

3
Forgetting the reserve runs out

Icebergs are real but finite. When refills stop and price knifes through, the floor you leaned on is gone and becomes overhead supply. If you don't flip — or at least exit — fast, the absorption trade becomes a falling-knife trade.

4
Confusing iceberg with spoof

Iceberg = legal hidden execution, trades actually print at the level. Spoof = fake orders cancelled before fill, no volume transacts. The single test: did size actually trade there? If yes, it's real demand; if it vanished untouched, it was theatre.

5
Overhyped: retail "iceberg detector" alerts on spot crypto

Most consumer iceberg indicators infer hidden size from aggregated trades or L1 quotes without true market-by-order data — so they fire constant false positives. Real detection needs MBO / footprint feeds, and crypto's fragmented venues split the hidden size across books. Treat a single-exchange "iceberg alert" as a low-signal prompt to go look, not a signal in itself.
