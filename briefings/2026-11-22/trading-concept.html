Alpha — Footprint Charts (Volume-at-Price)

Sunday, 22 November 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue 62 11 min read
TL;DR

A footprint chart opens each candle into a price ladder that records how many contracts traded at the bid and at the ask on every level, so you can see who forced each fill instead of only where the bar closed. It matters because the same green candle can be built on real buying or on sellers stepping aside, and the footprint separates the two. Practical takeaway: use it to confirm or reject a level you have already marked. Look for stacked imbalances and where the point of control sits, and never act on a single cell. It builds on the CVD issue of 20 November 2026, which covered the running version of the same aggression data.

§01
What It Is
Each candle, opened into who hit what
A footprint chart replaces each candle with a ladder of prices, printing the contracts that traded at the bid and at the ask on every level of the bar.

A footprint chart takes one candle and opens it into a ladder. Each row is a price the bar traded through, and each row carries two numbers: contracts that traded at the bid, where sellers hit resting buyers, and contracts that traded at the ask, where buyers lifted resting sellers. The candle still tells you where price went. The footprint tells you who was the aggressor on the way there.

Position first: the footprint is an execution record, not a forecast. A bar that closes higher on heavy selling aggression is a different event from a bar that closes higher on heavy buying, and an open-high-low-close candle cannot tell them apart. That places it in the same family as CVD. CVD adds the aggression numbers up over time, while the footprint shows where inside one bar that aggression landed. This builds on the CVD issue of 20 November 2026, so the running tally is not re-taught here.

The data requirement is strict. A footprint needs tick-level trades with the aggressor side recorded, not just candles. It only earns its keep on liquid contracts, because a price level with three prints in it tells you nothing about who was in control.

§02
Mechanics
Four steps from print to imbalance
It classifies every print as seller-initiated or buyer-initiated, sums both sides per price, then tests the ask at one level against the bid one tick lower.

The mechanic runs in four steps on every bar. First, classify each print: a trade that lands at the bid is seller-initiated, and a trade at the ask is buyer-initiated. Second, total both columns for each price. Third, derive delta per row and then per bar. Fourth, test the diagonal. Compare the ask at one price against the bid one tick lower, because that pair is the two sides of the same fight at adjacent levels. The output is a map of where one side kept winning and where it ran out of fuel.

Delta(p) = Ask(p) − Bid(p)
Positive means buyers were more aggressive at that price.
Bar delta = Σ Delta(p) across every level in the bar
The footer figure. It measures aggression, not direction.
Buy imbalance when Ask(p) ÷ Bid(p − 1 tick) ≥ 3.0
3.0 is the 300 percent default many platforms ship with. Tune between roughly 2.0 and 3.0, then hold the setting fixed while you test it.

Two checks matter more than any single cell. The point of control (POC) is the row with the most total volume, and price returning to it is a magnet worth noting, not a signal by itself. Second, stacked imbalances, several consecutive levels dominated by one side, mark sustained initiative. An isolated imbalance is usually noise.

§04
In Practice
CONFIRM LEVELS, DON'T FIND THEM
Judge a level by the aggression printed inside it, and require stacked imbalances before you give a footprint read any weight.
1

Read the diagonals, not the columns

Compare each ask against the bid one tick lower. A ratio that clears your fixed threshold on two adjacent levels is the signal. A large number sitting alone in one column tells you very little.

2

Mark zones, not single prints

A stacked imbalance becomes a zone you return to on a pullback. Price coming back into that zone while fresh aggression prints is the test, and a reaction without aggression is weaker evidence.

3

Start from a level you already marked

The footprint confirms or rejects a prior high, low, or value edge. It is poor at finding levels by itself, so keep the level-first order and let the bar answer the question you asked.

4

Judge absorption by price progress

Heavy volume at a level where price stops advancing is absorption. Heavy volume where price keeps running is initiative. The difference shows up in the response on the next bar, so wait for it.

§07
Common Traps
ONE CELL IS NOT A SIGNAL
Treating one big number in one column as a signal, when it only means something when it lines up with its neighbours and with price response.
1
Trading one cell as a signal

A single large ask number says little on its own. Require the diagonal ratio to clear your threshold on adjacent levels, and require a stacked run before you give the bar weight.

2
Reading delta as direction

Bar delta measures who was aggressive, not where price goes next. A bar can close higher on negative delta, and that mismatch is the thing to investigate, not a reason to flip your bias.

3
Tuning the ratio after the trade

Picking a 2.2 or 2.7 threshold because it flagged your winner is curve-fitting. Set one ratio per contract, keep it fixed, and judge it across a full sample.

4
Ignoring the feed and the session

Footprints depend on tick-level data, and thin contracts or quiet hours produce bars where a few prints look like a story. Check the depth before you trust a row.

!
Overhyped: footprint signal packs

Many vendors sell footprint indicators with buy and sell arrows. The chart measures trades that already happened and forecasts nothing. Treat any arrow that claims to predict the next bar as marketing, and test it on your own sample before paying for it.
