Section 01
Longevity & Performance Science

The most useful shift this cycle is in how aging risk is being read. Instead of relying on one clinical marker, the LonGenity group screened 4,265 plasma proteins in 1,025 older adults and tied the mortality-linked ones to chronic disease burden and to biological age, at both whole-body and organ level. The proteins were then re-tested in 1,124 BLSA participants, which is the step that separates a signature from a one-cohort association. The mechanistic bet is that a protein panel can show which organ system is ageing fastest, something a single clock score cannot do. A companion review in Aging (Vol 18, October 2026) argues the same direction: age-related dysfunction is unlikely to come from one universal mechanism.

Practical takeaway: proteomic panels are research tools, not clinic tests. The useful transferable idea is to track several independent markers over time rather than rely on one number. For Tony, that means pairing your existing biomarker log with a second, outcome-linked marker rather than swapping in a single new test.

No intervention has yet extended human lifespanExpert · Narrative Review

The Arclight survey (Issue 02, November 2026) ranks ten longevity directions by how well they hold up. Rapamycin is rated the most repeatable in mice, with a 10–20% lifespan extension reported through the NIA program, yet the survey notes that no human lifespan extension has been demonstrated for any intervention. Its ranking emphasises repeatability over mechanism, which is the right lens for a practitioner: a mouse result is a reason to measure, not a reason to dose.

Aging as several interacting systems, not one pathwayExpert · Narrative Review

A review in Aging (Vol 18, 9 October 2026) frames epigenetic regulation as a candidate lifespan-limiting centre within a wider polycentric model. Its practical consequence is that single-target interventions are unlikely to explain the whole trajectory, which supports tracking multiple biomarkers across systems rather than optimising one number.

Section 02
Training & Performance
Load and frequency move strength; volume moves sizeMeta · Overview of Reviews

The 2026 ACSM position stand, an overview of reviews published in Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise (58(4):851–872), concludes that resistance training load and weekly frequency change strength gains but not hypertrophy. Set volume shifts both strength and hypertrophy. For a PPL split, that places the main hypertrophy decision in how many working sets each muscle gets per week.

Source: Currier BS et al., ACSM Position Stand, 2026
Session frequency adds little once volume is matchedPreprint · Re-analysis

A re-analysis of volume-equated trials covering one to three sessions per muscle per week finds a frequency coefficient consistent with a negligible effect on hypertrophy. The authors add that the evidence at equated volume is mixed, so this is a provisional result, not a settled one. The practical read is that splitting the same weekly sets across more sessions is a scheduling choice, not a growth lever.

Source: medRxiv preprint, Oct 2026
Integrate now
  • Program weekly sets per muscle in the 12–20 range, which a 2026 narrative review calls a defensible starting band.
  • Use moderate loads of roughly 8–12 reps for most working sets, as suggested in a Jyväskylä conference summary.
  • Keep at least 60 s between sets, and 2 min on multijoint lifts.
Caveats
  • The rest and proximity-to-failure figures come from a conference summary, not a primary trial.
  • The 12–20 range is a narrative-review pooling, not a single RCT.
  • Frequency findings rest on a preprint that has not passed peer review.
Section 03
Supplements & Nutrition
NAC moves toward a supplement label under FDA reviewRegulatory

On 16 November the FDA announced a proposed rule to reverse the exclusion of N-acetyl-L-cysteine from the dietary supplement definition, with publication in the Federal Register scheduled for 17 November. If finalised, NAC products could be sold as supplements provided they meet the usual FD&C Act obligations. This is a regulatory status change, not an efficacy finding. Practical takeaway: check the label and dose, and do not treat the new status as evidence that NAC improves any specific outcome.

Source: FDA proposed rule, 16–17 Nov 2026
No supplement yet shown to extend human lifespanExpert · Review

An evidence review published in November 2026 states that no dietary supplement has been proven to slow human ageing or extend lifespan. It places vitamin D, B12, iron and omega-3 in a narrower role, justified mainly by a confirmed deficiency or a specific medical need such as high triglycerides. Mechanistically, the review gives no pathway-level case for a general longevity stack.

Source: InCare, Nov 2026
Evidence scores: broad coverage, few strong ratingsAggregator · Not a trial

An evidence-scoring aggregator reports that 58% of the supplements it lists have at least one meta-analysis, yet only 12 clear both a high evidence score and a high safety rating. Whey protein, probiotics and omega-3 appear among that group. The tool is a secondary scoring system rather than a primary study, so use it to prioritise reading, not to settle a dose question.

Source: Supstack evidence database, 2026
