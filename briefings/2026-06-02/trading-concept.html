Alpha — Stacked Imbalances

Tuesday, 2 June 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #39 11 min read
TL;DR

Stacked imbalances are three or more consecutive diagonal bid/ask imbalances on a footprint chart — adjacent price levels where one side traded far more aggressively than the other; they matter because they pin down exactly where initiative buying or selling actually happened, and those zones tend to hold as support or resistance on the retest. The practical takeaway: treat a stack as a high-probability location to defend or fade, but pull the trigger only with confirmation — a bounce, an absorption print, or a delta flip — never on the stack alone.

§01
What It Is
Plain-English definition
A stack is three-plus footprint imbalances piled at adjacent prices on the same side — a fingerprint of sustained aggression that the market tends to respect when price comes back.

Open a footprint chart and every candle becomes a ladder: at each price level you see how much volume hit the bid (aggressive selling) versus how much lifted the ask (aggressive buying). An imbalance is one diagonal side dwarfing the other — typically 3× or more. A single imbalance is noise. A stacked imbalance is three or more of them piled at consecutive price levels, all pointing the same way.

That stacking is the whole point. One aggressive print can be an accident — a market order from someone who needed out. But buyers (or sellers) overwhelming the other side at four prices in a row is not an accident; it is sustained initiative flow. Those levels become a shelf of demand or supply, and the traders who built it tend to defend it when price returns. Stacked buy imbalances mark a support shelf; stacked sell imbalances mark a resistance shelf.

This is a discretionary footprint read, not a quantitative microstructure metric — it is closer in spirit to reading the tape than to computing Order Flow Imbalance. Its strength is precision of location; its weakness is that it tells you nothing about who was aggressive or why.

§02
Mechanics
How the signal forms
Imbalance is measured diagonally — ask volume against the bid one tick below — because the best bid and best ask are always at least a tick apart; cross a ratio threshold at three consecutive levels and you have a stack.

The non-obvious part is the diagonal comparison. You do not compare bid and ask at the same price — the best bid and best offer never sit on the same tick, so a vertical comparison would just double-count the spread. Instead the footprint compares ask volume at a level against bid volume one tick below (a buy imbalance), and bid volume at a level against ask volume one tick above (a sell imbalance). It is asking: at this price, did aggressive buyers lifting the offer overwhelm the sellers willing to be hit just beneath them?

Buy (ask) imbalance @ P:   Ask_Vol(P) ÷ Bid_Vol(P − 1 tick) ≥ R
Sell (bid) imbalance @ P:  Bid_Vol(P) ÷ Ask_Vol(P + 1 tick) ≥ R
Stack:                  ≥ 3 consecutive same-side imbalances
Ratio R:             3 (default) · 4 (strict) · 5 (BTC / ES filter)
Diagonal because best bid and best ask are ≥ 1 tick apart — a vertical same-level comparison would measure the spread, not aggression.

The ratio threshold R is the one input that matters and the one beginners leave wrong. TradingView's footprint defaults to 300% (3:1) and highlights buy imbalances to the right of the level, sell imbalances to the left. ATAS treats a four-imbalance stack as a level the buyers or sellers control; many crypto and futures desks run 4:1 or 5:1 to thin out the noise in liquid books. [Sources differ] on the exact threshold — there is no canonical number, only the right number for your instrument and timeframe.

§04
In Practice
Trading the zone
A stack is a location, not a trigger — mark the shelf, then wait for the market to show its hand on the retest before you commit a cent.
1

Location, not trigger

The stack tells you where; a confirmation tells you when. Wait for a bounce, a fresh absorption print, or a delta flip at the zone before entering. Acting on the stack alone is front-running a level that may never get defended.

2

Tune the ratio to the instrument

3:1 floods a liquid book with imbalances; in BTC perps or ES, bump R to 4:1 or 5:1 so only the meaningful stacks survive. Re-check the setting when volatility regime changes — a quiet-session threshold lies to you in a fast tape.

3

Trade with the auction, fade against it

In a trend, stacks in the trend direction are continuation fuel — buy the demand shelf on the pullback. In balance, fade stacks at the range edges back toward value. Context decides whether a shelf is a launchpad or a wall.

4

Watch the flip

An unfilled stack that price slices straight through traps the aggressors who built it. The broken support shelf becomes resistance, and the trapped-longs unwind is often a cleaner, faster trade than the bounce you were waiting for.

Worked example — BTC demand-shelf retest
SetupUp-leg prints a 4-level stacked buy imbalance at 71,460–71,520. Price rallies, then pulls back to the shelf.
TriggerOn the retest, delta turns positive and a fresh buy imbalance prints at 71,480 (sellers absorbed).

Stop71,400 — below the whole stack + buffer · risk = 80 pts

§07
Common Traps
Where it goes wrong
The biggest mistake is treating a stack as an entry signal — it is a location, and trading a location without confirmation is how good levels lose you money.
1
Treating every imbalance as a level

A single imbalance is noise — one impatient market order. Only stacks of three or more at a structurally meaningful price are worth marking. Drawing a line at every lone imbalance turns your chart into confetti.

2
Leaving the ratio on default

3:1 is fine for a thin instrument and useless in a deep one, where it paints imbalances on half the levels. Match R to the book — 4:1 or 5:1 in BTC and ES — or you are reading static.

3
Entering blind, with no trigger

The shelf marks where aggression happened, not whether it will repeat. Demand a bounce, an absorption print, or a delta divergence on the retest. No confirmation, no trade — the level is context, not a buy button.

4
Ignoring the flip

When price knifes through a buy stack, the buyers who built it are trapped and the shelf inverts to resistance. Traders who keep buying "support" into a clean break are adding to the very fuel that drives the next leg down.

5
Overhyped: "stacked imbalances are institutional footprints you can follow"

Half marketing. A stack shows aggression, not who or intent — and in a thin crypto book a single retail market sweep can stack four imbalances with no institution anywhere near it. It is a context tool, not an X-ray of smart money. Treat it as evidence of force, then verify with structure and confirmation.
