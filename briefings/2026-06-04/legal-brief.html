House of Lords Tells the Bank of England to Drop Its £20k Stablecoin Cap Days Before the Bank Finalises Its Systemic Regime

"Rather than pre-emptively impose holding limits, the Bank should consider monitoring the growth of the market and imposing holding limits only if the financial stability risks clearly warrant it." House of Lords Financial Services Regulation Committee — "Stablecoins: waiting for regulation", 2 June 2026

A cross-party House of Lords committee told the Bank of England on 2 June to tear up the core of its proposed systemic-stablecoin regime. In a report bluntly titled Stablecoins: waiting for regulation, the Financial Services Regulation Committee said the Bank's planned holding caps — £20,000 per coin for individuals and £10 million for businesses — should be dropped in favour of monitoring market growth and intervening only if financial-stability risk materialises.

The committee also took aim at the Bank's reserve-backing model, which would require issuers of systemic sterling stablecoins to hold at least 40% of backing in unremunerated deposits at the Bank itself. Peers argued the no-yield reserve requirement and the holding caps together would smother a UK stablecoin market before it forms, pushing activity to dollar tokens and overseas venues.

The rebuke lands at an awkward moment. Deputy Governor Sarah Breeden already conceded last month that the proposals were "overly conservative," and the Bank has signalled its final policy and draft rules for systemic stablecoins will follow this summer, with the framework targeted for completion by year-end. The Lords report is the loudest political signal yet that those rules must loosen before they bind.

📎 Historical Precedent: Echoes the 2023–24 fight over the Bank's earlier discussion paper, where industry warned that a deposit-style cap would make sterling stablecoins commercially unviable — the same objection now carried by Parliament's upper chamber.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The Bank faces a credibility squeeze: a sitting deputy governor has called its own numbers "overly conservative," and a parliamentary committee has now put that critique on the record before the draft Code of Practice is published. Expect the Bank to relax the £20k individual cap (or convert it to a soft monitoring threshold) and to revisit the 40% unremunerated-reserve floor — though it is unlikely to abandon caps entirely given its stated deposit-substitution and run-risk concerns. The Brief's read: the holding limit survives in diluted form; the reserve-yield rule is the more likely concession, because that is where the UK is most visibly out of step with the US GENIUS Act and the dollar-stablecoin market it is competing against.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The committee report itself flags the counter-case: the Bank's mandate is financial stability, not market promotion, and Breeden has defended the caps as a way to "manage what we think is an important risk as stablecoins come into play." If a sterling systemic stablecoin scales fast and then breaks, the Bank — not the Lords — owns the cleanup, which is why a precautionary cap may be the rational choice even at the cost of slower adoption. The Brief's read: the stability case is real, but a permanent pre-emptive cap is a blunt instrument; calibrated, reviewable limits would address run-risk without freezing the market.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Per the committee's own framing, the rules as drafted disadvantage would-be sterling issuers and UK-based distributors relative to dollar incumbents. A loosening helps prospective GBP issuers and the banks and PSPs weighing tokenised-money products; Circle and other USD issuers benefit either way, since restrictive UK rules entrench dollar tokens as the default. The Brief's read: watch UK-licensed e-money firms and the large clearing banks (Lloyds, NatWest, Barclays) — a workable reserve-yield model is the difference between them building sterling stablecoins and ceding the rail to USDC.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish Sterling-stablecoin aspirants and UK bank tokenisation desks (LLOY.L, NWG.L, BARC.L) if caps/reserve rules ease; Circle (CRCL) regardless, as USD tokens stay the path of least resistance.

🔴 Bearish UK-domiciled issuers if the Bank holds the £20k cap and 40% unremunerated floor — capital migrates to dollar tokens and EU/US venues.

⚪ Neutral Tether (USDT) — UK retail caps barely touch a wholesale-and-offshore franchise; the GENIUS Act, not the BoE, is its binding constraint.

SEC's Draft 2026–2030 Strategic Plan Centres Crypto and Codifies the Retreat From Regulation-by-Enforcement

"Crypto asset technologies have the potential to revolutionize America's financial infrastructure and deliver new optionality, efficiencies, cost reductions, transparency, and risk mitigation for the benefit of all Americans." SEC Draft Strategic Plan for Fiscal Years 2026–2030, published 2 June 2026

The SEC published its Draft Strategic Plan for fiscal years 2026 through 2030 on 2 June, and digital assets sit at the centre of it. Objective 1.1 commits the agency to building "a firm regulatory foundation for digital assets and distributed ledger technologies through a rational, coherent, and principled approach" — language that elevates crypto from an enforcement target to a strategic priority of the Commission under Chair Paul Atkins.

The plan also formalises the enforcement turn that has played out case-by-case over the past year. It directs staff to focus on "fraud and manipulation" and to measure enforcement success "by deterrence and market clarity, not by case volume or fine totals" — an explicit repudiation of the prior regulation-by-enforcement posture. Atkins framed the release as "a new day at the SEC," while insisting the Commission "will not stray" from its statutory mandate of investor protection, fair markets, and capital formation. The plan is open for public comment through 2 July 2026.

📎 Historical Precedent: The last comparable strategic reorientation was the post-2008 Dodd-Frank build-out, which expanded the SEC's enforcement footprint; this plan runs the tape in reverse, narrowing enforcement and widening market-facilitation — the most explicit deregulatory mission statement since the agency's modern era.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

A strategic plan is not a rule, but it is the document that drives examination priorities, enforcement triage, and rulemaking sequencing for four years — so its signalling value is high. Read alongside the March 2026 SEC-CFTC joint interpretation and the five-category token taxonomy (Release 33-11412), it tells registrants the agency will pursue clarity through rulemaking and the Project Crypto agenda, not surprise actions. The Brief's read: the practical near-term payoff is fewer novel enforcement theories and faster movement on tokenised-securities and custody rulemaking; the risk is that a draft plan can be softened in the comment window or unwound by a future Commission.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Investor-protection advocates argue that measuring enforcement by "deterrence" rather than case volume is a euphemism for doing less, and that centring crypto in a five-year plan locks in a pro-industry tilt that outlasts the current Chair. The counter, voiced across the comment-file in prior dockets, is that a fraud-and-manipulation focus is the SEC's traditional core, not a retreat from it. The Brief's read: the honest tension is real — narrower enforcement plus faster rulemaking only protects investors if the rules actually land; if Project Crypto rulemakings stall, the plan becomes deregulation without the replacement guardrails.

🏗️ Industry Implications

The clearest beneficiaries are US-listed crypto-exposed issuers and registrants that spent 2023–24 in litigation — Coinbase (COIN), Kraken, and the spot-ETF complex — plus tokenisation infrastructure tied to the SEC's pending innovation-exemption work (Bloomberg reported the tokenised-stock framework was being prepared in May). The Brief's read: the plan is a green light for capital formation on-chain, but the value accrues to whoever clears the rulemakings first; exchanges (Nasdaq, NYSE/ICE, Cboe) are positioning for tokenised-equity venues, and the firm with the cleanest regulatory path wins the rail.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish COIN, CRCL and the US tokenisation/ETF complex — a four-year strategic commitment lowers regulatory tail-risk and accelerates on-chain capital formation.

🔴 Bearish Plaintiff-bar and enforcement-driven catalysts; firms relying on legal ambiguity for moat lose it as clarity arrives.

⚪ Neutral Non-US venues — the plan is domestic; offshore platforms neither gain nor lose directly, though US clarity raises the global compliance bar.

South African High Court Rules Bitcoin Is "Capital" Under Exchange-Control Law — Contradicting a 2025 Decision on Appeal

Bitcoin is "a financial asset that is capable of holding value and being used as a medium of exchange." High Court of South Africa, Gauteng Division — Mangundhla & Dangaiso v South African Reserve Bank, 1 June 2026

On 1 June, the Gauteng Division of the High Court held in Mangundhla & Dangaiso v South African Reserve Bank that bitcoin qualifies as "capital" under Exchange Control Regulation 10(1)(c), reasoning that it is a financial asset capable of holding value and serving as a medium of exchange. The court reached the conclusion by applying ordinary statutory interpretation — grammatical meaning, context and purpose — to a provision drafted decades before crypto existed.

The ruling directly contradicts the 2025 Standard Bank v SARB decision, which the Reserve Bank has appealed to the Supreme Court of Appeal, where judgment is still pending. The result is genuine uncertainty over whether crypto outflows are caught by South Africa's exchange-control regime — a live question precisely as the National Treasury and SARB consult on draft Capital Flow Management Regulations that aim to fold crypto assets into exchange-control law (comment open to 30 June).

📎 Historical Precedent: Mirrors early UK and Singapore property-status crypto cases (AA v Persons Unknown; ByBit v Ho) where courts retrofitted pre-digital legal categories onto crypto — here the category is exchange-control "capital," with cross-border-payment consequences.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Two High Court divisions now point in opposite directions, so the SCA's pending Standard Bank ruling becomes the decisive authority — until then, any opinion on the exchange-control treatment of crypto outflows from South Africa must carry an express conflict-of-authority caveat (per the Bowmans analysis of the judgment). The Brief's read: if "capital" classification stands, crypto cross-border transfers fall under SARB approval requirements, materially raising friction for South African exchanges and offshore-bound users; the draft Capital Flow Management Regulations are the Treasury's attempt to pre-empt the courts and settle the question by rule.

🏗️ Industry Implications

South African exchanges (Luno, VALR) and payment intermediaries face the prospect of exchange-control compliance overlays on cross-border crypto flows if the "capital" characterisation prevails. The Brief's read: this is a watch-item, not yet an operating change — the SCA appeal and the 30 June consultation will determine the rule of the road; firms should map which products touch cross-border value transfer and model a SARB-approval pathway as a contingency.

🟡 Medium — enacted but delayed; effective 1 Jan 2027, narrowed scope

Colorado Guts and Delays Its Landmark AI Act to 2027 as Illinois Tightens — the US State AI Patchwork Splits in Two

The federal order takes specific aim at the Colorado law, claiming it would "force AI models to produce false results" through its algorithmic-discrimination duties. White House Executive Order, "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence", 11 Dec 2025 [paraphrased framing; quoted phrase verbatim]

Colorado, home to the first comprehensive US state AI law, has hollowed it out. Governor Polis signed SB 189 on 14 May 2026, pushing the AI Act's effective date from 30 June 2026 to 1 January 2027 and replacing its risk-based architecture — the duty of care against algorithmic discrimination, deployer risk-management programs, impact assessments and AG reporting — with a narrower disclosure-and-transparency regime around automated decision-making.

The retreat runs directly opposite to Illinois, which in late May cleared SB 315 to mandate independent third-party safety audits of frontier labs. With Washington pressing federal preemption (the 11 December executive order, the DOJ AI Litigation Task Force, and the March National Policy Framework), the US is no longer drifting toward a single AI standard — the state map is actively diverging, leaving multistate deployers to navigate opposite obligations in neighbouring jurisdictions.

📎 Historical Precedent: Recalls the pre-CCPA state privacy scramble, where a patchwork of divergent state regimes (and a stalled federal bill) forced multistate firms to comply with the strictest common denominator — the same dynamic now forming around AI.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Hunton and King & Spalding client analyses, Colorado's delay buys deployers seven months but does not resolve direction — the narrowed statute still imposes disclosure duties from January 2027, and the federal preemption push remains aspirational until Congress acts, so existing state laws stand for now. The Brief's read: compliance teams should plan to the strictest applicable regime (Illinois-style audits, California SB 53 frontier-transparency) rather than betting on Colorado's lighter touch or on federal preemption arriving in time; the patchwork, not convergence, is the 2026–27 base case.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Google) face the inverse of relief: as Colorado eases, Illinois and California raise the bar, so the binding constraint shifts to the toughest state, not the average. The Brief's read: enterprise AI vendors selling into employment, housing, lending and healthcare use-cases should treat algorithmic-discrimination controls as a national baseline regardless of Colorado's rollback — the litigation and reputational exposure travels across state lines even where the statute does not.

Tether's USDT Still Has No Treasury Equivalency as the GENIUS Act Foreign-Issuer Clock Runs Toward July 18

"We expect the US to apply reciprocity to Tether International." Tether — on its GENIUS Act foreign-issuer (Article 18 reciprocity) pathway

The world's largest stablecoin remains, on paper, on the wrong side of US law. As of late May, Treasury had not issued the equivalency determination that USDT — a foreign-issued token — needs under the GENIUS Act's Article 18 reciprocity clause to serve US persons legally. Tether's answer has been a dual-track structure: USAT, a US-domiciled, federally-chartered, Washington-custodied stablecoin launched earlier in 2026, carries the domestic-compliance load while USDT keeps running globally on the bet that reciprocity arrives.

The clock is concrete. The next tranche of GENIUS Act implementing rules — issuer licensing, AML and capital standards for permitted payment stablecoin issuers (PPSIs) — takes effect 18 July 2026, and the FinCEN/OFAC AML and sanctions-compliance rule for PPSIs closes for comment on 9 June. Without an equivalency determination, USDT's lawful US access narrows even as USAT scales.

📎 Historical Precedent: Parallels the post-MiCA EU squeeze on USDT in 2024–25, when exchanges delisted non-compliant EMTs — Tether again opts for a compliant sibling token plus a reciprocity argument rather than direct registration.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per TRM Labs' analysis of the PPSI rule, foreign issuers without an equivalency determination occupy a grey zone: not banned outright, but exposed if US-facing distribution continues past the implementing deadlines. The Brief's read: Tether is hedging correctly — USAT gives it a clean domestic vehicle while it lobbies for reciprocity — but the reciprocity clause is discretionary, not automatic, and Treasury has every incentive to move slowly; counsel advising US venues should treat USDT's domestic status as unresolved and USAT as the compliant default for US-customer products.

🏗️ Industry Implications

US exchanges and PSPs face a near-term routing decision: list/settle in USAT or Circle's USDC for US customers, keep USDT for offshore flow. The Brief's read: the equivalency gap is a structural tailwind for Circle (CRCL) in the US market and a forcing function for Tether's USAT distribution — the firm that locks in US exchange and PSP integrations during this window captures the domestic settlement rail while USDT's status stays ambiguous.
