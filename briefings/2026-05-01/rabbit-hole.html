Rabbit Hole · 1 May 2026
Ideas · History

The CIA secretly spent millions promoting Jackson Pollock and abstract expressionism across Cold War Europe — using fake foundations, dummy patrons, and a spy who had just left MoMA — without any of the artists ever knowing. The artists being promoted were often left-wing and would have been furious; the operation was exposed in 1967 and raises a question that has never been fully resolved: did the CIA help invent the modern art canon?

TL;DR

During the 1950s, while McCarthyism was publicly purging left-wing artists from American life, the CIA was quietly spending millions to tour their paintings across Europe. Jackson Pollock's chaotic drip canvases and Mark Rothko's vast colour fields were presented to foreign audiences as proof of American freedom — the antithesis of Soviet socialist realism, which was rigid, figurative, and state-dictated. The funding was routed through a web of fake foundations and dummy patrons, and the artists — many of whom had Communist Party sympathies or would have despised the CIA — never knew. The operation was directed partly from MoMA, administered through a Paris-based front organisation called the Congress for Cultural Freedom, and exposed by a journalist in 1967. It raises questions about the art historical canon that remain genuinely unsettled: was abstract expressionism a great movement, or was it in part a manufactured triumph?

Section 01

The Story: How a Spy Agency Decided to Fight Communism with Paint

By 1948, American intelligence officials had concluded that they were losing the cultural Cold War. The Soviet Union had an enormous propaganda apparatus aimed at European intellectuals: Communist Party journals in every major language, funded youth movements, controlled trade unions, and a steady supply of visiting artists and writers who could testify that the USSR represented humanity's progressive future. The United States had nothing comparable. Congressional attitudes toward government-funded culture were actively hostile — the House Un-American Activities Committee was looking for communists in Hollywood, not trying to counter Soviet messaging in Paris.

The solution was to do it secretly. In 1950, the CIA established the International Organisations Division under Tom Braden, a former officer who had most recently served as executive secretary of the Museum of Modern Art in New York. From 1950 until the programme's exposure in 1967, the IOD quietly funded a global cultural operation designed to convince European — especially French — intellectuals that American democracy was compatible with serious art, genuine freedom, and intellectual ambition. Soviet propaganda depicted the United States as a cultureless commercial wasteland; the CIA intended to refute that claim with evidence.

The centrepiece was the Congress for Cultural Freedom, founded at a conference in West Berlin in June 1950 — a location chosen for maximum symbolic impact, given that the Soviets had blockaded the city just two years earlier. At its height, the CCF was active in 35 countries, ran offices in dozens of cities, published more than twenty prestigious literary and intellectual magazines, organised international conferences, and funded concerts, art exhibitions, and academic fellowships. It was, by any measure, a substantial cultural institution. Almost all of its funding came covertly from the CIA.

"Abstract Expressionism was the kind of art that made Socialist Realism look even more stylized and more rigid and confined than it was."

— Tom Braden, CIA International Organisations Division chief, speaking years after the programme's exposure

The choice of abstract expressionism as the vehicle was deliberate and shrewd. Soviet socialist realism was figurative, monumental, and state-directed — it showed heroic workers, noble leaders, and clear ideological messages. The new American painting was its polar opposite: non-figurative, gestural, apparently chaotic, and emphatically individual. Pollock dripped and splattered. Rothko built vast trembling fields of colour. De Kooning slashed and reworked. Franz Kline painted enormous black calligraphic strokes. None of it could be conscripted into state messaging. It looked, to a European eye trained on Soviet output, like the product of a society that left its artists genuinely alone.

Nelson Rockefeller, who governed MoMA and had deep ties to both the State Department and the CIA's cultural network, reportedly called abstract expressionism "Free Enterprise Painting." The phrase was not ironic. MoMA — controlled by Rockefeller money and socially connected to the State Department — became, in effect, the CIA's exhibition curating arm. The museum was contracted to organise and present the major travelling shows that the Congress for Cultural Freedom sponsored abroad.

1948–1949 — Tom Braden at MoMA
Before joining the CIA, Braden serves as MoMA's executive secretary under Nelson Rockefeller, building the institutional relationships that will later make MoMA the unofficial curatorial partner of the CIA's cultural programme.
1950 — Congress for Cultural Freedom founded, West Berlin
The CCF is launched at a conference attended by Bertrand Russell, Arthur Koestler, and John Dewey. It presents itself as an independent intellectual organisation defending Western liberalism against Soviet totalitarianism. CIA funding is concealed from all but its top administrators.
1952 — Festival of Twentieth-Century Masterworks, Paris
The CCF sponsors a major Paris cultural festival, establishing American arts as central to the Western tradition alongside European modernism. Boston Symphony Orchestra. Stravinsky. Abstract canvases from New York.
1958–59 — "The New American Painting" tours Europe
A landmark exhibition of abstract expressionism — Pollock, Rothko, de Kooning, Kline, Motherwell — visits eight European countries. When the Tate Gallery in London cannot afford the show, the Farfield Foundation (a CIA front) quietly provides the funds.
1967 — Exposure
Ramparts magazine and then the New York Times reveal CIA funding of the National Student Association. Subsequent reporting extends to the CCF. Encounter magazine's editors Stephen Spender and Frank Kermode resign on learning their publication was a CIA vehicle. The CCF is renamed and ostensibly defunded.

The irony was enormous and was not lost on those who noticed it. The very artists being promoted as symbols of American freedom were often the most politically suspicious of the American state. Pollock had been a Communist Party member in the 1930s. Rothko was a Jewish immigrant from Latvia who had retained significant left-wing sympathies. De Kooning was a Dutch immigrant who had lived through the Depression with socialist views. Had they been told that the CIA was spending money to send their paintings around Europe as weapons in an anticommunist psychological operation, their reactions would, in all probability, have ranged from outrage to fury.

Section 02

The Mechanism: Fake Foundations, Long Leashes, and the Problem of Plausible Deniability

The money could not simply flow from the CIA's budget to an art exhibition. Congress had not authorised it, and even in the early Cold War, the discovery that the government was secretly funding art would have been explosive — particularly given that McCarthyism had made "government involvement in culture" synonymous with subversion in the popular imagination. The solution was a system of what intelligence officers called "cut-outs": intermediary organisations that handled the money at one or more removes from Langley.

How the Money Moved

Step 1 — CIA to shell foundations. The Agency channelled funds through a network of apparently independent philanthropic foundations. Key vehicles included the Farfield Foundation, the Hoblitzelle Foundation, and several others. These organisations had real boards and real staff, but their primary function was to move CIA money into legitimate cultural projects without leaving Agency fingerprints.

Step 2 — Foundations to the CCF. The Congress for Cultural Freedom received its operating budget from these foundations, along with some genuine private donations that served as additional cover. CCF staff — most of whom did not know the source of their funding — administered the magazines, conferences, and exhibitions in good faith.

Step 3 — CCF to exhibitions and magazines. The CCF funded individual projects: the Encounter magazine editorial budget, the cost of shipping paintings to European venues, the fees for concerts. At this point the money was entirely untraceable to the CIA.

The "long leash" policy. CIA officers deliberately stayed two or three degrees removed from the artists and cultural figures being promoted. The goal was that no artist, editor, or exhibition curator could ever truthfully say they had knowingly worked for the CIA. Most of them hadn't.

The system worked for seventeen years. It was elegant in its simplicity: the CIA didn't need to tell Pollock he was a Cold War asset. It just needed to ensure that his paintings appeared in the right European cities at the right moments, with the right critical framing. The Farfield Foundation found the money for the Tate when the Tate said it couldn't afford "The New American Painting" in 1959. Nobody at the Tate needed to know why a small American foundation was suddenly interested in their budget shortfall.

The CCF's cultural output was genuinely impressive and not entirely dishonest. Encounter magazine — co-founded by the poet Stephen Spender and the political writer Irving Kristol, later godfather of neoconservatism — published Bertrand Russell, Isaiah Berlin, Arthur Koestler, and a roster of serious European and American intellectuals. The intellectual content was real. The editorial independence was substantially real. The magazine's CIA funding was the one thing that was concealed, and it was concealed successfully enough that Spender, who was one of its founders, did not know until 1967. He resigned immediately when he found out.

The exposure came not through the art side of the operation but through a parallel programme funding the National Student Association. In February 1967, Ramparts magazine revealed that the NSA had been receiving CIA money to steer American student delegates at international conferences toward anti-Soviet positions. The story cascaded. Within weeks, journalists had traced the same funding architecture — shell foundations, cut-out organisations — to the CCF. On 14 February 1967, the New York Times confirmed the connection. The Saturday Evening Post ran a detailed account of the Encounter funding. The edifice collapsed almost immediately.

Section 03

The Connections: Did the CIA Help Write the Art History Books?

The most uncomfortable question raised by the CIA's cultural programme is not whether it happened — it is documented — but whether it worked, and what it means that it did. Abstract expressionism is now taught in every art history curriculum as one of the defining movements of the twentieth century: the moment when the centre of gravity of the Western art world shifted from Paris to New York, and when American art achieved genuine global prestige for the first time. Did the CIA contribute to that outcome?

The Case That It Mattered

  • The CIA funded at least three major European touring exhibitions of abstract expressionism in the 1950s, providing crucial international visibility at the exact moment the movement was establishing its reputation.
  • European critical reception of American abstract art was significantly shaped by which shows appeared, when, and with what institutional backing — all of which the CIA indirectly controlled.
  • The Tate Gallery's acquisition of "The New American Painting" — one of the most influential shows in British art history — was financially enabled by Farfield Foundation money.
  • Encounter magazine helped build the intellectual framework in which abstract expressionism was discussed and legitimised among the European intelligentsia.

The Case That the Art Stood On Its Own

  • The CIA promoted existing artists — it did not create them. Pollock, Rothko, and de Kooning were already producing major work before any CIA money touched the enterprise.
  • European critics who championed American abstract art — including the influential French critic Michel Tapie — developed their views independently.
  • The movement's dominance persisted long after the CIA programme was exposed and disbanded, suggesting it had genuine artistic substance that didn't depend on covert promotion.
  • The CIA was one promoter among many — MoMA, private galleries, and commercial dealers were simultaneously building the movement's reputation through entirely normal channels.

The honest answer is probably: both things are true, and the coexistence is uncomfortable. Abstract expressionism had real artistic merit and would have achieved significant recognition without CIA assistance. But the specific shape of its international prestige — the timing, the European venues, the critical framing — was partially engineered by an intelligence agency that had its own reasons for wanting that particular art to look like the natural expression of a free society. History and propaganda got tangled, and seventeen years later, nobody fully unravelled them.

The deeper issue is what the programme reveals about the Cold War understanding of culture as infrastructure. Both superpowers treated intellectual and artistic life as strategic terrain. The Soviets were explicit about it: socialist realism was state doctrine, artists were workers in the ideological factory. The American approach was more sophisticated — and in some ways more insidious — because it operated through apparent freedom. The CIA didn't tell artists what to paint. It selected which painters to promote, funded the machinery of international prestige, and let the artists do what they would have done anyway. The result was a form of cultural manipulation that left no visible fingerprints on the art itself, only on its reception.

There is also a McCarthyism paradox worth sitting with. In the same years that the CIA was promoting left-wing and formerly Communist artists like Pollock and Rothko as symbols of American freedom abroad, HUAC was investigating Hollywood figures with similar backgrounds and ending careers domestically. The left hand of the American state was destroying the reputations of people the right hand was using as propaganda assets. Whether this was a case of institutional incoherence or something more deliberate — the appearance of domestic toughness combined with covert international flexibility — is still debated by historians.

The programme's legacy is partly visible in a question that art historians still argue about: when Clement Greenberg — the most influential American art critic of the postwar era, whose formalist theory of "flat" painting perfectly aligned with the CIA's need for non-representational art — built his critical framework championing abstract expressionism, was he acting independently? Greenberg had no known CIA connection. But the CIA's money was simultaneously building the institutional apparatus that made his critical voice more audible. Influence in cultural ecosystems rarely travels in straight lines.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

The Paris Review Connection

The Paris Review — the prestigious literary journal that published early Kerouac, Plimpton interviews, and the work that defined a generation of American writing — also received CIA funding through the CCF network. Its founder Peter Matthiessen later acknowledged he had been a CIA informant. The Review has argued its editorial independence was genuine; historians continue to debate the extent of CIA influence on its early direction.

Encounter's Long Afterlife

Despite the 1967 scandal, Encounter magazine continued publishing until 1991 — another 24 years after its CIA funding was exposed. It retained serious contributors throughout, suggesting the intellectual enterprise had developed genuine momentum independent of its covert origins. The scandal that should have destroyed it became, eventually, just part of its history.

Soviet Cultural Warfare in Return

The KGB ran its own mirror programme, funding peace movements, anti-nuclear campaigns, and left-wing journals in the West. Operation INFEKTION later spread disinformation about AIDS being a US bioweapon. Both superpowers were playing the same game — the American version simply had better cover and, arguably, better product.

Clement Greenberg's Formalism

The critical theory that made abstract expressionism intellectually respectable — Greenberg's argument that modernist painting's mission was to explore the inherent flatness of the canvas — happened to be perfectly tailored to non-representational art. Greenberg had no known CIA link, but his theory arrived at exactly the right moment to give CIA-promoted art a philosophical architecture that made it look inevitable rather than constructed.

Frances Stonor Saunders' 1999 Book

Who Paid the Piper? (published in the US as The Cultural Cold War) is the definitive account of the CIA/CCF relationship, drawing on declassified documents and interviews. When it appeared, it reignited the debate and introduced the story to a new generation. It remains readable, meticulous, and genuinely surprising even if you already know the outline.
