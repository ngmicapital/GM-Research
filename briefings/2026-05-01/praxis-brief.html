01 Key Ideas & Insights
Essay · Mark Manson · The Breakthrough #207 · 30 April 2026

Why Your To-Do List Will Never End — Jevons Paradox and the AI Work Trap

Mark Manson opened his Thursday newsletter with a pattern everyone who has adopted AI tools recognises but few have named cleanly: the to-do list doesn't shrink. It grows. He names the mechanism — Jevons Paradox, articulated by economist William Stanley Jevons in 1865 — and applies it without sentimentality to the AI moment:

"Inventing the train didn't reduce how much stuff got shipped — it multiplied it 100 times, because suddenly it was cheap and fast to move things across a country that had previously been too expensive to bother with. Instead of shrinking, demand exploded. AI is just the train, but for your brain." — Mark Manson, The Breakthrough #207, 30 April 2026

The economic logic is exact: when a task gets cheaper to perform, the constraint shifts from the cost of the task to the budget it had previously consumed. Remove one constraint, and the system fills the freed capacity with three more constraints. The practical result is not fewer tasks — it is more tasks, executed faster, at greater density.

Manson cites the ActivTrak Productivity Lab's 2026 State of the Workplace report — one of the most comprehensive behavioral datasets of the AI adoption era, tracking 443 million hours of digital work activity across 1,111 organisations and 163,638 employees:

These numbers confirm Jevons at scale: AI adoption compressed individual task time and immediately filled the freed hours with more tasks. The HBR study from February 2026 (UC Berkeley, 8-month ethnographic observation at a 200-person tech company) adds the mechanism: 83% of workers said AI increased their workload. Workers extended into early mornings, evenings, and weekends — voluntarily, because doing more felt possible and intrinsically rewarding. The cognitive load is invisible to managers precisely because it is self-inflicted.

The Jevons frame clarifies what most productivity conversation misses. AI adoption is not a supply problem — it is a demand problem. The question is never "how do I do more?" It is "what should I decline to do with the capacity I have recovered?" The to-do list will never end because human demand for tasks is infinite. The only variable is how deliberately you constrain intake. That is a policy decision, not a tool decision.

Manson's closing line — "Nobody ever got to the end of their to-do list and thought: 'Good, I'm done now'" — is the diagnosis. The prescription it implies is not a productivity method. It is a philosophy about what the list is for: not completion, but selection. The practitioners who will benefit most from AI are not those who use it to do more, but those who use it to do the same things while protecting more time for fewer things that matter.

Essay · Ryan Holiday · Daily Stoic · 30 April 2026

"Why Are You Surprised?" — Marcus Aurelius on the Cost of Misplaced Expectations

The Daily Stoic's Thursday send opened with a premise that lands harder the more time you spend managing people, navigating institutions, or watching politics: the surprise you feel when people behave badly is your error, not theirs. Ryan Holiday distils the argument from Book IX of Meditations:

"That sort of person was bound to do that. You might as well resent a fig tree for secreting juice." — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Book IX

The rhetorical structure is precise. Marcus is not counselling indifference to bad behaviour — he is counselling accuracy in expectations. The jerk who pulls a jerk move, the passive-aggressive parent who says something passive-aggressive, the dishonest politician who lies: none of these are surprising events. They are predictable outputs of known inputs. The surprise is a failure of observation, not a response to an anomaly.

The cost Holiday points to is specific: "Think about all the hours we waste talking about what they did. All the nights we toss and turn at 2:00 am, replaying it." This is not abstract philosophising. The replaying of predictable behaviour — which is to say, most interpersonal friction — is one of the most reliably expensive cognitive expenditures available to any professional. It drains planning capacity, defers decisions, and substitutes narrative for action.

  • Before the next interpersonal disappointment: ask whether the behaviour was actually surprising, or whether you already knew this person was capable of it. If the latter, adjust the expectation, not the reaction.
  • The characterological audit: compile a short, private map of the people you work with and what their character has consistently demonstrated. Operate from that map, not from hopes about who they might become.
  • Holiday's counter-focus: "We should focus on being the kind of person who is bound to do and be something very different — that is to say, kind and empathetic, honest and respectful. That's what our character has fated for us."

The Stoic move here is double: stop outsourcing your equanimity to other people's choices, and stop being surprised by the predictable. Both are forms of cognitive hygiene. The first is about sovereignty; the second is about accuracy. Together they free a significant amount of bandwidth for problems you can actually affect.

The Meditations passage Holiday draws from — Book IX, 42 — is one of the most-cited across the Stoic revival of the past two decades, and for good reason. Its practical reach is unusually wide: it applies to personal relationships, institutional friction, political disappointment, and market behaviour with equal force. The discipline it recommends is straightforward: expect what the evidence predicts. Not cynicism — calibrated realism.

Meditation Series · Michael Taft × Waking Up · 30 April 2026

"You Without Borders" — Michael Taft Drops Five New Nondual Awakening Sessions

Michael Taft — meditation teacher, author of The Mindful Geek, founder of the Deconstructing Yourself podcast, and one of the more technically rigorous teachers in the contemporary contemplative space — has added five new sessions to his Nondual Awakening series on the Waking Up app. The series email subject line says it plainly: You Without Borders.

Each session approaches the same fundamental insight from a different angle — the edgelessness of sensation, the spaciousness of thought, compassion as radiance, the practice of letting go — united by a single claim: "What we usually perceive as separate and confined is, and has always been, interconnected and free." The five new sessions:

  • Body Turns to Space — dissolving the experiential boundary between body-sensation and surrounding space
  • Thoughts Turn to Space — noticing the spaciousness in which thoughts appear, rather than identifying with their content
  • Wind in the Sky — working with the energetics of breath and awareness as an undivided field
  • Sun of Tonglen — compassion as non-local radiance rather than personal effort
  • Dropping the Ball — releasing the effortful grip on any object of meditation

Taft has been meditating for over 35 years, with extensive training in both Buddhist and Hindu Tantric traditions. He is a co-founder of the Alembic centre in Berkeley and was editorial director of Sounds True. His approach is notable for combining experiential precision with an unwillingness to accept imprecision in describing what is actually happening in contemplative experience — what he calls "the geek sensibility."

The Nondual Awakening series is worth the attention of practitioners at any stage — not because nondual awareness is the endpoint of meditation, but because the specific skill Taft is training (dissolving the subject-object boundary in real time, across different sense fields) is rarely addressed directly by standard mindfulness instruction. The result of consistent practice is not mystical dissolution but a practical shift in how friction, threat, and overstimulation register. The container gets larger. The contents stay the same size.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · From ActivTrak 2026 State of the Workplace · HBR · Manson

Protecting Focus in the Age of AI Work Inflation

The ActivTrak and HBR data together form a clear brief on the state of knowledge work in 2026: AI has made individuals more capable of executing tasks, and organisations have immediately consumed that surplus capacity with more tasks. Focus time is at a three-year low. Multitasking is up 12%. The workday is extending into its edges — early mornings, evenings, weekends — not because of policy but because the psychological cost of stopping has dropped. When doing more is easy, the permission structure for stopping hasn't kept pace.

Four practices that the data and the Jevons frame imply for individual practitioners:

  • Define a daily intake cap before the day starts. Jevons Paradox operates because capacity expansion is met with demand expansion automatically. The only counter is a deliberate constraint on intake set in advance — before the inbox opens, before the first AI-assisted output generates a new action item. Decide what category of work you are doing today, and decline requests outside it until tomorrow.
  • Track "focus hours" as a first-class metric alongside output. The ActivTrak data shows focus time at a three-year low precisely because output metrics (tasks completed, emails sent, projects advanced) have never been higher. The invisible cost is the cognitive depth being traded for throughput. A simple daily log — "today I had X hours of uninterrupted focus" — creates visibility where currently there is none.
  • Use AI to protect time, not only to fill it. The practitioners who will benefit from Jevons reframing are those who use AI-recovered time for protected deep work rather than for more shallow tasks. Concrete action: for every hour saved by AI automation, designate that hour as blocked focus time before reassigning it to anything else.
  • Set a weekly stopping rule. The HBR study found that weekend work increased because AI removed the natural friction that previously made working on Saturdays feel costly. Re-introduce the rule explicitly: no work emails or AI-enabled tasks between Friday 6pm and Monday 8am. The rule does the work the friction previously did.

The paradox the data confirms: the workers who will extract the most value from AI over a five-year horizon are not those doing the most work today. They are those doing the most focused work today — using AI to protect their deep-work budget, not to spend it faster. The compounding is in the depth, not the throughput.

Practice · From Daily Stoic · Marcus Aurelius · Meditations Book IX

The Character Map — Practical Stoic Expectation Management

Marcus Aurelius's instruction — stop being surprised by predictable people — is not only philosophical. It is operational. The gap between what people have consistently shown you they will do and what you expect them to do is a measurable quantity with a measurable cost. Closing the gap is a practice, not just an attitude adjustment. Three concrete tools for implementing the Daily Stoic's Thursday prompt:

  • The pre-mortem on people. Before any significant collaboration or negotiation, ask: "What is this person likely to do under pressure, based on what they have done before?" Write the answer. Most friction in professional relationships arrives because this question was never asked in advance. Writing it down primes the expectation, reduces the surprise cost, and often prompts a decision about whether to proceed at all.
  • The character ledger. Keep a brief, private log of the five to ten people who have the most impact on your professional life. One column: what their behaviour has consistently been. One column: what you have been hoping or expecting. Where there is a gap, the gap is your error — either correct the expectation or exit the relationship. People are far more consistent than we want them to be, and that consistency is a planning resource if you use it as one.
  • The "fig tree" rule. When someone does the predictable thing and you feel the friction rising, name the source: "I am surprised because I ignored available evidence." The naming converts frustration into information. Respond to the actual event (what to do next), not to the fact of the surprise (the emotional charge). The faster you make that transition, the more bandwidth you preserve.

04 On the Horizon
AI / Work

The "AI Productivity Paradox" Is Becoming the Dominant Workplace Narrative of 2026

The convergence this week — Manson naming it through Jevons Paradox, ActivTrak quantifying it at scale, HBR explaining the mechanism, Fortune citing Torsten Slok on why AI will create more lawyers and accountants not fewer — signals a phase shift in the public discourse on AI and work. For the first three years of the generative AI era (2023–2025), the dominant framing was productivity gain. The 2026 narrative, now supported by multiple independent datasets, is corrective: AI intensifies work. It does not reduce it. The implications for HR strategy, workforce planning, compensation design, and mental health policy are not yet widely integrated. The organisations that update their mental models now will have a significant advantage in the talent market over the next 18 months, as the workload cost of AI adoption becomes the leading attrition driver in knowledge work.

Contemplative Practice

Nondual Awareness Is Moving Quietly from Retreat Centre to Research Lab

Taft's new sessions land at a moment when clinical and neuroscientific interest in nondual awareness is accelerating. A 2024 paper published in Brain and Cognition (Josipovic) characterises nondual awareness as a distinct mode of consciousness with identifiable neural signatures distinct from both focused attention and open monitoring states. Research from Harvard's Contemplative Studies program is building on this. The practical implication: nondual meditation is no longer exclusively devotional — it has a growing evidence base for counsellor resilience, burnout prevention, and therapeutic effectiveness. Watch for the first randomised controlled trial of nondual awareness training as a burnout intervention to publish within 12–18 months. The demand side is obvious; the supply of qualified teachers remains the bottleneck.

Operating Discipline

"Capacity Budgeting" Is the Missing Corporate Discipline of the AI Era

The ActivTrak data makes one structural gap visible: organisations have adopted AI tools aggressively and measured the output gains. Almost none have measured — or constrained — the demand inflation that accompanies them. The term beginning to circulate in HR and strategy circles is "capacity budgeting": treating employee cognitive capacity as a fixed resource with a budget, the same way capital or headcount is budgeted, and explicitly allocating that budget before AI-recovered time is consumed by new task demand. Firms experimenting with this include several mid-size consultancies and at least one major tech company. The framework will arrive in mainstream corporate practice approximately 18–24 months after the first Fortune 500 publishes it in an annual report. That event is probably within 12 months. Practitioners who build the personal version now — a personal capacity budget maintained alongside the task list — will have the advantage of iteration before it becomes organisational policy.

Philosophy / Practice

The Stoic Revival Has a New Generation — and a Harder Edge

Holiday's Daily Stoic has been one of the most commercially successful philosophy-adjacent properties of the past decade. The cultural pattern now visible across multiple signals: the Stoic revival of 2012–2020 emphasised emotional resilience and self-improvement. The 2024–2026 iteration is more epistemically demanding. The Marcus Aurelius practitioner who surfaces this week is not using negative visualisation or memento mori as productivity tools — they are using Meditations as an operational manual for accurate perception of other people's character. That is a harder, more honest use of the text. Watch for the next major Stoic publication to be less self-help and more decision science — a book that treats ancient philosophy primarily as an error-correction system for human cognition rather than a source of motivational content. The market for that book exists and is large.

Economics / Labour

Jevons Paradox Is About to Become the Standard Frame for AI Labour Economics

Torsten Slok (Apollo Global Management chief economist) published a widely-shared analysis this week via Fortune arguing that AI will create more lawyers and accountants, not fewer — citing Jevons. This is the same economic logic Manson applies at the individual level. The convergence of a popular philosopher, an Apollo economist, two academic research teams (UC Berkeley, NBER/Atlanta Fed), and a workforce analytics company all arriving at the same Jevons conclusion in the same fortnight is not coincidence — it is a phase transition in how the AI-and-labour debate is framed. The next step: policymakers adopting the frame, which will shift the regulatory conversation from "AI will eliminate jobs" (the 2023–2024 version) to "AI will multiply demand for cognitive labour while concentrating gains among workers with the most capacity to manage intensified workflows." The political consequences of that latter framing are different, and more complex, than the elimination narrative.
