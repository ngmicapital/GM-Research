Research Radar

The ARA paper proposes replacing the research paper with a machine-executable knowledge package — the formal argument that the PDF as the unit of scientific output is already obsolete.

"The Last Human-Written Paper" — Agent-Native Research Artifacts (ARA), arxiv 2604.24658

ResearchARAResearch infrastructureAgent-native

Authors: multi-institution team (full author list in preprint). Published: April 27, 2026. One-line finding: replacing the narrative PDF with a structured, machine-executable research artifact — including scientific logic, executable code with full specs, an exploration graph preserving failures, and evidence grounding every claim — raises question-answering accuracy from 72.4% to 93.7% and reproduction success from 57.4% to 64.4% on PaperBench and RE-Bench. The paper proposes a four-layer protocol for what it calls an Agent-Native Research Artifact (ARA): (1) scientific logic layer, (2) executable code with full specifications, (3) an exploration graph that preserves dead ends, and (4) evidence grounding every claim in raw outputs. Three tools support the ecosystem: a Live Research Manager (captures decisions during development), an Ara Compiler (translates legacy PDFs into ARAs), and an Ara-native review system that automates objective checks so human reviewers focus on novelty and significance. The title is a provocation: if the ARA protocol is adopted, human narrative is relegated to framing, not substance.

⚡ Implication for dc in 6 months: Two threads. For academic work (legal-brief pipeline, Jason Allen collaboration): formal legal scholarship has stricter citation and methods norms than ML papers, and the "replace narrative with executable artifact" framing doesn't map directly to law review articles — but the exploration graph concept (preserving research dead-ends) is directly applicable to any systematic literature survey. The Ara Compiler is more immediately interesting — it can translate legacy PDFs into structured formats, which is a solved problem for the legal-brief ingestion pipeline. For the briefings harness: the ARA protocol is essentially what the daily briefings already do (claim → source → action), just formalised. The parallel isn't accidental — agent-native outputs are already replacing human-authored summaries in research contexts. This paper names the transition.

KV cache hitting 80–88% hit rates in production — real cost savings starting to show in provider economics

UpdateKV cacheInference costProduction

Production deployment studies published this week (arxiv 2604.25724) show that key-value cache (KV cache) techniques are now achieving 80–88% cache hit rates in compound AI system deployments — meaning 80–88% of inference calls are being served from cached states rather than full computation. Combined with prompt caching (which Anthropic and OpenAI both offer), this is materially reducing per-query cost in pipelines that reuse system prompts or shared context windows. One cited production deployment achieved a 3.9× throughput improvement and 30–40% cost reduction vs prior static-allocation inference.

⚡ Implication: The briefings harness already benefits from prompt caching on repeated system prompts. The actionable upgrade: ensure every scheduled briefing task is sending the system prompt in a cacheable format (same hash, same position in the messages array) rather than regenerating it with today's date embedded in the system prompt. A 30% cost reduction on the $13/dev/day guidance from Issue #34 is material. Check the harness skill files to confirm the system prompt structure is cache-friendly.

🔬 Plain-English Explainer — why "chips that think in memory" are the next cost collapse

Imagine an AI model as a giant reference book. Every time the AI answers a question, it has to flip through the entire book — but the book lives in a warehouse (off-chip memory) and someone has to physically carry it to the desk (the compute chip) one page at a time. Today's AI chips spend most of their energy on that carrying, not on the actual reading. The TPU 8i's CAE and Groq's LPU are both attacks on the carrying problem — they put more of the book physically inside the desk. The practical result: same answer, 2–5× less energy, and latency measured in milliseconds instead of seconds. For traders, this matters for two reasons. First, every 30% drop in inference energy cost is eventually a 30% drop in API pricing — which is why Gemini and GPT-5.5 will get cheaper faster than models alone predict. Second, for AI-compute DePIN tokens like RENDER and AKT: the distributed GPU model is "we have the book in many warehouses; rent access cheaply." If hyperscaler chips get the book small enough to fit in the desk, the economic edge of distributed warehouses narrows. The inflection is 2–3 years away, not this quarter — but it's the structural tail risk to track for any DePIN position held beyond 2026.
