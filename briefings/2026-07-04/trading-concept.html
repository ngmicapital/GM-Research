Alpha — Cash-and-Carry Basis Trade

Saturday, 4 July 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue 68 11 min read
TL;DR

The cash-and-carry basis trade is buying an asset spot while simultaneously shorting its futures or perpetual contract, pocketing the price gap between the two legs as a market-neutral, close-to-riskless yield — the position doesn't care whether Bitcoin goes up or down, because a rise in spot is offset dollar-for-dollar by a loss on the short future, and vice versa; the only thing you're harvesting is the premium itself, either through convergence at expiry (dated futures) or through funding payments (perpetuals). It sounds like free money because in a narrow sense it is — Jack Schwager's own framing of arbitrageur activity as what keeps futures tied to cash markets in the first place is the whole justification for the trade existing — but the size of the premium is compensation for real frictions: financing cost, margin/liquidation risk on the leveraged leg, exchange counterparty exposure, and the risk that the “riskless” spread reprices against you mid-trade. This isn't an exotic instrument reserved for quants — it's the dominant reason 20–35% of Bitcoin spot ETF assets exist at all, and when the basis compresses, that money leaves just as mechanically as it arrived.

§01
What It Is
Getting paid for the gap, not the direction
The cash-and-carry basis trade is buying an asset spot and shorting its future or perp at the same time, collecting the price gap between the two legs as market-neutral yield.

The cash-and-carry basis trade is how you get paid for a market's impatience without taking a view on which way it moves. You buy the underlying asset in the spot market and simultaneously sell (short) a futures or perpetual contract on that same asset for the same notional size. If the future is priced above spot — the normal state in a bullish or leverage-hungry market, called contango — you've locked in that premium as your profit the moment both legs are on. From there, price can do anything: spot up, future up by the same amount, your gain on one leg exactly offsets your loss on the other, and the premium you captured at entry is yours regardless. That's the entire trade. No prediction required.

The mechanism differs slightly depending on the instrument. With dated futures (CME Bitcoin futures, quarterly contracts), the premium is capped and known at entry — it must converge to zero by expiry because the future and spot become the same thing on settlement day, so your return is a fixed spread over a fixed number of days, annualized for comparison. With perpetual futures, which never expire, the same spot-long/perp-short structure instead harvests the funding rate — the periodic payment (typically every 1, 4, or 8 hours depending on the exchange) that perpetual exchanges use to keep the perp price anchored to spot. When perps trade rich relative to spot because long demand dominates, longs pay shorts; you're short, so you collect. It's the same economic trade wearing two different mechanical costumes.

This is not a fringe strategy. Following the 2024 launch of US spot Bitcoin ETFs, leveraged funds built one of the largest net-short positions in CME Bitcoin futures history specifically to run this trade against ETF longs — as of late January 2026, leveraged funds held roughly 15,399 short CME contracts against just 3,003 longs [CME Group / CFTC Commitment of Traders data]. Estimates put 20–35% of all capital sitting in spot Bitcoin ETFs there not because the holder believes in Bitcoin, but because the basis trade against CME futures was throwing off double-digit annualized yield with a hedged, market-neutral structure. When you hear that ETF inflows or outflows moved by billions in a week, a meaningful share of that flow is basis-trade capital rotating in or out — not conviction capital changing its mind.

Basisthe price gap between a futures/perp contract and the spot price of the same asset
Contangofutures trading above spot — the normal state when carrying/holding cost or long demand dominates
Backwardationfutures trading below spot — signals stress, hedging demand, or bearish positioning
Funding Ratethe periodic payment perpetual exchanges use to anchor perp price to spot; positive funding means longs pay shorts
Delta-Neutrala position with zero net directional exposure — gains on one leg are designed to offset losses on the other
Remember The basis trade is market-neutral on direction but not risk-free on everything else — you're being paid a spread for carrying financing, liquidation, and redemption risk that only shows up when it's already too late to exit cheaply.
§02
Mechanics
From premium to position to convergence
The trade opens when the future trades at a premium to spot, holds flat P&L as both legs move together, and closes profit either at expiry convergence or via ongoing perp funding payments.

The mechanical sequence is identical whether you're running it on CME quarterlies or a perpetual swap — only the profit-realization event changes:

1. Screen for a rich basis. Compare the futures/perp price to spot. A meaningfully positive annualized spread (dated futures) or an elevated funding rate (perps) is the entry signal. 2. Buy spot, short the derivative, matched notional. Same dollar size on both legs — this is what makes the position delta-neutral. The short leg is margined, typically with leverage, since you don't need to post 100% of notional to hold a hedged short. 3. Hold while the position self-offsets. Spot and the derivative move together (they're pricing the same asset), so your mark-to-market P&L stays close to flat regardless of Bitcoin's actual path — the basis captured at entry is locked in economically, even if it isn't cash in hand yet. 4. Realize the premium. For dated futures, close both legs (or let the future expire) as the premium mechanically converges to zero. For perps, collect funding payments at every settlement interval for as long as you hold the position, then unwind when funding compresses or flips.

Annualized Basis = (Futures Price − Spot Price) / Spot Price × (365 / Days to Expiry)
for dated futures; for perpetuals, substitute the average funding rate per interval × intervals per year in place of the discrete premium-to-expiry calculation — both express the same underlying yield concept

A worked number makes the mechanics concrete. With spot BTC at $30,000 and a 90-day future at $30,600, the basis is $600, or 2% flat / roughly 8.1% annualized. Buy 1 BTC spot; short $30,600 notional of the future using leverage so only ~$3,060 margin is required on that leg. If Bitcoin rallies to $32,000 by expiry: the spot leg gains $2,000, the short future leg loses $1,400 (it only had $600 of premium above the now-$32,000 print to lose), net $600 captured — the exact basis locked in at entry, regardless of the $2,000 move in either direction [BitMEX, worked example]. That's the delta-neutral property in action: the P&L is the basis, full stop, whichever way Bitcoin actually went.

InstrumentRealization mechanismKey variable
Dated futures (CME quarterly)Convergence at fixed expiry dateAnnualized basis at entry — known, capped, doesn't drift day to day
Perpetual futuresOngoing funding payments, no expiryFunding rate — resets every settlement interval, can compress or flip negative anytime

The perp version is structurally riskier precisely because it has no forced convergence date — you're re-underwriting the trade every funding interval, and nothing forces the market to keep paying you.

§04
In Practice
Where the 'riskless' trade actually bites
The basis is the yield on a leveraged, two-legged position — treat the annualized number as compensation for financing, liquidation, and redemption risk, not as free money.
1

Compare annualized basis to your actual cost of capital

An 8% annualized basis is only attractive relative to your financing cost, margin opportunity cost, and the risk-free rate available elsewhere. Institutions run this trade at scale because their funding cost is low; retail running it on exchange margin can see the spread eaten by borrow/funding fees on the short leg.

2

Track the basis itself as a sentiment gauge, not just a trade

A blown-out annualized basis (15–30%+) signals aggressive long positioning and leverage-hungry sentiment even if you never put the trade on — it's the same information Open Interest and funding percentile readings give you, from a different angle.

3

Size the short leg for liquidation, not just notional match

Matching notional makes you delta-neutral in theory, but the short leg is leveraged and margined — a sharp adverse move against under-margined shorts can trigger a liquidation on that leg alone well before the spot gain catches up, breaking the hedge exactly when you need it most.

4

Watch redemption mechanics, not just your own position

If you're exposed to basis-trade-driven flow (e.g. holding a spot ETF for other reasons), understand that a meaningful share of co-holders are basis traders who will redeem mechanically the moment the spread compresses — their exit can move the underlying even though your own thesis hasn't changed.

Worked example — the trade opening today, roughly
SetupBTC spot ~$62,600 (4 Jul); BTC perp funding at 0.005267% per interval, 5.77% annualized [TrueNorth derivatives feed, 44th %ile 7d — moderate, not overheated]

ContrastSame trade on ETH today would be harvesting funding at the 94.6th percentile (far richer, but far more crowded — and crowded trades unwind hardest) [Morning Edge, 4 Jul 2026]

§07
Common Traps
Where 'market-neutral' stops being neutral
The single biggest mistake is treating the basis as a fixed, riskless coupon instead of a leveraged spread that can compress, flip, or become impossible to exit all at once.
1
Calling it "riskless" and sizing it like it is

The basis is captured, but the position underneath it is leveraged on the short leg. A sharp adverse move can trigger liquidation on the short before the offsetting spot gain is realized or even marked — the hedge only protects your net P&L if both legs survive to the same moment.

2
Ignoring rate-flip risk on perpetual carry

Positive funding is not a guaranteed coupon. A rate paying 0.05% per interval can flip negative the next interval, turning a short-perp position from funding-collector to funding-payer overnight — with no forced expiry to bail you out the way dated futures have.

3
Underestimating basis risk at the moment you actually need to exit

The trade's math assumes you can close both legs near-simultaneously at the prices you modeled. In a fast market or thin book, the perp/future can gap away from spot right as you try to unwind — you eat a real loss on a trade that was "market-neutral" the whole way through, except at the door.

4
Missing that basis-trade unwinds move the underlying, not just derivatives

When the annualized basis compressed from roughly 6.63% to 4.46% in late 2025, about $4 billion left Bitcoin ETFs over 53 days [CFB] — not because sentiment on Bitcoin itself changed, but because the arbitrage stopped paying. If you hold the underlying for unrelated reasons, a basis unwind by other holders is a real flow risk to your position too.

5
[Overhyped] Treating a headline "11% annualized" basis yield as a stable income product

Basis yields advertised in ETF-wrapper marketing (Defiance and others have filed basis-trade-harvesting ETF structures) describe a historical or current snapshot, not a fixed rate. The trade's own history shows yields compressing sharply in single-digit-month windows (May 2024, then again in a dramatic hedge-fund exit around Feb 2025) — this is a variable, crowding-sensitive spread wearing a bond-like sales pitch. Tony's context: treat any "stable yield" framing of a leveraged derivatives spread with the same skepticism as a stablecoin yield claiming to be riskless — the risk didn't disappear, it moved into the fine print.
