The Man Who Fed the World and Poisoned It

Fritz Haber invented the process that sustains half of all humans alive today — and personally supervised the first chemical weapons attack in history. The same chemistry feeds 4 billion people and built the Beirut bomb.

Fritz Haber cracked the problem of nitrogen fixation in 1909 — turning atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia, and ammonia into fertilizer — enabling the food production that sustains roughly half of all humans alive today. He also supervised the first large-scale chemical weapons attack at Ypres in 1915, his wife shot herself in protest the following night, and the pesticide developed by his institute later became Zyklon B. The Haber-Bosch process is the most consequential chemistry discovery in human history, and its inventor is one of the most morally catastrophic scientists who ever lived.

The One-Paragraph Version

In 1909, Fritz Haber solved the problem that was about to starve the world: how to pull nitrogen from the air and turn it into fertilizer. The Haber-Bosch process he invented now sustains roughly half of all humans alive — nearly 4 billion people exist because of it. The same man personally supervised the world's first mass chemical weapons attack at Ypres in 1915; his wife, a chemist herself, shot herself with his service revolver in protest the following night; and the pesticide developed by his institute later became Zyklon B, used to murder Jews in the Holocaust — including, in all probability, some of his own relatives. One man. One discovery. Eight billion people and counting.

Section 01

The Story

By the late 19th century, agronomists understood that civilisation was running on borrowed time. The world's population was approaching 1.6 billion and accelerating. Crop yields depended entirely on fixed nitrogen — the form plants can absorb — and the finite global reserves of it were being strip-mined. Peru's guano islands were nearly exhausted. Chilean saltpeter, shipped across oceans at enormous cost, was the primary source of nitrogen fertilizer for the entire Western world. British scientist William Crookes warned in 1898 that mass famine was coming within a generation unless chemistry could do what nature had never managed at scale: pull nitrogen from thin air.

The problem was the molecule itself. Atmospheric nitrogen — N₂ — is one of the most chemically inert substances in existence. Its two atoms are locked together by a triple covalent bond with a dissociation energy of ~941 kJ/mol. The molecule is so stable, so unreactive, that it will sit in a flask with almost anything and do nothing. Lightning can break it. Certain soil bacteria can break it, slowly, using a remarkable enzyme called nitrogenase. But for the industrial quantities civilisation required, neither was remotely sufficient.

Fritz Haber was born in 1868 in Breslau into one of the city's oldest Jewish merchant families. By 1905 he was at the Karlsruhe Polytechnic, a physical chemist of formidable precision, working on the thermodynamics of gas reactions. He became obsessed with the nitrogen problem. By 1908, he had filed a patent on the synthesis of ammonia from its elements. In the summer of 1909, Haber and his assistant Robert Le Rossignol staged a definitive laboratory demonstration at Karlsruhe — running a reaction at extreme heat and pressure over an osmium catalyst — and produced liquid ammonia drop by drop, at about 125 mL per hour. A trickle. But proof.

"With four kilograms of nitrogen per head per year, we can feed the world. Haber has given us this." — Carl Bosch, BASF engineer, after scaling the process to industrial production

A final coda so dark it sounds invented: the pesticide Zyklon B was developed by chemists working under Haber at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute in the early 1920s, as an agricultural fumigant. The Nazis repurposed it. Haber's own relatives almost certainly died in the gas chambers using chemistry that came from his institute — the institute he was expelled from for being Jewish.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The Haber-Bosch process solves a problem with a fundamental tension at its core. The reaction N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ is thermodynamically favourable at low temperatures (it's exothermic, releasing ~92 kJ/mol, and it reduces the number of gas molecules, so pressure favours it). But at low temperatures, the reaction is kinetically glacial — the triple nitrogen bond barely moves. At high temperatures, the kinetics improve but the thermodynamics flip: equilibrium swings back toward the starting materials. You cannot have both at once.

Haber's insight was finding the operational sweet spot — and using a catalyst to make it practical. The industrial process runs at 400–500°C and 150–200 atmospheres of pressure. These are brutal conditions. In 1909, industrial metallurgy had no reactors rated for them. Carl Bosch at BASF had to invent new steel alloys, new sealing methods, and a new understanding of hydrogen embrittlement — the phenomenon whereby carbon steel becomes brittle and fails when hydrogen diffuses into it at high pressure and temperature. He solved it by lining reactors with a soft iron inner skin that absorbed hydrogen before it could reach the structural steel.

The catalyst is the process's heart. The rate-determining step is the dissociation of N₂ at a solid surface — breaking the triple bond enough for nitrogen atoms to react with hydrogen. Haber originally used osmium (rare, expensive, impractical at scale). Bosch's team at BASF screened thousands of candidate materials and settled on promoted iron — finely divided iron derived from magnetite (Fe₃O₄), doped with aluminium oxide, potassium oxide, and calcium oxide. The potassium acts as an electronic promoter, making the iron surface more electron-rich and more aggressive at attacking the N₂ bond. This promoted iron catalyst, essentially unchanged from 1913, still runs in Haber-Bosch plants today.

The numbers are staggering. The landmark 2008 paper by Erisman et al. in Nature Geoscience estimated that 44–48% of the global population in 2000–2008 was dependent on synthetic nitrogen for their food. Vaclav Smil's definitive 2001 book Enriching the Earth reaches similar conclusions. A striking proxy: nearly 50% of the nitrogen in human tissue today passed through a Haber-Bosch reactor. The atoms in your muscles and DNA were, within living geological memory, inert gas in the atmosphere.

The process consumes approximately 3–5% of world natural gas production and produces 1–3% of global CO₂ emissions. It runs at roughly 80 times the thermodynamic minimum — because the extreme conditions required to break the nitrogen triple bond demand enormous energy inputs. Researchers have been trying to crack ambient-temperature nitrogen fixation (as biology does with nitrogenase enzymes) for over a century. No one has managed it at industrial scale.

Section 03

The Connections

The same chemistry that feeds the world blows it up. The Haber-Bosch process was critical to Germany's ability to wage World War I after the British naval blockade cut off Chilean nitrate imports in 1914. Without synthetic ammonia, Germany could not manufacture explosives and would likely have run out of munitions within a year. Historians estimate the process extended the war by two to three years — meaning the industrialised mass death of the Western Front was in part enabled by the same discovery that later fed billions.

Ammonium nitrate — a direct downstream product of Haber ammonia — is both a critical fertilizer and a powerful explosive oxidiser. The chemistry is the same; only the context differs. On April 19, 1995, Timothy McVeigh used approximately 2 tonnes of ammonium nitrate fertilizer to destroy the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building in Oklahoma City, killing 168 people. On August 4, 2020, 2,750 tonnes of improperly stored ammonium nitrate at the Beirut port detonated — one of the largest non-nuclear explosions in history, killing more than 200 people and displacing 300,000.

The nitrogen pollution crisis. Crops absorb on average only about 40% of applied nitrogen. The rest runs off into waterways, triggering algal blooms, oxygen depletion, and dead zones. The Mississippi River Basin has increased nitrogen inputs to the Gulf of Mexico by two to seven times over the past century. The resulting Gulf of Mexico hypoxic zone — the second largest dead zone in the world, spanning over 1.6 million hectares — costs U.S. seafood and tourism industries an estimated $82 million per year. Nitrogen fertilizers also account for nearly 60% of global nitrous oxide (N₂O) emissions — a greenhouse gas 300 times more potent than CO₂ over a 100-year timeframe.

Clara Immerwahr as the moral centre. The story's deepest irony is that the person who most clearly saw what Haber was doing — and refused to accommodate it — was a chemist herself, trained in the same tradition, married to the man who had become its most celebrated practitioner. Her doctoral thesis was on the solubility of metallic salts. She had built her career on the same careful empiricism as her husband. Her suicide note has never been found. Her suicide itself became a kind of argument: that there are uses of science which a scientist is obligated to refuse, at any personal cost.

"What Fritz is doing is a perversion of the ideals of science... It is a sign of barbarity, corrupting the very discipline which ought to bring new insights into life." — Clara Immerwahr, on her husband's chemical weapons work, 1915

The Nobel paradox. Haber's 1918 Nobel Prize remains one of the most debated in the prize's history — not because his chemistry was wrong, but because it was simultaneously the most life-giving and life-destroying scientific advance of the century. Albert Einstein, who knew Haber personally, expressed deep ambivalence. The Nobel Committee acknowledged only the ammonia synthesis. The citation contains no mention of Ypres. That silence is its own kind of statement.

Further Down the Hole

📖

Enriching the Earth — Vaclav Smil

The definitive scholarly treatment of Haber-Bosch. MIT Press, 2001. 813 footnotes. Smil traces the process from Haber's lab notebooks through to modern agrochemistry and its environmental consequences. Dense but rewarding — the kind of book you cite for the rest of your life.

🧬

Erisman et al. (2008) — "How a Century of Ammonia Synthesis Changed the World"

The landmark Nature Geoscience paper that quantified what the process actually means demographically. The "half of humanity" figure comes from here. Eight pages that anyone interested in food, population, or environmental chemistry should read.

💀

The Alchemy of Air — Thomas Hager

A narrative biography of both Haber and Carl Bosch. More accessible than Smil and better on the human drama — Clara Immerwahr's story, the Nobel controversy, Haber's exile. Reads like a tragedy in the classical sense: a man destroyed by the same qualities that made him great.

💣

The Beirut Explosion and the Ammonium Nitrate Chain

The 2020 Beirut port disaster is the most vivid recent demonstration that Haber's chemistry is inseparable from its destructive twin. The cargo — 2,750 tonnes of ammonium nitrate — had sat in a warehouse for six years after being confiscated from an abandoned ship. The political failure that left it there is a separate story, but the chemistry is pure Haber-Bosch.

🌱

The Quest for Ambient Nitrogen Fixation

Biology does what Haber couldn't: fix nitrogen at room temperature and pressure, using the enzyme nitrogenase. Researchers have been trying to replicate this for over a century. Recent advances in electrocatalysis and engineered microbes have made real progress — but nothing yet at industrial scale. The prize for whoever cracks it is almost inconceivably large.
