CLARITY Act: Senate Returns April 13 With Markup on Deck — May Deadline or Years of Delay

"If we don't get the CLARITY Act passed by May, digital asset legislation will not pass for the foreseeable future." — Senator Bernie Moreno [paraphrased from FinTech Weekly, April 2026]

The Senate returns from Easter recess on April 13 with the CLARITY Act's Banking Committee markup targeted for the week of April 13 or April 20 — the only two in-session weeks before the end of April. Senator Cynthia Lummis confirmed the markup for the "second half of April," with only 18 working weeks remaining on the Senate calendar before the October midterm recess. The bill, H.R.3633 (Digital Asset Market Clarity Act), already passed the House 294-134 in July 2025.

The Tillis-Alsobrooks stablecoin yield compromise reached March 20 is the baseline entering markup: passive yield on stablecoin balances is banned; activity-based rewards (tied to payments, transfers, platform use) remain permitted. The language is written broadly to close structuring workarounds through affiliates. Coinbase and Stripe have objected — Coinbase's 4% USDC yield product is directly in scope. Contested DeFi provisions and ethics language (whether senior officials should be barred from personally profiting from crypto) remain unresolved going into markup.

📎 Historical Precedent — The last major crypto market structure bill, FTX-era Digital Commodities Consumer Protection Act (2022), died without a committee vote after midterm dynamics shifted. Pattern: crypto legislation requires sustained bipartisan momentum that midterms can sever.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
The markup window is genuinely narrow: April 13-30, then May recess looms. The Commodity Exchange Act § 4(a) and Securities Act registration exemptions at the core of CLARITY are settled; the DeFi and ethics provisions are the fracture lines. If committee markup fails, the CFTC retains spot commodity jurisdiction over the 16 named assets from the March 17 SEC-CFTC interpretation, but without statutory clarity on DeFi protocols, enforcement discretion continues to govern. The bill's passage timeline — markup, floor vote, conference with House, signature — is tight even if markup succeeds this month. Winners if enacted: Coinbase (COIN), Kraken, Uniswap Labs; losers: unregistered token issuers, DeFi protocols without governance entity.
🔄 Contrarian Take
The stablecoin yield ban may actually benefit incumbents more than harm them. Coinbase's 4% product attracted retail deposits away from banks; the ban levels the playing field toward DeFi lending protocols where activity-based rewards are easier to structure. The louder the industry objects, the more the yield ban signals to banking regulators that the compromise is working as intended. Senate passage may accelerate DeFi adoption precisely by killing the CeFi yield advantage.
🏗️ Industry Implications
Coinbase faces immediate product restructuring if the yield ban survives markup unchanged — its USDC rewards product would require conversion to activity-linked structures. Circle (CRCL, NYSE) benefits as USDC's issuer if volume shifts from yield-seeking to payment-use stablecoins. Uniswap Labs and Aave are watching DeFi provisions closely; a broad definition of "DeFi broker" could trigger FinCEN and CFTC registration obligations for protocol developers. Ripple's RLUSD stablecoin — built on payments rather than yield — is strategically positioned for any version of the bill.
📊 Portfolio Impact

Bullish [COIN, CRCL, XRP, AVAX, SOL]: Market structure clarity removes the legal overhang that has suppressed institutional entry; passage is the clearest near-term catalyst for listed crypto equities.

Bearish [Yield-product CeFi platforms, unregistered token issuers]: Passive stablecoin yield ban compresses a key retail monetization channel; registration requirements add compliance cost.

Neutral [BTC, ETH]: Both already classified as commodities under the March 17 SEC-CFTC joint interpretation; CLARITY provides incremental but not transformative clarity for the two largest assets.

OCC National Trust Bank Rule Effective — Eleven Crypto Firms Race for Federal Charter in 83 Days

"The Final Rule clarifies that the OCC had never interpreted the rulemaking to prohibit national trust banks from engaging in non-fiduciary activities — including non-fiduciary custody and safekeeping activities, which are authorized as part of the business of banking under 12 U.S.C. 24(Seventh)." — OCC Final Rule preamble [paraphrased, Stinson LLP analysis, March 2026]

The OCC's amended national bank chartering rule took effect April 1, 2026, replacing the term "fiduciary activities" in 12 CFR 5.20 with "operations of a trust company and activities related thereto." The practical consequence: national trust banks can provide digital asset custody as a core, non-fiduciary activity without needing a separate regulatory hook. The OCC finalized the rule March 2, after proposing it in January. Eleven firms filed for or received conditional OCC national trust bank approvals in 83 days — Circle, Ripple, Paxos, BitGo, Fidelity Digital Assets, and most recently Coinbase (April 2), which received preliminary approval for Coinbase National Trust Company, a de novo non-insured national trust company based in New York.

These charters permit digital asset custody on behalf of clients but bar deposit-taking or lending — a custodian model, not a commercial bank. Coinbase stated it is "not becoming a commercial bank" and will not engage in fractional reserve banking. ABA and ICBA have formally demanded the OCC slow the approval process, citing insufficient time to evaluate systemic risks of crypto-native firms entering the federal banking framework.

📎 Historical Precedent — OCC's 2020-21 conditional approvals for Anchorage and Protego created the federal trust charter template for crypto; both faced preopening conditions lasting 18+ months. The 2026 wave is broader and faster, reflecting a deliberate pro-crypto chartering posture under Comptroller Jonathan Gould.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
A federal OCC trust charter under 12 U.S.C. 27(a) preempts state money transmission licensing for chartered activities, directly reducing regulatory fragmentation for custodians operating across 50 states. Firms with conditional approvals must clear preopening conditions — AML/BSA program build-out, key personnel hires, OCC safety-and-soundness review — before receiving final charters. The ABA/ICBA pushback is a lobbying action, not a legal challenge, and has no binding effect on the OCC's chartering authority. The race for federal trust status creates a two-tier custody market: OCC-chartered custodians (federally preempted, GENIUS Act-compatible) vs. state-licensed trust companies (fragmented, higher compliance cost). Coinbase, Circle, and Paxos are positioned to dominate institutional crypto custody under the new structure; state-only custodians face disintermediation.
🔄 Contrarian Take
Preopening conditions are not formalities. Anchorage Digital waited nearly three years between conditional and final OCC approval. If the OCC applies comparable scrutiny to 11 firms simultaneously, most will not reach operational charter status in 2026. The charter announcements are regulatory positioning, not operational infrastructure — investors pricing in an OCC premium on Coinbase or Circle equity may be running ahead of the actual delivery timeline.
🏗️ Industry Implications
State-chartered digital asset custodians — Wyoming SPDI banks, New York Trust Company licensees — face structural disintermediation as OCC-chartered competitors gain federal preemption and cleaner GENIUS Act integration. Institutional allocators (asset managers, pension funds) will increasingly require OCC-chartered custodians for digital asset mandates, as federal supervision satisfies fiduciary duty standards more cleanly than state licensing. Fireblocks, Anchorage Digital (already chartered), and BitGo (conditional approval) are the infrastructure layer; Coinbase National Trust enters as a vertically integrated competitor.
📊 Portfolio Impact

Bullish [COIN, CRCL/Circle, Paxos, Ripple, Anchorage Digital]: Federal charter status creates a custody moat for institutional mandates — winners in the two-tier custody market that is now structurally established.

Bearish [State-only custodians, Wyoming SPDI-chartered banks]: Federal preemption erodes their competitive position; no equivalent pathway to national scale without OCC application.

Neutral [BTC, ETH spot markets]: Charter wave improves custody infrastructure broadly, benefiting all assets held in custody without creating a specific price catalyst.

📋 What To Do

Treasury Opens State Stablecoin Path — GENIUS Act "Substantially Similar" NPRM Defines Arbitrage Framework

"Treasury recognizes that states may impose additional or more stringent requirements so long as they do not conflict with the Act or other federal law, and do not so transform the regime that it can no longer be reasonably viewed as substantially similar to the federal framework." — U.S. Treasury, FR Doc. 2026-06489, April 3, 2026 [PRIMARY]

Treasury published FR Doc. 2026-06489 on April 3, 2026, implementing GENIUS Act § 4(a)'s "substantially similar" standard for state stablecoin regulatory regimes. Under the GENIUS Act (S.1582), payment stablecoin issuers with consolidated outstanding issuance under $10 billion may opt for state rather than federal regulation — but only if their state's regime is "substantially similar" to the federal framework. Treasury's proposed principles define what that means across six domains: licensing/applications (§ 5), supervision/enforcement (§ 6), custody requirements (§ 10), insolvency treatment (§ 11), transition to federal oversight (§ 4(d)), and reserve requirements. States may add more stringent rules but cannot conflict with federal law or alter substantive standards.

Critically, Treasury's definition of "Federal regulatory framework" excludes guidance or interpretations not published in the Federal Register — meaning informal agency positions do not count as the baseline. The Conference of State Bank Supervisors (CSBS) filed a comment letter supporting broad state flexibility. The rule is in proposed form; comments are open.

📎 Historical Precedent — The New York BitLicense (2015) created the first state-specific crypto licensing framework; firms that obtained it faced a compliance burden that drove many offshore. The GENIUS Act state pathway is structurally different — federal preemption is the ceiling, not the floor, and Treasury's "wide latitude" framing is designed to avoid the BitLicense's exclusionary effect.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
The $10B threshold is a deliberate size gate: it captures startup and mid-tier issuers while funneling major issuers (Tether, USDC at $44B+ supply) into the federal regime. The arbitrage question is which states will offer the most issuer-friendly "substantially similar" regime. Wyoming, with its SPDI framework, and Texas, with its virtual currency transmission law, are best positioned to be first movers. The CSBS's active engagement signals state regulators want the pipeline, not just deference to federal rules. For lawyers: the six-domain scope (licensing through insolvency) is unusually comprehensive — any state law that deviates on reserve composition, custody segregation, or insolvency waterfall could fail the "substantially similar" test even if it exceeds federal minimums in other areas.
🔄 Contrarian Take
The "wide latitude" framing masks a significant federal veto: Treasury alone decides what is "substantially similar," and the exclusion of non-Federal-Register guidance from the baseline means Treasury can shift the goalposts through rulemaking without notice. States that build regimes under this NPRM may find themselves re-licensed or overridden when a future Treasury revises the principles. The state pathway could become a regulatory sandbox that the federal government can close at will.
🏗️ Industry Implications
Smaller stablecoin issuers (sub-$10B) — including PayPal's PYUSD, Paxos's USDP, Wyoming-based issuers — can now engage with state regulators to define the compliance pathway that suits their business model before being swept into the full federal regime. Wyoming and Texas banking regulators are the immediate winners in the jurisdiction race. Large issuers (Circle/USDC at $44B+, Tether/USDT) are unaffected — they are above the threshold and must comply with the federal framework directly.
📊 Portfolio Impact

Bullish [PYUSD (PayPal), sub-$10B stablecoin issuers, Wyoming/Texas-chartered fintechs]: State pathway provides a less onerous compliance route to US market access — lower barrier to entry for new stablecoin products.

Bearish [International stablecoin issuers (Tether/USDT) operating above threshold]: No state pathway available; must meet full federal framework or risk non-compliant status in US market.

Neutral [Circle/USDC]: Already above threshold; federal regime applies regardless of state NPRM outcome.

📋 What To Do
TraderSub-$10B stablecoin issuers gaining state-pathway access expand the competitive set for USDC and USDT — monitor comment period responses from Wyoming and Texas regulators as signals of which state regime moves first.
CounselFile a comment letter on FR Doc. 2026-06489 by the close of the comment period — the six-domain "substantially similar" scope definition is where practitioner input will shape Treasury's final rule.
ComplianceMap your stablecoin issuance volume against the $10B threshold and model which state regimes are most likely to be approved as "substantially similar" to determine your optimal licensing jurisdiction.
FounderIf you are a sub-$10B issuer, engage Wyoming or Texas banking regulators now about "substantially similar" certification — first-mover state approval creates a competitive license moat.

CFTC Prediction Markets ANPR: 40 Questions, April 30 Comment Deadline — Sports Contracts Under Heightened Scrutiny

"The Commission is issuing this ANPR to evaluate whether and how to update its regulatory framework for event contract derivatives traded on prediction markets, including whether some event contracts should be prohibited as contrary to the public interest." — CFTC, FR Doc. 2026-05105, March 16, 2026 [PRIMARY]

The CFTC published an Advanced Notice of Proposed Rulemaking on March 16, 2026 (FR Doc. 2026-05105) seeking public comment on whether to amend or issue new regulations for event contract derivatives on prediction markets under the Commodity Exchange Act. The 40-question ANPR spans: application of CEA core principles to prediction markets, the "contrary to public interest" determination for prohibited contracts (7 U.S.C. § 5c(c)(5)(C)), the listing process for new event contracts, insider information risks, and how event contracts differ from swaps and futures. The comment deadline is April 30, 2026 — 20 days from today.

Simultaneously, the CFTC's Division of Market Oversight issued a staff advisory signaling heightened scrutiny for sports-related and other event contracts. Kalshi, which successfully litigated the right to list election contracts (D.C. Circuit, September 2024), and Robinhood Events are the primary regulated venues. The CFTC's Innovation Task Force — also recently launched — will work alongside the ANPR process.

📎 Historical Precedent — CFTC's 2012 prohibition on Nadex's election and box-office event contracts under § 5c(c)(5)(C) established the "contrary to public interest" framework; Kalshi's 2024 D.C. Circuit win substantially narrowed the CFTC's discretion to invoke that prohibition for election contracts.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
The ANPR is a pre-rulemaking signal: the CFTC sees prediction markets growing rapidly (Kalshi, Polymarket, Robinhood Events) and wants the regulatory framework to catch up before a major manipulation or integrity incident forces reactive enforcement. Question 12 in the ANPR — whether event contracts create information advantages tantamount to insider trading — is the critical one for crypto prediction markets (BTC price, ETH ETF approval contracts). If the CFTC extends the insider information framework to crypto-pegged event contracts, crypto firms operating prediction market products will need CFTC registration and CEA compliance. The April 30 deadline gives industry 20 days to shape the record.
🏗️ Industry Implications
Kalshi and Robinhood Events benefit from regulatory clarity regardless of outcome — ambiguity is the current constraint on product expansion. Polymarket, which operates offshore and accepts crypto, faces the highest regulatory risk if the CFTC extends the ANPR's framework to non-registered platforms serving US users. Sports leagues (NFL, NBA) will file comments opposing sports-related contracts under the "contrary to public interest" standard — their lobbying weight is substantial and could result in a categorical prohibition on sports event contracts.

AI Governance Fractures Widen — 19 State Laws Enacted in 2026, EU High-Risk Deadline Eight Weeks Out

"The Framework's most consequential section is its recommendation for federal preemption of state AI laws that impose undue burdens, with the goal of establishing a single, minimally burdensome national standard rather than fifty discordant ones." — Consumer Finance Monitor, analysis of White House National Policy Framework for AI, April 8, 2026 [paraphrased]

Nineteen new state AI bills were enacted in 2026, with 27 more having passed both chambers awaiting signature. The Colorado AI Act is scheduled to take effect June 30, 2026, imposing developer and deployer obligations on high-risk AI systems — the first state high-risk AI law in the US. The White House National Policy Framework for AI (released March 20, 2026) explicitly recommends federal preemption of state AI laws that "impose undue burdens," a position Congress has declined to adopt in two prior sessions. The preemption conflict is now live.

In the EU, the AI Act's high-risk system rules take effect August 2, 2026 — eight weeks away. Organizations must have documented AI inventories, risk classifications, third-party due diligence protocols, and model lifecycle controls in place. High-risk categories include: biometric systems, critical infrastructure AI, employment-related AI, and credit/insurance scoring models. Fines under the AI Act reach €30M or 6% of global turnover for high-risk violations.

📎 Historical Precedent — GDPR's August 2018 effective date saw enforcement begin 18 months later; companies that treated it as a deadline rather than a starting point faced retroactive scrutiny. EU AI Act high-risk rules have no equivalent grace period for post-August enforcement.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
The US is heading toward 50 AI compliance regimes absent federal preemption — a structural advantage for large incumbents (Google, Microsoft, Anthropic) who can absorb multi-state compliance costs, and a structural barrier for startups and mid-tier AI developers. Colorado's June 30 effective date is the most immediate risk for US AI businesses — it applies to AI systems deployed to Colorado residents, regardless of where the developer is headquartered. EU AI Act high-risk compliance requires documentation infrastructure that typically takes 6-12 months to build; August 2 is not a planning date, it is an enforcement commencement date.
🏗️ Industry Implications
AI legal tech platforms (Harvey, Clio AI, Thomson Reuters CoCounsel) serving regulated industries must classify their products under EU AI Act high-risk categories by August 2. Crypto AI tools — on-chain AI oracles, AI-driven trading systems — face both the EU AI Act and emerging CFTC automated trading oversight. Financial services firms using AI for credit, AML, or fraud scoring need EU AI Act conformity assessments completed before August; NIST AI RMF alignment is the fastest compliance pathway for US-domiciled firms with EU exposure.
