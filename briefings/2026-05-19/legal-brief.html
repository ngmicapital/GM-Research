FDIC GENIUS Act Application-Procedures NPR Comment Period Closed Monday 18 May at 23:59 ET — Final Submissions Filed By ABA, ICBA, BPI, ABASA, and Industry Coalition On Application Standards For State Nonmember Banks Issuing Payment Stablecoins Through Subsidiaries; Substantive Prudential Rule (April 7 NPR) Remains The Separate Open Track

"[The proposal would treat] applications by FDIC-supervised institutions to engage, through a subsidiary, in the issuance of payment stablecoins on a substantially similar basis as the standards established for federally regulated stablecoin issuers under [the GENIUS Act] section 7." — FDIC Notice of Proposed Rulemaking, 90 FR 59409, "Approval Requirements for Issuance of Payment Stablecoins by Subsidiaries of FDIC-Supervised Insured Depository Institutions" (19 Dec 2025) [paraphrased]

The FDIC's notice of proposed rulemaking titled "Approval Requirements for Issuance of Payment Stablecoins by Subsidiaries of FDIC-Supervised Insured Depository Institutions" — published at 90 FR 59409 on 19 December 2025 — closed its comment period at 23:59 Eastern time on Monday 18 May 2026. The window was extended from the original 17 February 2026 deadline after the ABA, ICBA, Bank Policy Institute, and ABA Securities Association requested additional time on the basis that the FDIC had not yet published its companion substantive-standards rule.

The NPR is the FDIC's licensing-pathway rule under section 7 of the GENIUS Act: it establishes how an FDIC-supervised state nonmember bank or state savings association seeks approval to issue a payment stablecoin through a subsidiary. The procedural rule is structurally analogous to OCC's federal-charter pathway and is one of the six implementing rules required to operationalise the statute by the 18 July 2026 statutory finalisation deadline. The companion substantive-standards rule — published 7 April 2026 and addressing 1:1 reserves, 2-business-day redemption, capital and liquidity treatment — is on a separate comment track that does not close yesterday.

Final-day positioning was sharp. The American Bankers Association, Independent Community Bankers of America, and Bank Policy Institute filed asking for tighter activity-restriction guardrails on the issuing subsidiary (including limits on yield-equivalent payments that could substitute for deposit interest, the subject of last week's separate ABA letter to OCC). Coinbase, Circle, Ripple, and the Blockchain Association coalition filed for narrower interpretations of "substantially similar" so that state-pathway issuers retain a meaningful operational delta from federal-pathway issuers (the dual-pathway architecture the GENIUS Act explicitly preserves). The FDIC is now in drafting toward a final rule, likely Q3 2026 to meet the 18 July statutory deadline for the broader implementation package.

The dual-track structure matters: FDIC's application procedures (this NPR, closed yesterday) and FDIC's substantive standards (the 7 April NPR, separate timeline) together comprise the bank-subsidiary stablecoin issuance regime. The two must be read in conjunction. Treasury's "substantially similar" determination under section 4(c)(3) of the GENIUS Act — separately released 3 April 2026 — sets the federal/state baseline that constrains both.

📎 Precedent: OCC Interpretive Letter 1174 (Jul 2020) — first established that national banks may issue stablecoins; the GENIUS Act now statutorily federalises and conditions this authority and imposes parallel obligations on state-chartered FDIC-supervised institutions seeking to issue through subsidiaries.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The FDIC application-procedures NPR is the gating rule for the state-pathway stablecoin issuer business model that Circle, Paxos Singapore-licensed entities, and any state-chartered fintech using a state nonmember bank wrapper relies on. Per Sullivan & Cromwell client memo (15 Dec 2025), the proposal anchors the FDIC's review on the same statutory standards Treasury must apply to federal issuers — meaning state-pathway issuers face a federal-equivalent procedural floor even if state law remains nominally permissive. Per Mayer Brown's "Stable Rules for Stablecoins" alert, the binding compliance question is the activity-restriction overlay on the issuing subsidiary: any yield-equivalent payment structure that approximates a deposit-interest substitute will likely be carved out, but transaction-linked rewards remain on the table. The ABA's 13 May letter to OCC (covered in our Sunday edition) flagged exactly this loophole on the federal side; the same constituency just filed against the equivalent on the FDIC track. The FDIC's final rule will need to be co-published with the OCC's parallel final rule to avoid a federal/state arbitrage — Comptroller Gould's prior remarks signalled he expects functional harmonisation. The Brief's read: the procedural rule lands close to the NPR text with marginal industry-friendly narrowing on "substantially similar"; the binding political fight on yield-equivalent activity restrictions will be resolved in the OCC final, not the FDIC final.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Per Chapman and Cutler's GENIUS Act Rulemaking Tracker, the bear case is a sequencing failure: FDIC publishes the application-procedures final rule on time (Q3 2026) but the substantive-standards rule slips into Q4, leaving banks with a procedural pathway but no operating standards to apply for under. This was the structural risk Sullivan & Cromwell flagged in its December 2025 client memo. The opposite contrarian scenario is also live: FDIC tightens "substantially similar" interpretation in the final rule on Treasury's October 2025 prudential-equivalence guidance, making the state pathway effectively unviable and forcing all serious bank-issued stablecoin issuers onto the OCC federal-charter pathway — collapsing the dual-pathway architecture Congress explicitly designed.

Community and regional banks watching this as their own potential entry point: First Foundation, Customers Bank, Cross River — all rumoured to be evaluating subsidiary issuance pathways. Stablecoin distribution partners (Coinbase, Robinhood Crypto, Kraken, Block/Square Cash App) benefit from any rule that broadens the pool of compliant US issuers. Banking software stack (Fiserv FI, FIS, Jack Henry JKHY) absorbs the implementation engineering work either way.

SEC Chair Atkins 8 May SCSP AI+ Expo Remarks Flag "Crypto Vaults" as Next Regulatory Frontier — Commits to Innovation Exemption Formal Proposal, Onchain Trading and Settlement Rulemaking; Calls Congress to "Send the CLARITY Act to President Trump's Desk"

"I continue to echo my call for Congress to send the CLARITY Act to President Trump's desk. Because, while I intend to future-proof our efforts through notice and comment rulemaking, there is no more powerful way to future-proof than enshrining sound statutory language in law." — Chair Paul S. Atkins, SEC, Remarks at the Special Competitive Studies Project AI+ Expo, Washington DC (8 May 2026)

SEC Chair Paul Atkins's 8 May 2026 remarks at the Special Competitive Studies Project AI+ Expo previewed the operational shape of the SEC's onchain rulemaking package. Atkins named "crypto vaults" — onchain software applications designed to allow users to earn yield passively through deployment of assets into yield-generating opportunities — as a discrete category requiring SEC clarification, and tied them to a broader review of how onchain exchanges, brokers, dealers, and clearing systems function under existing securities law.

The operational sequence Atkins outlined: the SEC is "on the cusp" of releasing the Innovation Exemption formal proposal (text under White House review per Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs records); the exemption provides a 12–36 month regulatory sandbox for tokenised securities trading on-chain with volume caps, whitelisting, and periodic reporting. Beyond the exemption, Atkins committed the SEC to formal notice-and-comment rulemaking for hybrid onchain market structures rather than enforcement-first treatment — explicitly aligning the SEC posture with the Project Crypto framework rolled out at the joint SEC-CFTC event in February 2026 and the March 17 SEC-CFTC interpretive release (90 FR 33-11412).

Atkins's remarks restated his call for Congress to pass the CLARITY Act. The framing matters: Atkins is signalling that he prefers statutory clarity to administrative interpretation as the durable basis for the onchain regime, despite his own willingness to use rulemaking authority. This places the SEC on the same political side as Coinbase, Ripple, the Blockchain Association, and the 120+ firm crypto industry coalition that filed the 23 April letter pressing Senate Banking to advance the bill — and provides political cover for Senate-floor Republicans seeking bipartisan cloture votes.

Per Crowdfund Insider's coverage of the SEC Investor Advisory Committee briefing, the formal Innovation Exemption proposal exceeds 400 pages and includes safe-harbour provisions for capital raising alongside the tokenised-trading sandbox. Per Sidley Austin "Breaking Down Project Crypto" alert, the underlying conceptual framework draws directly on Commissioner Hester Peirce's prior Token Safe Harbor proposal architecture.

📎 Precedent: SEC Regulation A+ (2015) — established mini-IPO exemption with volume caps and SEC reporting; the Innovation Exemption proposal is structurally analogous but designed for onchain tokenised securities trading rather than primary capital raising.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Sidley Austin's "Breaking Down Project Crypto" alert, the operative question is whether Atkins's Innovation Exemption lands as a true 12-month sandbox with credible scale caps (preserving SEC retraction authority) or as a permissive runway that becomes de facto the regulatory regime by political inertia — the Token Safe Harbor critique Peirce previously navigated. Per Morgan Lewis "Crypto Clarity" commentary, the "crypto vaults" framing is the SEC's first explicit acknowledgement that yield-generating onchain protocols (Ethena USDe/sUSDe, Pendle, Aave Atokens, Lido stETH, Rocket Pool rETH, Spark sDAI/sUSDS) sit somewhere distinct from the five categories in the SEC-CFTC March 17 token taxonomy and require their own treatment. Per Morrison Foerster's Project Crypto memo, the political tell in Atkins's CLARITY Act endorsement is that the SEC views statutory clarity as protective: a future Democratic chair cannot easily unwind statute, but can unwind rule. The Brief's read: the formal Innovation Exemption proposal lands inside 30 days; the crypto-vaults rulemaking is on a Q4 2026 timeline. Yield-bearing DeFi protocols should expect a discrete formal regulatory question by year-end.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Per Greenberg Traurig's "SEC Clarifies Status of Crypto Assets" insight, the bear case for the Innovation Exemption is that it is structurally a regulatory question dressed as an administrative exception: the SEC cannot create new categories of registration relief by exemption alone, and the proposal will face Article I challenge from any party harmed by its operational scope (likely DeFi-protocol issuers excluded from sandbox eligibility, or competitor TradFi venues arguing competitive disadvantage). The Senators Warren/Van Hollen letter to Atkins (received March 2026) telegraphs the political opposition. The opposite contrarian: the SEC may slow-walk the formal proposal through summer 2026 in favour of Congress delivering CLARITY first, then issue the exemption as an implementing rule rather than an interpretive exception — collapsing the litigation surface area.

Tokenised treasury issuers benefit asymmetrically because their fund mechanics map more cleanly to existing securities frameworks; pure DeFi yield protocols face the steepest reclassification risk. Per Norton Rose Fulbright's "SEC and CFTC Progress" alert, expect Chainlink (LINK) to benefit from onchain-clearing rulemaking via its CCIP and Proof-of-Reserve infrastructure positioning.

High CFTC · Norton Rose Fulbright · Cleary Gottlieb · Holland & Knight [PRIMARY/SECONDARY] US · Prediction Markets / Preemption
Signal: 🟢 High — Two amicus briefs filed in two appellate venues by sitting CFTC Chair; SCOTUS-cert vehicle now substantively cued up.

CFTC Files Dual Amicus Briefs Asserting Exclusive Federal Jurisdiction Over Prediction Markets — Massachusetts SJC (24 April, Case SJC-13906) and Sixth Circuit (12 May, KalshiEx v. Schuler No. 26-3196); Operational Setup for the SCOTUS Pathway Chair Selig Floated on 6 May

"The federal district court in Ohio took an improperly narrow view of the Commission's jurisdiction, and we are asking the Court of Appeals to correct that error." — Chair Michael S. Selig, CFTC, Statement on Sixth Circuit Amicus Brief Filing in KalshiEx LLC v. Schuler, No. 26-3196 (12 May 2026)

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission filed amicus briefs in two appellate venues over the past four weeks asserting exclusive federal jurisdiction over prediction markets under the Commodity Exchange Act. The 24 April 2026 brief was filed in the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court in Commonwealth of Massachusetts v. KalshiEx LLC, SJC-13906. The 12 May 2026 brief was filed in the US Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit in KalshiEx LLC v. Matthew T. Schuler, et al., No. 26-3196, an interlocutory appeal from the Northern District of Ohio.

Both briefs advance the same doctrinal argument: Congress has established a comprehensive regulatory framework for commodity derivatives — including event-contract markets commonly referred to as prediction markets — that the CFTC administers; this federal regulatory structure preempts state laws when applied to CFTC-regulated markets, thereby establishing exclusive federal jurisdiction. Chair Michael Selig's accompanying statement on the Mass SJC filing: "Some states continue to pursue ever-escalating, illegal enforcement actions against CFTC-regulated exchanges, despite rulings from multiple courts halting those efforts."

The Mass SJC and Sixth Circuit are running on parallel and partially divergent tracks. The Sixth Circuit is reviewing a Northern District of Ohio order that the CFTC argues took "an improperly narrow view" of agency jurisdiction. The Mass SJC, by contrast, is the higher court of the Commonwealth deciding whether state gambling law applies to KalshiEx's election-event contracts under a state-court constitutional preemption analysis. A loss in either venue is potentially appealable to the Supreme Court; the structural utility of running both is that any cert vehicle benefits from circuit and state-court alignment on the same preemption question.

This builds directly on Chair Selig's 6 May 2026 interview with CryptoTimes, in which he floated the Supreme Court pathway as the operational resolution mechanism for the state-versus-CFTC prediction-markets fight. The Schiff (D-CA) and Curtis (R-UT) "Prediction Markets Are Gambling Act" companion bills remain the legislative counter-vehicle. The Ninth Circuit consolidated oral argument on 16 April in the Kalshi/Robinhood/Crypto.com cases against the Nevada Gaming Control Board is the third pending appellate forum — a decision is expected "in coming weeks" per Norton Rose Fulbright's "Prediction Markets at a Crossroads" alert.

📎 Precedent: KalshiEx, LLC v. CFTC, No. 23-3257 (D.C. Cir. 2024) — held that the CFTC's prior public-interest event-contract review framework misapplied statutory criteria; the current litigation cycle is the inverse-direction question of whether the CFTC's affirmative jurisdiction preempts state regulators.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Cleary Gottlieb's "Prediction Markets for Those Who Don't Predict" memo, the operational doctrine in play is field preemption under the Commodity Exchange Act read together with the Supremacy Clause: if the Sixth Circuit or Mass SJC accepts the CFTC's framing, state gaming and gambling laws cannot reach CFTC-regulated event contracts traded on designated contract markets. The Ohio district court's "improperly narrow" framing was that the contracts at issue were not commodity derivatives within the meaning of the CEA — a textual challenge the CFTC's brief addresses head-on. Per Holland & Knight's "Federal Appeals Court: CFTC Jurisdiction Over Sports Event Contracts Likely Exclusive" alert, the unresolved question is whether the preemption analysis differs for sports-outcome contracts (Crypto.com Nevada track) versus election-outcome contracts (Kalshi Mass track) — the doctrinal answer should be no, but the political optics differ enough that some appellate panels may distinguish them. Per Norton Rose Fulbright's analysis, the Schiff-Curtis legislative counter-vehicle is structurally weak because it asks Congress to amend the CEA's commodity-derivative definition rather than carve out a sports/election exception — a high legislative bar. The Brief's read: CFTC's dual-amicus strategy is deliberate cert-vehicle construction. Expect Supreme Court grant inside 18 months if either appellate decision aligns with the agency.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Per SBA Office of Advocacy "CFTC Prediction Markets" comment, the bear case for the CFTC posture is that the agency overreads its own jurisdiction: the CEA's "event contract" definition is narrower than the agency's recent rulemakings have assumed, and a textualist Supreme Court may rule against the agency on Chevron-deference-aftermath grounds (Loper Bright). If the Court accepts certiorari and rules against the CFTC, the agency loses categorical authority over the prediction-market category and the matter reverts to state regulators jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction. The Hyperliquid Policy Center's parallel push for decentralised-market regulatory accommodation (see Story #5) complicates the cert-vehicle calculus: if the cert grant resolves on a designated contract market, the holding may not reach decentralised onchain prediction-market protocols at all, leaving a sharper preemption gap.

🏗️ Industry Implications

CFTC-registered prediction-market venues directly benefit on a CFTC win in either appellate forum: Kalshi, Polymarket (US relaunch via QCX subsidiary), Crypto.com (sports-event contracts pathway), Robinhood event contracts, ForecastEx. Decentralised onchain prediction markets (Polymarket protocol, Augur, Limitless, Drift's prediction markets, hyperliquid HIP-3) face an asymmetric outcome: if the appellate forums confirm CFTC preemption only over designated contract markets, decentralised venues remain in regulatory limbo until the CFTC's separate ANPRM rulemaking concludes (see Story #5). Per The Block's coverage of CFTC ANPRM comments, state gaming regulators (Nevada GCB, Mass Gaming, New Jersey DGE) face an erosion of categorical authority over event contracts that fall under CFTC perimeter. Sportsbook operators (DraftKings, FanDuel/Flutter, BetMGM, Caesars) face a structural competitive question if CFTC-perimeter sports-event contracts become regulatory-arbitrage substitutes for state-licensed sportsbook product.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish CFTC-registered prediction-market venues (Robinhood HOOD prediction-market business line, Crypto.com CRO indirectly), Polymarket private valuation, regulated event-contract infrastructure.

🔴 Bearish US-listed sportsbook operators (DKNG, FLUT.L) on margin from regulated sports-event-contract substitute; state gaming regulator authority over event contracts.

Hong Kong Virtual Asset Trading Platform Dealer and Custodian Licensing Bill Targeted For Legislative Council Introduction Within 2026 — SFC and FSTB Finalise Framework As HKMA First-Batch Stablecoin Licences Move Through Final Review; 36 Applications Under Assessment

"Hong Kong plans to complete proposals to regulate virtual asset dealers and custodians and introduce the rules to the city's Legislative Council in 2026." — Joint statement, Financial Services and the Treasury Bureau (FSTB) and Securities and Futures Commission (SFC), per CoinDesk policy coverage (25 December 2025) [paraphrased]

Hong Kong's Financial Services and the Treasury Bureau and Securities and Futures Commission have publicly committed to introducing virtual-asset dealer and custodian licensing legislation at the Legislative Council in 2026. The legislation is the next perimeter-expansion step in Hong Kong's three-track regulatory architecture: (i) the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing Ordinance VATP licensing regime (live since 1 June 2023), (ii) the Stablecoins Ordinance regime (Cap. 656, in force since 1 August 2025), and (iii) the forthcoming dealer/custodian regime that closes the perimeter on OTC and custody activity that currently falls outside both regimes.

Parallel operational signal: the HKMA confirmed in mid-April that it is in the final stages of assessing 36 applications for stablecoin issuer licences under the Stablecoins Ordinance, with the first batch a "limited number" prioritising financial stability, full reserve backing, robust redemption, and AML/CFT controls. First licences had been targeted for March 2026 in Financial Secretary Paul Chan's late-February remarks; the timeline has slipped marginally but remains on Q2 issuance posture.

Stablecoin licence stack architecture: minimum financial resources of HK$25 million paid-up capital, HK$3 million liquid capital, and excess liquid capital equal to at least 12 months operating expenses. Issuance is restricted to fiat-referenced stablecoins; any issuer offering a Hong-Kong-dollar-pegged stablecoin from anywhere in the world must be HKMA-licensed. The framework is the most prescriptive issuer-side stablecoin regime among major APAC venues and is being read as the template for any future Hong-Kong-government-backed digital currency rail.

The forthcoming VATP dealer/custodian bill is expected to extend a comparable conduct, capital, and consumer-protection overlay to OTC desks (currently a regulatory gap) and pure custody providers (currently outside the AMLO VATP regime unless they also operate an exchange). Per Davis Polk's "Hong Kong's new stablecoin licensing and regulatory regime" alert, the legislative architecture mirrors the dual-statute model Hong Kong has historically used for incremental financial-services perimeter expansion.

📎 Precedent: Securities and Futures Ordinance (Cap. 571) — Hong Kong's foundational financial-services licensing framework; the VATP dealer/custodian bill is structurally analogous to the Type 1 dealing / Type 8 securities margin / Type 13 trustee custody licence architecture that SFO uses for traditional securities.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Sidley Austin's "Hong Kong Implements New Regulatory Framework for Stablecoins" alert, Hong Kong's positioning is deliberate: build the most-comprehensive stablecoin + VATP + dealer + custodian perimeter in APAC, then bid for institutional tokenisation flow against Singapore (MAS) and Tokyo (FSA). The HKMA's HK$25 million paid-up capital threshold is consciously calibrated above MAS's Singapore-licensed Major Payment Institution thresholds — Hong Kong is positioning for higher-grade institutional issuers, not retail-volume issuance. Per Norton Rose Fulbright's "China Rolls Out Updated Crypto Framework" memo, the Hong Kong perimeter and the mainland China RWA tokenisation regime are operationally distinct but increasingly designed to interoperate — Hong Kong as the institutional gateway for Greater Bay Area tokenised assets. The forthcoming VATP dealer/custodian bill closes the operational arbitrage opportunity that currently lets unlicensed OTC desks operate in Hong Kong by structuring around the exchange-only AMLO framework. The Brief's read: Hong Kong is on track to be the first APAC jurisdiction with a complete regulated stack (issuer / exchange / dealer / custodian) by mid-2027. Singapore retains the prudential-treatment edge (Group 1 framework); Hong Kong gains the perimeter-completeness edge.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Per HKMA Stablecoin Issuer Regulatory Regime page commentary, the bear case is that Hong Kong's prescriptive issuer-side framework is so high-friction that institutional issuers route through Singapore or Dubai's lighter-touch regimes instead — leaving Hong Kong with a few flagship licensees (likely the established financial-services groups: HSBC, Standard Chartered, Bank of China Hong Kong, possibly JPMorgan HK or Citi HK) and no genuine fintech-driven issuance density. The 36-application pipeline is large in absolute terms but small relative to Singapore's expected post-Group-1-framework issuance pipeline. Separately: the VATP dealer/custodian bill, if drafted to over-replicate SFO Type 1/8/13 licensing burden, could deter the OTC desks (DRW, Cumberland, B2C2 Asia, FalconX Hong Kong) it is intended to capture — pushing them to Singapore or Dubai instead.

Hyperliquid Policy Center Comment Letter Pushes CFTC Toward Functional Framework Accommodating Decentralised Prediction Markets — ANPRM Rulemaking Now in Review After 30 April Comment Close

"[We ask] the Commission to design flexible and functional regulations that can accommodate decentralized market systems and provide a legal framework for participation by US citizens in decentralized markets." — Hyperliquid Policy Center, comment letter to the CFTC on Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking, Prediction Markets, 91 FR (2026-05105), filed April 2026 [paraphrased per CryptoTimes coverage]

The Hyperliquid Policy Center submitted a comment letter to the CFTC in response to the Commission's 16 March 2026 Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking on prediction markets (Federal Register 91 FR 2026-05105), advocating for regulatory clarity that accommodates decentralised, blockchain-based platforms alongside centralised designated contract markets. The ANPRM comment period closed 30 April 2026; the CFTC is now reviewing stakeholder input to determine next steps in formal rulemaking.

The HPC letter asks the Commission to design flexible and functional regulations rather than entity-based registration rules, on the basis that the existing DCM framework cannot meaningfully be applied to a decentralised onchain protocol like Polymarket's permissionless markets, Augur, or Hyperliquid's own HIP-3 event contract architecture. The letter argues that pure entity-perimeter rulemaking would push US participants offshore and into less-regulated venues, defeating the consumer-protection objective.

This complements the parallel jurisdictional litigation track (see Story #3) but addresses a different policy question. The amicus briefs and the cert-vehicle architecture address who regulates CFTC-jurisdiction prediction markets — the CFTC or state regulators. The HPC comment letter addresses what the regulatory architecture should be within CFTC perimeter once jurisdiction is settled — entity rules or functional rules.

📎 Precedent: CFTC Concept Release on Cleared OTC Swaps (2010) — first time the CFTC engaged with functional rather than entity-based perimeter design for novel derivatives; the HPC ANPRM comment effectively asks the Commission to repeat the analytical approach for decentralised prediction markets.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Cleary Gottlieb's "Prediction Markets for Those Who Don't Predict", the binding question is whether the CFTC can functionally regulate a decentralised onchain protocol without an entity to register, examine, or sanction. The HPC letter sketches an architecture — protocol-level on-ramps (regulated KYC providers, transaction monitoring) plus market-integrity rules at the smart-contract layer — that retains CFTC enforcement leverage via the on-ramp without requiring entity registration of the protocol itself. Per SBA Office of Advocacy's CFTC Prediction Markets comment, the Schiff-Curtis legislative counter-vehicle is structurally hostile to this approach: it pre-empts CFTC functional rulemaking by amending the CEA's definitional perimeter to exclude prediction markets entirely from the commodity-derivative category. The Brief's read: CFTC functional rulemaking in this space is the regulator's preferred path but politically vulnerable on three fronts — Schiff-Curtis legislative pressure, the pending SCOTUS-cert vehicle that may resolve jurisdiction without resolving architecture, and the Article I question Sen. Warren has raised on agency exemption-style rulemaking authority.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Direct beneficiaries of a functional CFTC rule that accommodates decentralised protocols: Polymarket protocol (permissionless onchain markets), Hyperliquid (HYPE) (HIP-3 event contract architecture), Augur, Drift Protocol prediction markets, Limitless, ParaSwap prediction layer, Manifold, Kalshi onchain experiments. Per Norton Rose Fulbright's policy memo, indirect beneficiaries on the infrastructure side: Chainlink (LINK) oracle services for outcome resolution, The Graph (GRT) indexing, Pyth Network (PYTH) data feeds. Adverse outcome if CFTC defaults to entity-perimeter rulemaking: protocol operators face structural exclusion from US participants, accelerating offshore migration — likely to Cayman, BVI, or Dubai DFSA jurisdictions.

South African Reserve Bank Draft Capital Flow Management Regulations Comment Period Extended to 30 June 2026 — Crypto Asset and Cross-Border Financial Transaction Provisions Open For Industry Feedback Until T-42 Days

"[The extension allows] for broader engagement and comprehensive review of proposals, particularly regarding crypto assets and cross-border financial transactions." — National Treasury of South Africa, announcement of comment-period extension, May 2026 [paraphrased]

The South African National Treasury, working with the South African Reserve Bank, has extended the comment period on the draft Capital Flow Management Regulations from the original 18 May 2026 deadline to 30 June 2026 (T-42 days from today's brief). The proposed framework consolidates South Africa's exchange-control architecture under a single regulatory instrument and explicitly captures cross-border crypto-asset flows as a regulated category — the first time SARB has proposed a unified crypto-and-fiat capital-flow regime.

The substantive proposals: a unified declaration framework for cross-border value movement (fiat and crypto), reporting thresholds for crypto-asset transfers above ZAR 200,000 equivalent, integration with FATF Travel Rule infrastructure, and a transition pathway for existing exchange-control authorised dealers (banks) to expand their authorisation to cover crypto-asset cross-border services.

South Africa's existing FSCA crypto-asset service provider licensing regime (live since 2023) is operationally separate but functionally adjacent — the FSCA licenses domestic CASPs; the new SARB framework regulates the cross-border flow itself. The combination produces South Africa's most comprehensive crypto-regulatory stack to date.

⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

South Africa is using the Capital Flow Management framework to integrate crypto-asset cross-border flows into existing FATF-aligned exchange-control architecture. The Brief's read: the framework is likely to land near the proposed text after the 30 June close, with marginal industry-friendly threshold adjustments. South African banks (Standard Bank, ABSA, FirstRand, Nedbank) are positioned as the primary beneficiaries via authorised-dealer expansion; pure-CASP issuers (VALR, Luno, Altcoin Trader) face heightened cross-border-flow reporting and may need bank partnerships to execute large transfers.

Medium FCA · Bank of England · Skadden · White & Case · Paul Hastings [PRIMARY/SECONDARY] UK · Stablecoin Coordination
Signal: 🟡 Medium — Coordinated FCA/BoE document expected during 2026; FCA CP25/14 policy statement targeted Summer 2026.

UK Bank of England and FCA Joint Approach Document on Stablecoin Regulation Expected During 2026 — Coordinating Non-Systemic Issuance (FCA Regime) With Systemic Stablecoin Oversight (BoE Regime); FCA CP25/14 Policy Statement Targeted Summer 2026

"The Bank and the FCA will publish a joint approach document in 2026 to clarify how rules will apply in practice and support a smooth transition between regimes." — FCA Consultation Paper coverage, per Skadden "UK FCA Publishes Consultation Paper on Stablecoin Issuance and Cryptoasset Custody in the UK" advisory [paraphrased]

The Financial Conduct Authority and the Bank of England have publicly committed to publishing a joint approach document during 2026 to coordinate the two parallel UK stablecoin regimes: the FCA's non-systemic stablecoin-issuance regime (consulted on in CP25/14, published May 2025) and the Bank of England's systemic-stablecoin oversight regime (consulted on in the BoE November 2025 paper, comment period closed 10 February 2026). The FCA Policy Statement on CP25/14 is targeted for Summer 2026; the joint approach document is expected to follow once both regulators have finalised their respective policy positions.

The architecture: any UK-domiciled qualifying stablecoin (UKQS) issuer is regulated by the FCA until it crosses a systemic threshold (volume, user base, payment-system dependency), at which point the Bank of England's systemic oversight regime overlays. The joint approach document is intended to set out the operational hand-off — including who supervises during the migration window, how the issuer transitions to BoE oversight without losing FCA authorisation, and how the two regimes interact during steady-state systemic supervision.

The HM Treasury draft statutory instrument on UKQS perimeter (covered separately, comment closes Friday 22 May) is the third leg of the UK stablecoin regulatory stack: it carves UKQS payment activity out of the FSMA "dealing as principal/agent" perimeter, leaving the FCA and BoE regimes as the primary regulatory layers. Per White & Case's "Bank of England consults on regulating systemic stablecoins" alert, the policy intent across the three instruments is to deliver the most-comprehensive UK stablecoin issuer regime ahead of EU MiCA's 1 July 2026 hard cliff — explicitly positioning London as a venue for non-EU stablecoin issuers redomiciling out of MiCA-constrained EU jurisdictions.

⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Paul Hastings's "Bank of England Consults on Systemic Stablecoins" alert, the operational unknown is the systemic threshold itself: BoE has not yet published a definitive numeric (volume in circulation, share of UK payment flows) for when an FCA-authorised stablecoin crosses into BoE supervision. Practitioners are pricing the threshold around £5 billion in circulation or 5–10% of UK payment-system volume, but neither figure is in the published consultation. The Brief's read: the joint approach document is the operational document that matters more than CP25/14 itself — it sets the actual compliance pathway for issuers planning to scale through the threshold. Expected Q3 2026 publication. Until it lands, large prospective UK issuers (rumoured: HSBC GBP stablecoin pilot, Standard Chartered Zodia Markets pilot, possibly a Lloyds Banking Group offering) cannot finalise launch architecture.
