Alpha — Time & Sales

Tuesday · 19 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #25 11-min read
TL;DR

Time & Sales — "the tape" — is the print-by-print receipt of every trade that fills, classified by which side crossed the spread: bid, below bid, ask, above ask. Every advanced orderflow tool (CVD, footprint, delta) is just an aggregate of these prints — reading the raw tape is how you check whether the aggregate matches the texture underneath, and whether an apparent move is being driven, defended, or absorbed in real time.

§01
What It Is
The print stream · the original orderflow tool · the source data for everything else
Time & Sales is the live feed of every executed trade — timestamp, price, size, and crucially, which side of the spread it filled on — and it's the raw input that every aggregate orderflow indicator is built from.

Everything we have covered in this series — CVD, footprint, delta, absorption, sweep orders, large prints — is a summarisation of the same underlying data stream: the exchange's trade tape. The tape is the receipt of what actually happened, print by print, after the orderbook collided with itself. CVD aggregates it cumulatively; footprint charts bucket it by price level; delta divides it per candle. The tape itself is the un-bucketed source.

The information density of a single print is higher than any single bar. A trade record carries the timestamp (down to milliseconds), the price (which level cleared), the size (how much filled), and the aggressor side (buyer or seller crossed the spread). On a modern crypto tape you also get the price relative to the prevailing bid/ask — which is what separates a routine ask-fill from a sweep that ate two or three levels in one shot. Most retail traders look only at price and volume; the side and the level relative to the spread is where the signal lives.

There are four print classifications you will see on any serious tape. Ask — a buyer paid the ask (passive seller hit). Bid — a seller hit the bid (passive buyer hit). Above ask — a buyer swept through the best ask and the next level up; aggressive. Below bid — a seller hit the bid and the next level down; aggressive. The first two are routine market activity. The second two are urgency. Reading the tape is, before anything else, watching the ratio of routine to urgent.

The discipline is older than computers. Jesse Livermore learned tape reading at fourteen as a quote-boy in a Boston bucket shop in 1891, posting prices off the paper ticker tape. Richard Wyckoff wrote Studies in Tape Reading in 1910. Both were doing — manually, off paper — exactly what a Binance trade-feed widget does today: classify prints, judge effort against result, and infer who is forcing the issue.

If you only remember one thing The tape is the only place where price, time, size, and side coexist on the same line. Every other orderflow tool is downstream of it — most of the alpha is reading prints, not charts of prints.
§02
Mechanics
Print classification · the tick rule · the three reading dimensions
Every print is classified by where it filled relative to the prevailing bid/ask — this is the tick rule — and then read across three dimensions: speed, size, and aggression at level.

At the data level, every exchange trade record has a price and a size; some venues also send a side flag indicating whether the trade was a market-buy or a market-sell. Where the flag is missing, traders use the Lee–Ready tick rule: compare the trade price to the prevailing bid/ask quote — fills at or above the ask are classified as aggressive buys, fills at or below the bid as aggressive sells. Modern crypto feeds (Binance, Bybit, OKX, Coinbase Pro websocket trade streams) supply the side flag directly, which is more accurate than tick-rule inference.

Print classification (tick rule, simplified): if price >= prevailing_ask → aggressive buy if price <= prevailing_bid → aggressive sell if bid < price < ask → ambiguous / mid (rare on tight books) Sweep detection: if size_filled > size_at_top_of_book → swept ≥ 2 levels (a single market order eating layers) The three reading dimensions: SPEED → prints per second; rate of arrival SIZE → average and max print size; conviction AGGRESSION → ratio of above-ask / below-bid sweeps to routine bid/ask fills at the level
In crypto the tape never sleeps and never auctions — there is no open/close print, no after-hours premarket halt to reset the read. This makes the speed dimension more useful than in equities: a sudden velocity change against a flat backdrop is a real signal, not a session artefact.

The three reading dimensions — speed, size, aggression — are not independent. They modulate each other. A fast tape with small prints is retail panic or chop. A slow tape with large prints is institutional working an order, probably via TWAP/iceberg. A fast tape with large aggressive prints into a level is a directional attack on liquidity. A slow tape with repeated large prints at the same price and no price movement is absorption — someone passive is eating everything thrown at them.

The last row matters more than it looks. SMB Capital's trading floor coined the read: "was it real or fake I don't know and I don't care but what I DID NOTICE was that the prints were lethargic at that level." The presence of stacked size in the book tells you nothing — that size can be pulled. The print response at the level tells you what's actually happening. Fast and one-sided into a level = the level is being challenged for real. Lethargic into a level = the move is petering out and the bounce/rejection is in play.

§04
In Practice
Reading texture · validating levels · the entry trigger
The one rule: the tape validates the chart, not the other way around — when the chart says "level should hold" and the tape says "prints are lethargic", trust the tape.
1

Mark the Level First, Then Watch the Tape

Tape reading without a structural reference is staring into a fire hose. Pre-mark the levels that matter — session high/low, VWAP, prior POC, swing pivot, liquidation cluster — then watch the tape as price approaches. The tape's job is to answer: is this level being respected or being run? Random tape-watching produces opinions; tape-watching at a level produces trades.

2

Speed Change at the Level Is the Signal

Absolute speed is meaningless — every tape can be fast or slow depending on session. What matters is the delta: did prints accelerate or decelerate as price arrived at the level? Acceleration into a level = real challenge, side that pushed has urgency. Deceleration into a level = the push is fading, expect rejection or fade. Watch the speed change in the last 10–20 seconds before the level prints, not the level itself.

3

Spot Absorption Before It Resolves

Absorption is the highest-value print pattern the tape produces. Look for: repeated large prints at the same price (≥3 prints, ≥3–5× normal size), aggressive side that should be moving price (all at ask or all at bid), and price refusing to advance. When you see all three for 5–20 seconds, the resolution will be violent — usually against the side that was aggressing. The passive participant being absorbed is signalling they want the next move.

4

Two Prints Is the Confirmation, Not One

A single sweep print is exciting but unreliable — it could be a flush, a single algo, a fat finger. Two sweeps in the same direction within a few seconds, with the second equal or larger than the first, is the confirmation: this is a directional flow, not an accident. Wait for print #2. The cost of waiting is 5–15 seconds and a few ticks; the benefit is avoiding ~30% of false starts.

§07
Common Traps
Five ways traders blow themselves up reading the tape
The single biggest mistake: trading the tape without a level, expecting the prints to tell you direction in a vacuum — they won't.
1
Trading the Tape Without a Level

The tape is a confirmation/timing tool. It doesn't generate direction by itself in a typical regime. Traders who try to "read the tape and trade what they see" without a pre-marked structural reference end up chasing every flurry of activity, getting chopped to death by what is mostly noise. Mark levels first, watch tape second.

2
Confusing Book Size With Real Liquidity

A 100-BTC stack on the bid means nothing if it can be — and routinely is — pulled before it fills. The Level 2 book is intentions; the tape is reality. Iceberg/algo participants display tiny size and absorb continuously, while spoofers display huge size that vanishes. Trust prints (what filled), not quotes (what's promised). This is the single most expensive mistake newer crypto tape readers make.

3
Reading a Single Print as a Signal

One sweep is anecdote; two same-direction sweeps within seconds is data. Single prints can be flushes, fat fingers, or one algo crossing the book for inventory reasons. Wait for the confirmation print — it costs you 5–15 seconds and a few ticks of entry, but it removes most of the false positives. The traders who skip the confirmation print are the same ones who complain that tape reading "doesn't work in crypto".

4
Watching the Wrong Venue

Aggregated tape (or a Bookmap-style multi-venue view) tells a different story than a single-venue tape. If you watch only Binance perp tape during a US-hours move driven by Coinbase spot, you'll miss the lead. Pick the venue that's setting price for the regime — Coinbase spot during US RTH, Binance perp during Asia hours, Bybit on certain liquidation cascades — or use an aggregator. Watching Bybit tape during a Coinbase-led move is reading yesterday's news.

5
"Tape Reading Is Dead In The Algo Era" — Honest Counterpoint

This claim gets repeated in every cycle and it's overstated, but not without basis. HFT now accounts for ~73% of total market volume in tradfi and a large share of crypto perp flow — algos do generate fake size, cancel before fill, and execute via icebergs specifically to make their flow invisible to manual tape readers. The honest take: tape reading produces less standalone edge in 2026 than it did in 2006. The signal hasn't vanished, but it's noisier, and discretionary tape readers must combine it with other structural inputs (CVD, footprint, OI, liq maps) to recover the edge. As a confluence tool it's still indispensable. As a standalone alpha generator, it's harder than the Livermore biographies suggest. Read it knowing what it can and can't deliver.
