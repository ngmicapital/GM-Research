TL;DR

Between 1958 and 1976, Iceland — a nation of 200,000 people, no standing army, and a GDP built almost entirely on cod — went to war with Britain three times over fishing rights. All three times, Iceland won. The Royal Navy sent frigates; Iceland's coast guard cut their nets and fired live shells. Iceland threatened to leave NATO; the United States quietly pressured Britain to back down. What Iceland understood, and Britain never did, was that the real conflict was not about fish at all. It was about a 300-mile stretch of frigid ocean called the GIUK Gap — the only corridor Soviet submarines could use to break into the Atlantic — and whoever controlled Iceland controlled it. Britain lost control of its deep-sea fisheries. Iceland gained sovereignty over 200 nautical miles of ocean. And that 200-mile boundary, forced into existence by one small island's leverage over Cold War geography, became the global standard written into international law in 1982 — governing the waters of every coastal nation on Earth today.

Section 01

The Story: A Small Nation Picks an Unwinnable Fight — and Keeps Winning

In the early 1950s, cod was not just food in Iceland. It was the country's economic immune system. Fish products accounted for roughly 70 to 80 percent of all Icelandic export earnings. The country had no significant agriculture, almost no manufacturing, and a population smaller than many English market towns. It existed on cod the way Saudi Arabia exists on oil — which meant that when the cod stocks near shore started thinning in the post-war years, the threat was existential.

The problem was that Britain, Germany, Belgium, and other nations had been fishing Icelandic waters for centuries under the prevailing international norm: territorial waters extended only 3 nautical miles from the coast. Beyond that, the sea was open to anyone with a trawler. British deep-sea fleets working out of Hull and Grimsby had operated in those waters for generations, and they were not inclined to leave.

Iceland's first move, in 1952, was modest: extending its zone from 3 to 4 nautical miles. Britain protested but did not escalate. Then in 1958, Iceland made the move that lit the fuse — extending the zone to 12 miles. The First Cod War had begun.

1958 — First Cod War
Iceland declares a 12-mile fishing zone. Britain sends Royal Navy frigates to escort and protect British trawlers inside the zone. Skirmishes at sea, but no live fire. The conflict ends in 1961 with total British capitulation — the 12-mile limit is accepted.
1972 — Second Cod War
Iceland extends to 50 miles. Royal Navy returns. The Icelandic Coast Guard vessel Ægir deploys a purpose-built net-cutting device for the first time on 5 September 1972, slicing the trawling wires of a British vessel. The enraged crew throws coal and a fire axe at the Icelandic ship. By 1973, Iceland fires live 57mm shells at the Grimsby trawler Everton, holing her bow. Britain capitulates again under NATO pressure.
1975–1976 — Third Cod War
Iceland extends to 200 miles. Seven Royal Navy frigates escort British trawlers at peak of hostilities. Iceland briefly severs diplomatic relations with Britain — the only time in NATO's history one member has cut ties with another. The US and NATO lean hard on Britain. On 1 June 1976, Britain accepts the 200-mile limit.
1982 — UNCLOS
The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea codifies the 200-nautical-mile Exclusive Economic Zone as universal international law. The Cod Wars' outcome is embedded in the legal architecture governing every ocean on Earth.

Each round followed the same pattern: Iceland raised the stakes; Britain deployed the Navy; there were collisions, cut nets, warning shots, live fire; and then the Americans quietly told Britain to stand down. Three times. The same result each time.

"The United Kingdom had little option other than to accept Icelandic demands. American pressure over Iceland's NATO membership was a crucial reason for British capitulation in all three Cod Wars."

— US Naval War College Review, 2023

The most astonishing fact is not that Iceland won once. It is that British planners never figured out why they kept losing, and kept sending the Navy back expecting a different result. The fish were the decoy. The real game was always geopolitics.

Section 02

The Mechanism: Why a Nation With No Army Always Held the Winning Card

To understand why Iceland won, look at a map of the North Atlantic. Specifically, look at the gap between Greenland, Iceland, and the United Kingdom — the GIUK Gap. During the Cold War, Soviet nuclear submarines based on the Kola Peninsula needed to transit this passage to reach the open Atlantic, where they could threaten American cities and carrier battle groups. The gap is relatively shallow compared to the deep ocean on either side, which made it ideal for hydrophone networks, patrol aircraft, and submarine ambushes.

The GIUK Gap: What It Actually Is

The Greenland–Iceland–UK Gap is a series of three submarine choke-points in the North Atlantic. It runs roughly 1,500 miles from Greenland to Norway, with Iceland sitting at the centre. NATO built an extensive SOSUS (Sound Surveillance System) hydrophone network along the seabed here to detect Soviet submarine transits.

The US military base at Keflavik, Iceland, anchored this entire defensive system — hosting long-range patrol aircraft, early-warning radar, and anti-submarine warfare operations. Without Iceland, the gap could not be monitored effectively. Without monitoring the gap, Soviet submarine-launched ballistic missiles could reach US coastal cities within minutes of a launch order.

Iceland contributed no troops to NATO. Its entire strategic value was its geography. And its government understood this perfectly.

This created an extraordinary asymmetry of leverage. Britain had nuclear weapons, a standing army, a navy with genuine warships. Iceland had none of those things. But Iceland had something Britain could not match: the ability to destroy NATO's submarine surveillance architecture by simply withdrawing from the alliance. And Icelandic politicians — even cabinet members who were personally pro-Western — were completely willing to make that threat, because it was their only card and it worked.

When Iceland threatened during the Third Cod War to withdraw from NATO and expel American forces from Keflavik, it was not a bluff. The US Atlantic Command ran the numbers. Losing the Keflavik base and the GIUK Gap monitoring network would have required massive compensation elsewhere — at enormous cost, with no guarantee of equivalent capability. Henry Kissinger's State Department made clear to Whitehall that the fish were not worth it. Britain was to settle, immediately.

The mechanism, then, is this: Iceland had made itself strategically indispensable to a superpower. Britain, as a secondary power within that superpower's alliance structure, was subordinate. When Iceland pushed, the superpower pushed Britain. Britain could fight Iceland at sea — it was winning those individual skirmishes — but it could not fight NATO's strategic logic. There was no escalation path that led to British victory.

There is a secondary mechanism worth examining: the net-cutter itself. Iceland's coast guard developed a purpose-built device — essentially a shaped cutting piece on a long wire — that could be dragged across a trawler's stern at speed, hooking onto the fishing warps and severing them. Losing a set of trawl wires cost thousands of pounds. Losing a catch on top of that made the entire voyage uneconomical. When the Icelandic coast guard demonstrated it could reliably destroy fishing gear faster than it could be replaced, the economic calculation for individual trawler captains shifted. The Royal Navy could escort you to the fishing grounds. It could not replace your gear at sea.

Section 03

The Connections: From Cold War Cod to the Rules That Govern Every Ocean

The 200-nautical-mile Exclusive Economic Zone now defines how every coastal nation on Earth controls the waters off its shores — who can fish there, who can drill for oil, who can lay cables, who needs permission to operate. It covers 38 percent of the world's ocean surface. The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea formally enshrined it in 1982, six years after Iceland forced Britain to accept it at gunpoint (or net-cutter point, at least).

The concept had precedents: Chile and Peru had declared 200-mile zones in 1947 to protect their anchoveta fisheries. But those declarations were dismissed by major maritime powers as unilateral assertions from minor states. What Iceland did was different. Iceland forced a major power — a permanent member of the UN Security Council — to accept the 200-mile principle through sustained confrontation. That acceptance became the proof of concept that made universal adoption possible.

What Britain Lost

  • Access to Iceland's rich fishing grounds, worked by British fleets since the Middle Ages
  • The deep-sea trawler industry centred on Hull and Grimsby — effectively destroyed within a decade
  • Roughly 5,000 direct trawlerman jobs in Humberside alone, plus associated processing industries
  • Diplomatic credibility: the only NATO member to have relations severed by an ally

What the World Gained

  • The 200-mile EEZ as universal international law (UNCLOS 1982)
  • A precedent that small nations can assert maritime sovereignty against great powers
  • The framework that now governs oil, gas, and mineral rights under 38% of the ocean
  • A template for using alliance leverage as asymmetric geopolitical force

The connection to Brexit is direct and largely unacknowledged. One of the most emotional British arguments for leaving the EU was the complaint that European fishing fleets had taken access to British waters — the same waters Britain had been forced to concede in the Cod Wars era when it accepted the 200-mile EEZ principle. Brexit's promised return of "our fish" was, in structural terms, an attempt to replay the Cod Wars from the winning side. The fish themselves have not cooperated: North Sea cod stocks remain severely depleted, and British fishing communities have not recovered their pre-1976 character.

There is also a lesson in institutional design. Iceland won partly because its government and its fishing industry were tightly aligned — fishing was politically and economically central in a way it never was for Britain, where trawlermen in Hull had far less political weight than London's financial and diplomatic interests. Britain repeatedly chose compromise when confrontation would have been unsustainable; it chose the diplomatic relationship over the fishing industry every time. The Cod Wars are a case study in how a government's domestic political economy shapes its international negotiating posture — often more than its actual military capability does.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

The GIUK Gap Today

Russia's submarine fleet is active again. NATO has quietly resumed intensive GIUK Gap monitoring. The Keflavik base, run down after the Cold War ended, was reopened for US P-8 Poseidon anti-submarine patrols in 2016. The geography has not changed.

Hull After the Cod Wars

Hull's St Andrews Dock processed hundreds of trawlers a year at its peak. By the mid-1980s it was derelict. The social consequences — unemployment, depression, community collapse — were severe and largely invisible to the politicians who negotiated them away. The dock is now a heritage site.

ICGV Ægir's Net-Cutter

The device that won the Cod Wars was improvised from naval engineering concepts. The first operational cut, on 5 September 1972, was observed by the British trawler crew who responded by hurling coal and a fire axe at the Icelandic vessel. The Ægir's log records the axe as landing on deck.

Chile and Peru, 1947

The forgotten origin of the 200-mile concept: both nations declared 200-mile maritime zones to protect their anchovy fisheries from American and European fleets. The US dismissed both declarations — then watched Iceland force the same principle onto Britain thirty years later.

The Smallest NATO Member

Iceland remains the only NATO member with no standing army. It contributes its geography. The alliance's founding logic — that US extended deterrence required European bases — made Iceland's membership simultaneously cheap and irreplaceable, a structural dynamic Iceland exploited brilliantly.
