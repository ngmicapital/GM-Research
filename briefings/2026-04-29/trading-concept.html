Alpha — Liquidity Heatmaps

Wednesday · 29 April 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #7 11-min read
TL;DR

A liquidity heatmap is the order book rendered over time as a thermal grid — bright zones are heavy resting limit orders or projected liquidation clusters; dim zones are voids price punches through. The practical edge: layer the order-book heatmap (Bookmap-style — what's actually resting right now) on top of the liquidation heatmap (Coinglass-style — projected forced exits) and you have the same map whales and HFT desks read to plan stop hunts. Trade rejections at walls that hold; trade through walls that pull; treat magnet zones as targets, never as entries.

§01
What It Is
Resting orders vs projected liquidations · two different maps, often confused
A liquidity heatmap is the limit-order book painted across time and price — colour intensity scales with size, so you can see walls form, hold, pull, or get eaten without reading a single number.

Standard candles tell you where price went. The heatmap tells you what was waiting at every price along the way. On the right edge of the screen sits the live order book, vertical: bids stacked below the last trade, asks stacked above. As time advances, that snapshot scrolls left, leaving a trail. The brighter a horizontal band, the more resting size sat there. Walls show up as horizontal yellow streaks. Voids show up as black gaps. Spoofs show up as flashes that vanish before price arrives.

Two distinct visualisations both go by the name "heatmap" and they are not the same instrument — confusing them is the first beginner mistake:

Order-book heatmap (Bookmap, TradingLite, Hyblock, Multibook): renders actual resting limit orders from the live exchange feed. What you see is real, current liquidity. Walls can be pulled. Iceberg orders refill. This is the microstructure layer — what's being staged to defend or attack price right now.

Liquidation heatmap (Coinglass, CoinAnk, CounterFlow): renders projected liquidation prices for outstanding leveraged positions. It's not orders sitting in the book — it's where forced market sells/buys will fire if price reaches them. Coinglass calls these "magnetic zones" because liquidations, unlike limit orders, cannot be cancelled — once the trigger price prints, the unwind is automatic. Whales and bots know exactly where these clusters sit.

Tony, you already trade Wyckoff supply/demand structure. Heatmaps are the microstructure receipts for that structure: they show you the actual size defending the springs and upthrusts you mark. The Trading Riot crew has a useful framing — "the heatmap is a way to determine where liquidity is in the market and how liquidity-providers are behaving". That's the whole game in one sentence.

RESTING LIQUIDITYLimit orders sitting in the book waiting to be filled WALLA bright cluster of resting orders large enough to cap a move VOIDA dim, thinly-quoted price zone where price travels fast MAGNET ZONEA liquidation cluster that price tends to gravitate toward ABSORPTIONA wall that holds while aggressive size repeatedly tries to break it PULLResting size cancelled before it gets filled — typically a spoof
If you only remember one thing The heatmap is not a forecast. It's a vulnerability map. Bright bands mark where size is staged; voids mark where there's nothing to slow price down. Read it as terrain, not as a target.
§02
Mechanics
How intensity is computed · time-decay · the three heatmap families
Each pixel = (price level × time bucket). Brightness = volume of resting orders at that level during that bucket; for liquidation maps, brightness = projected liquidation notional at the trigger price.

An order-book heatmap is built from one stream: the exchange's full L2 depth feed. For every snapshot (often 10–100 times per second), the engine maps each price level's resting size to a colour ramp. The ramp is usually logarithmic — without it, a single 500 BTC wall would saturate the screen and erase every smaller order.

Order-book heatmap intensity: I(p, t) = log( Σ Vresting(p, t) ) × normalisation Where: p = price level (rounded to tick or visual bucket) t = time bucket (e.g. 100ms snapshot) Vresting = sum of bid + ask size at that level Liquidation heatmap intensity: L(p) = Σ ( leverage × position_size ) for all open positions whose liquidation price = p Magnet pressure (Coinglass framing): M(p) = L(p) × proximity_weight(price_now, p) ↑ as price approaches a cluster, the magnet strengthens
Note: order-book maps decay forward in time — each pixel is fixed once written. A wall that existed at 14:00 stays in the historical strip even after it's pulled. Liquidation maps usually re-render daily because position turnover invalidates yesterday's projections; Coinglass explicitly warns "actual liquidation amounts may be lower than the levels shown" because positions can be closed or hedged before trigger.

The three families you'll actually use have different feeds, costs, and use-cases. Pick by what you're trying to see:

FamilyToolSourceBest for
Order-book (real)Bookmap, TradingLiteLive L2 feeds (Binance, Coinbase, CME)Spotting walls, absorption, spoofs in real time

Liquidation (projected)Coinglass, CoinAnkDerivatives open interest + leverageMapping where forced-flow magnets sit

The two families do not contradict each other — they layer. A bright Bookmap wall at a price plus a bright Coinglass liquidation cluster at the same price is a conviction zone: real defenders are stacked exactly where forced flow will fire if they fail. That's the setup whales build their plays around. A bright cluster on Coinglass with a thin Bookmap profile around it is the inverse — a magnet with no defence, the kind of zone that gets visited fast and bounced from violently.

One subtlety on the order-book side: not all bright bands are honest. Spoofed walls bloom large, hold while price is far away, then pull the moment price approaches. Iceberg orders do the opposite — they look small but refill repeatedly as fills come in, leaving a horizontal smear of repeated reloads at the same price. Bookmap's iceberg tracker keys off exactly that signature: the Bookmap blog calls it "the telltale pattern of repeated refills". You can read it by eye once you've seen it once.

§04
In Practice
Four rules · one worked BTC short into the down magnet
Trade rejections at walls that hold and breaks of walls that pull; treat magnet zones as targets, not entries; always verify the heatmap at the level, not just the projection toward it.
1

Trade rejections of walls that hold

When price tags a bright wall and absorption begins (aggressive prints into the level, wall stays bright, CVD spikes but price doesn't move), fade into the wall on the next pullback. The Bookmap rule: "large liquidity is repeatedly hit but still holds" — that's your edge. Stop goes just past the wall; if it cracks, you're wrong fast.

2

Trade through walls that pull

If the wall thins or vanishes as price approaches, you have a spoof, not a defence. Trade with the move once the size is gone — the void above (or below) means thin liquidity and outsized follow-through. The signal is the disappearance, not the original size.

3

Magnet zones are targets, never entries

The biggest mistake on Coinglass is buying a long-liquidation cluster because it "looks like support". It is not support — it is forced selling waiting to fire. Use it as a target for shorts (or to scale longs out before the level), not as a long entry. The actual entry is the rejection structure that forms after price touches the magnet.

4

Verify at the level, not just toward it

A wall that looks bright from far away may be entirely fake by the time price arrives. Don't pre-commit on what the heatmap shows from 2% out. Wait for price to get within tick range and re-check what's actually still resting. Resting size is a snapshot, not a contract.

Worked example — BTC short into the 74,200 long-liq magnet
SetupBTC 76,300, broke down through 77,400 sell wall on Tuesday's session — the wall pulled before price arrived (rule 2). Down magnet at 74,200 carries ~$420M in long liquidations from the late-April leverage build.
EntryShort 76,400 on retest of broken wall (failed reclaim).
Stop76,950 — above the failed-reclaim swing high.
Target 175,000 — first defensive shelf, scale 50% off.
Target 274,200 — primary magnet, scale remaining 50%, do not hold through; magnets bounce.
R:R~1 : 4.0 to T2 (550 risk / 2,200 reward).
InvalidationReclaim of 77,000 with rebuilding wall above — short thesis is dead, wait for new structure.
Timeframe fit: heatmaps are a 1m–1h execution tool layered over 4h–1D bias. Use the order-book heatmap for entries and stops; use the liquidation heatmap for targets and bias. Don't trade the heatmap on the daily — by then the levels have already played out.

§07
Common Traps
Four mistakes · one honest "overhyped" callout
The single biggest mistake: confusing the liquidation heatmap with the order-book heatmap, and trading magnets as if they were resting limit orders.
1
Treating the liquidation heatmap as a price prediction

It is not a forecast. It is a vulnerability map. Price may visit the magnet today, in three weeks, or only after a structural shift. The Coinglass disclaimer is explicit — "actual liquidation amounts may be lower than the levels shown" — because positions can be closed or hedged before trigger. Don't size like the magnet is a guaranteed touch.

2
Trusting walls that haven't been tested

A 500 BTC wall sitting 2% away looks impressive on Bookmap. Most pull. The wall is only meaningful if it has eaten aggressive flow and held. Until then, it's furniture. Wait for the test before you trade the wall.

3
Reading the heatmap projected, not at the level

Order-book size is a real-time stream — what you see at distance is not what will be there on contact. Re-check liquidity in the final ~0.3% of approach. Pre-committing based on the projection is how traders get filled into walls that have already evaporated.

4
"Magnet certainty" is overhyped

Be honest: the trader-X meme that "price always sweeps the liquidation cluster" is selectively remembered. Magnets are visited often enough to be tradable as targets, but the base rate is well below 100% on any given timeframe. Bigger and closer magnets resolve more reliably than smaller and farther ones — but plenty of clusters expire untouched when the regime changes. Treat magnets as probabilistic targets, not destinations.
