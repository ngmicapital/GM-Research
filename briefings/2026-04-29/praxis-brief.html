01 Key Ideas & Insights
Essay · Mark Manson · The Breakthrough #206 · 27 April 2026

The Underrated Life Skill of Knowing When to Give Up

Mark Manson — author of The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F*ck and one of the more durable voices in popular philosophy over the past decade — opened his weekly newsletter on Sunday with a line lifted from Rilke that frames the entire piece, and is worth sitting with before reading anything else:

"The purpose of life is to be defeated by greater and greater things." — Rainer Maria Rilke

Manson's headline thesis is the deliberately heretical pairing: knowing when to give up is an underrated life skill. The dominant orthodoxy across self-help, sport, founder culture, and Stoic-adjacent literature is that persistence is virtue and quitting is failure. Manson's reframe is not that persistence is wrong — it is that indiscriminate persistence is the more common and more expensive error. Holding on to what isn't working consumes the budget that could have been redeployed to what would.

The two journaling prompts he offers are doing more work than they appear to:

  • What are you still holding onto that you already know isn't working?
  • What happens if you give it up?

The first question is diagnostic — it surfaces a list. The second is the more revealing one, because it forces an honest pricing of the alternative. Most "I can't quit because…" arguments dissolve under the second question. The reasons people offer for persisting are usually identity-protection, sunk-cost, or social-cost — not strategic.

The Rilke line is the operating principle, not the conclusion. To be defeated by greater and greater things requires that you stop being defeated by smaller and smaller ones. The mechanism for that is strategic abandonment — closing positions that are below the cost of capital, so the capital can be redeployed against problems large enough to be worth losing to. The discipline isn't never quitting. It's quitting fast enough to play larger games.

Manson pairs the essay with a one-week practice: let go of one thing you've been refusing to give up on. The instruction is calibrated. Not "let go of everything" — that is its own form of avoidance dressed as enlightenment. One thing. The point is to break the habit of treating all commitments as equally non-negotiable. The reader replies he excerpts confirm the pattern: people who tried it reported relief, not loss, when they let go of what they had already known was over.

Newsletter · Shane Parrish · Farnam Street · Brain Food #678 · 26 April 2026

Three Lines from Brain Food: Munger on Bad Judgment, Rockefeller on Wealth, McIlroy on Process

Shane Parrish's Brain Food #678 surfaced three quotations this week that, taken together, form a tighter argument about what high-functioning people actually optimize for than any of them does individually. The format — three "Insights," delivered with no commentary — is itself the message: high-quality thinking compresses; low-quality thinking expands. The three quotes that travelled:

"I sought good judgment mostly by collecting instances of bad judgment, then pondering ways to avoid such outcomes." — Charlie Munger

The Munger line is the inversion most practitioners miss. The default approach to developing judgment is to study examples of good judgment — the case studies of successful decisions, the autobiographies of people who got it right. Munger's lifelong method ran the other direction: maintain a personal museum of failure. Document the disasters — your own and others' — and reverse-engineer what would have prevented them. The corpus of bad judgment is larger, more concretely instructive, and less contaminated by survivorship bias than the corpus of good judgment. Inversion as a thinking tool is well-known. Inversion as a life-long collection habit is less practiced.

"Wealth isn't a distinction. If I have no other achievement to my credit than the accumulation of wealth, then I have made a poor success of my life." — John D. Rockefeller

The Rockefeller line lands harder when you remember the source. This is not a critic of wealth observing that money is hollow. This is the man who held a higher share of US GDP than any individual before or since, looking back at the achievement and rejecting it as a sufficient identity. The diagnostic is permanent: any goal that, once achieved, leaves the achiever feeling diminished was the wrong goal. Most people will not have to confront this at Rockefeller's scale. But the principle generalizes — careers, titles, follower counts, all the proxies we build lives around. The test is at the finish line, not the starting line.

"One of the things that I love is focusing on the process over the prize." — Rory McIlroy

The McIlroy line lands differently in 2026 — the year he finally won the Masters and completed the career grand slam after a decade of public near-misses. Coming from someone who has just collected the prize they spent fifteen years chasing, "process over prize" is a non-trivial admission. The prize, once obtained, was less valuable than the practice that produced it. The corollary that follows: any practice you can only sustain by promising yourself the prize is unstable. Any practice you would continue regardless of the prize is the one that compounds.

Parrish's editorial choice — these three quotes, stacked, no analysis — is the signal. Munger says study failure. Rockefeller says don't measure with money. McIlroy says love the work. Read sequentially, they form a complete decision rule for a meaningful life: collect what doesn't work, refuse the cheap proxies, and pour yourself into the process that survives the absence of the prize.

Reflection · School of Practical Philosophy · Weekly Wisdom #021 · 28 April 2026

Attention as Proper Work: Where Krishnamurti, Plato, and Mary Oliver Agree

The School of Practical Philosophy in Sydney — a quiet, multi-decade institution running introductory courses in Vedanta, Plato, and Western contemplative traditions — opened its weekly newsletter this week with a triangulation that is more interesting than its components. Three thinkers across two and a half millennia, working from utterly different traditions, converge on the same point about what makes a life worth examining:

"The ability to observe without evaluating is the highest form of intelligence." — Jiddu Krishnamurti
"The unexamined life is not worth living." — Plato, Apology 38
"To pay attention, this is our endless and proper work." — Mary Oliver

What Plato (4th century BCE), Krishnamurti (20th century), and Mary Oliver (Pulitzer-winning poet, late 20th to early 21st century) agree on, working entirely independently: attention is not a faculty among many. It is the faculty that determines whether a life happens at all. Time spent without attention is time that did not, experientially, occur. The reason a year can pass in a blur is not that it was uneventful. It is that it was unattended.

The Krishnamurti formulation is the one most useful as a working tool. Observation without evaluation is harder than it sounds — most of what we call "paying attention" is actually evaluative narration of an experience already in progress. The skill is to drop the running commentary and let the thing in front of you arrive on its own terms. Practitioners of meditation will recognize this as the core instruction. Practitioners of strategy will recognize it as the prerequisite for seeing what is actually in front of you rather than what you expected to find there.

  • The diagnostic test: Where is your attention right now? If you cannot name it, the answer is "nowhere," and the day you are in is leaking out the side.
  • The micro-practice: When attention drifts, notice without self-criticism, and return. The drift is not failure. The noticing is the practice.
  • The orientation: Treat attention as the substrate of every other capacity — focus, presence, judgment, love. They all run on it. None of them run without it.

The corollary in 2026 is sharper than at any point in the previous twenty centuries. The most expensive hour of the day for the average professional is not the one billed at the highest rate — it is the one shredded across notifications, dashboards, and ambient anxiety. Most of those hours are unrecoverable, not because the time is gone, but because no attention was ever paid to what occupied them. The practitioner question that follows: which of my hours today were attended, and which were merely consumed? The honest answer is the diagnostic.

02 Strategy & Practice
Long-Form · Dr. Tommy Wood × Tim Ferriss · tim.blog · 24 April 2026

How to Keep Your Brain Sharp: The Practical Playbook Beyond the Basics

Dr. Tommy Wood — Associate Professor of Pediatrics and Neuroscience at the University of Washington, with a research program built around modifiable risk factors for dementia — published a long-form guest post on Tim Ferriss's blog last week that pushes well past the standard "exercise, sleep, Mediterranean diet" advice. The piece is a tour of what the actual evidence shows once you exhaust the obvious. Six elements stand out as the most practical and least-discussed:

  • B-vitamins and Omega-3s as a pair, not independently. Wood's emphasis: the studies showing meaningful slowing of brain aging required both in combination. Either alone underperforms. The lab numbers to track are homocysteine (B-vitamin status) and omega-3 index. Treat them as a single intervention.
  • Processing-speed training via gameplay. The 20-year ACTIVE study showed processing-speed training reduces dementia risk. The applied form: Super Mario, StarCraft, and similar games train the same circuits. This is not novelty advice — it is one of the few cognitive interventions with two-decade follow-up data.
  • Oral health as a stealth lever. Periodontal pathogens have been recovered from the brains of Alzheimer's patients. The intervention is unfashionably simple: floss daily, treat gum inflammation seriously. The cost is trivial; the upside, if the model holds, is large.
  • The shingles vaccine signal. Multiple large epidemiological studies now show shingles (zoster) vaccination is associated with significantly lower dementia incidence — likely because herpes-family viruses are stealth contributors to neuroinflammation. This is one of the strongest non-trivial findings in dementia prevention of the past five years and remains under-discussed in mainstream brain-health content.
  • Sensory loss as cumulative cognitive risk. Untreated hearing loss and vision impairment compound dementia risk. Hearing aids are the single most cost-effective intervention available for adults over 60. The vanity friction is the real obstacle, not the cost.
  • Glycine for sleep architecture. 3g of glycine at bedtime improves slow-wave sleep without sedation. Wood treats sleep quality as the highest-leverage modifiable risk factor for dementia and treats glycine as one of the few sleep aids with both evidence and a clean safety profile.

The strategic frame: brain health is not won by maxing any single intervention. It is won by stacking eight to ten high-evidence, low-friction interventions and running them concurrently for thirty years. Wood's playbook is most valuable as a checklist for what most practitioners are not doing — flossing, omega-3 plus B-vitamin pairing, the shingles vaccine, glycine — rather than as instruction on what they already are.

The full post — "How to Keep Your Brain Sharp: A Practical Playbook Beyond the Basics" — is at tim.blog/2026/04/24/how-to-keep-your-brain-sharp/. Wood's book, The Stimulated Mind, is the long-form version of the same argument.

Podcast · Cathy Lanier × Tim Ferriss · The Tim Ferriss Show #862 · 23 April 2026

Cathy Lanier on Operating Under Extreme, Sustained Pressure

Cathy Lanier — Chief Security Officer of the NFL since 2016, the longest-serving Chief of Police in Washington DC's history (2007–2016), and a 9th-grade dropout who built two consecutive careers from that starting point — sat down with Tim Ferriss for a conversation that, on its surface, is about NFL security operations but is functionally a master class in operating under pressure that does not stop. Three transferable principles for any practitioner whose work routinely intersects with high-stakes, high-uncertainty decisions:

  • Decision velocity matters more than decision quality, up to a point. Lanier's framing of running an NFL Sunday — 32 stadiums, 17 simultaneous games, multiple credible threats per week — is that the cost of a 95% decision made in 30 seconds is almost always lower than the cost of a 99% decision made in 30 minutes. The corollary: the discipline of making good-enough decisions fast is more learnable than the discipline of making perfect decisions slowly.
  • Pre-decided protocols beat real-time judgment in crisis. Lanier's stadium playbooks are specific to a fault — exact thresholds for evacuation, exact escalation paths, exact decision rights. The reason is not bureaucratic. It is that judgment degrades under acute stress; protocols don't. The work of a security leader is largely the work of pre-deciding before the crisis, so judgment is reserved for the genuinely novel.
  • Reducing crime in DC by 21% while population grew 15% — Lanier's record from 2007–2015 — was driven, in her telling, less by tactics and more by relentless presence. She walked her city. She knew her commanders. She knew the corners. Most large operational improvements are not algorithmic; they come from senior leaders being physically present in the work at a frequency their peers will not match.

The Lanier biography — dropout to two of the highest-stakes security jobs in America — is a useful counterweight to the credential-obsessed reading of executive ability. The frequently-cited variable across her career is not credentialing. It is sustained tolerance for sustained pressure, paired with a willingness to be the person who makes the call when no one else will. Both are trainable. Neither is taught in any standard curriculum.

04 On the Horizon
Philosophy / Practice

"Strategic Quitting" Is Quietly Becoming the Productivity Counter-Movement of 2026

Manson's piece this week is one of three high-signal essays in the past two months arguing for the same heretical position: indiscriminate persistence is a more common failure mode than premature abandonment. Annie Duke's Quit (2022) is now in its third printing surge; Seth Godin's The Dip is being recirculated; and Manson's framing aligns with a wave of founder-side content arguing that the right time to shut down most projects is sooner than the founder believes. The countercurrent matters because the dominant orthodoxy in startup, sport, and self-help has been "never quit" for fifteen years. When that orthodoxy starts to crack — and the cracks are visible now — the reflexive virtue of grit becomes a more contested category. Watch for "strategic quitting" as a published framework with its own vocabulary by year-end.

Brain Health / Longevity

The Shingles Vaccine — Dementia Link Is About to Move From Footnote to Headline

Wood's emphasis on the shingles (zoster) vaccine in his brain-health playbook reflects a wave of epidemiological evidence that has accumulated quietly over the past two years. The 2024 Welsh "natural experiment" study (Eyting et al., Nature) found shingles vaccination reduced dementia incidence by ~20% over seven years; multiple replication studies in 2025–2026 have reinforced the signal. The mechanism is plausible — herpes-family viruses including VZV are increasingly implicated in chronic neuroinflammation. If the next wave of randomized data confirms the effect, the shingles vaccine becomes the largest population-scale dementia-prevention tool currently available. Watch for the FDA, NICE, and major guideline bodies to formally update recommendations within 12–18 months. The vaccine is currently recommended for adults 50+; the prevention case may justify younger uptake.

Attention / Discourse

Attention as the Scarcest Resource — Now Being Priced as Such

The convergence of Plato, Krishnamurti, and Mary Oliver on attention-as-proper-work, surfaced this week by the School of Practical Philosophy, lands differently in 2026 than at any prior point. The attention-economy framing is no longer a critic's complaint — it is the working assumption of the firms competing for it. The structural shift now visible: "attention hygiene" is becoming a stand-alone category, with paid services, dedicated devices (Light Phone, reMarkable), residential retreats, and corporate policies all targeting it. Watch for the first major employer to formally measure and report aggregate "attended hours" per employee as a productivity metric, distinct from "logged hours." The leading edge of this is already visible in legal and consulting firms experimenting with deep-work mandates. Once a Fortune 500 publishes the metric, the category is permanent.

Operating Discipline

Pre-Decided Protocols Are Replacing Real-Time Judgment in High-Stakes Operations

Lanier's framing — that the work of a security leader is largely pre-deciding before the crisis arrives — is the same logic emerging across enterprise risk, AI safety, and regulated industries. The pattern: in any environment where decisions must be made under acute time pressure, the leader who has rehearsed the decision tree in advance outperforms the leader who has higher peacetime IQ but is reasoning from scratch in the moment. Anduril's playbook, JPMorgan's incident response posture, and the FAA's safety culture all converge on the same insight. Expect "decision pre-commitment" frameworks (decision journals, pre-mortem rituals, escalation protocols documented at the org chart level) to migrate from defense and aviation into mainstream corporate operations over the next 24 months. The companies that adopt early will be visibly more decisive in the next downturn.

Inversion / Mental Models

Munger's "Collect Bad Judgment" Method Deserves a Modern Revival

Charlie Munger's articulated method — that good judgment was acquired largely by collecting and studying bad judgment — is one of the more under-implemented mental tools in popular circulation. Most "lessons learned" databases inside companies are checkbox exercises. A genuine personal failure museum — written, dated, structured by inversion — is rare. With Munger's death in late 2023 and the recent wave of biographical work (Janet Lowe's updated Damn Right!, the Daily Journal compilations), the method is overdue for systematized treatment. Watch for the first credible "inversion-as-practice" book — likely from a behavioural economist or decision scientist — to formalize the workflow within 12–18 months. The market exists; the product does not yet.

Cognitive Training

Game-Based Processing-Speed Training Is The Sleeper Cognitive Intervention

Wood's reference to the 20-year ACTIVE study and the practical translation — Super Mario, StarCraft, and similar games — is one of the few cognitive interventions with multi-decade follow-up showing reduced dementia incidence. The mechanism is processing speed, which appears to be a more modifiable trait than working memory or fluid intelligence. The implication for the cognitive-training market: the path is not Lumosity-style brain games marketed to seniors, which have repeatedly failed to translate to real-world outcomes. The path is real games with real demands on real-time decision-making, repurposed as cognitive maintenance. Watch for the first consumer brand to position a competitive video game (or VR equivalent) as evidence-backed cognitive maintenance — and for insurance companies to take the model seriously by 2027–2028.
