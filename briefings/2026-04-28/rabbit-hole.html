TL;DR

Fritz Haber was a German Jewish chemist who, in 1909, solved one of the oldest problems in agriculture: how to pull nitrogen from the air and fix it into a form plants could eat. The Haber-Bosch process that resulted now produces the synthetic fertilizer that feeds roughly half the human race — approximately four billion people alive today would not exist without it. The same man then personally oversaw the world's first large-scale chemical weapons attack, at Ypres in April 1915. His wife, one of Germany's first female PhD chemists, called his weapons work "a perversion of science" and shot herself in the heart with his army pistol days after the attack. Haber won the Nobel Prize in 1918 amid calls for his prosecution as a war criminal. He died in exile after the Nazis forced him out, despite a lifetime of fervent German nationalism. The pesticide company he helped found later supplied Zyklon B to the death camps — where members of his own family were murdered. He is probably the clearest case history ever produced of a single human being being responsible simultaneously for more lives saved and more deaths caused than almost anyone who ever lived.

Section 01

The Story — The Chemist Who Cracked the Problem That Was Starving the World

By the late 19th century, a specific biological constraint was emerging as the hard ceiling on human population. Plants need nitrogen to build proteins and DNA. Nitrogen makes up 78 percent of Earth's atmosphere. But atmospheric nitrogen — two atoms bonded together in a molecule called N₂ — is almost inert. Plants cannot use it. The only natural sources of plant-available nitrogen were guano deposits, lightning strikes that split N₂ into reactive compounds, and certain soil bacteria that could fix nitrogen biologically. None of these were scaling fast enough. Agronomists calculated that without new nitrogen sources, global food production would peak and then collapse under rising population pressure. The chemist William Crookes told the British Association in 1898 that civilisation faced "a life and death question" — a nitrogen famine within decades unless chemistry found a solution.

Fritz Haber was born in Breslau (now Wrocław, Poland) in 1868, the son of a Jewish dye merchant. He converted to Christianity as a young man — part of a pattern of assimilation common among German Jews who believed integration was both possible and prudent — and built a career in physical chemistry, moving to the Technische Hochschule in Karlsruhe in 1894. His early work was competent but not transformative. The nitrogen problem, which he turned to in the first decade of the 20th century, would change that.

The chemistry was known. Ammonia — NH₃, one nitrogen atom bonded to three hydrogen atoms — is highly reactive and easily converted to the nitrates plants need. The problem was that synthesising ammonia from nitrogen gas requires breaking the N≡N triple bond, one of the strongest bonds in chemistry. It takes enormous energy to do it. Haber worked out that it could be done under high pressure and high temperature, with an iron catalyst — but the challenge was finding conditions where the reaction would proceed fast enough to be useful without destroying the equipment or the catalyst.

In July 1909, at a laboratory at BASF's facilities in Ludwigshafen, Haber demonstrated the process at small scale: nitrogen and hydrogen fed over an iron catalyst at around 200 atmospheres pressure and 500°C, producing a steady drip of liquid ammonia. BASF engineer Carl Bosch then spent four years solving the industrial engineering problem — building reactors that could hold 200 atmospheres of pressure at scale without exploding, finding catalysts that lasted long enough to be economical, working out the logistics of a continuous industrial process. By 1913, the first commercial plant was operating at Oppau, Germany. By 1914, it was producing 20 tonnes of fixed nitrogen per day.

"Haber took nitrogen from the air and turned it into bread."

— Common summary among historians of science and agriculture

The scale of what followed is difficult to convey. Before Haber-Bosch, global food production was constrained by natural nitrogen cycles. After it, the constraint was lifted. Synthetic nitrogen fertilizer allowed crop yields to increase on existing farmland rather than requiring ever-expanding acreage. The global population in 1909 was about 1.7 billion. It is now roughly 8.2 billion. The gap — approximately 4 billion people, though estimates vary between 3 and 4 billion — represents human lives that could not have been fed without Haber's process. About half the nitrogen atoms in the average human body today, embedded in every protein and every strand of DNA, passed through a Haber-Bosch plant.

Haber received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1918, specifically for nitrogen synthesis. He was not present in Stockholm to collect it — Germany was at war, and Haber was otherwise occupied. The war had given his talents an entirely different application.

Section 02

The Mechanism — How Nitrogen Is Pulled from Air, and How a Chemist Turns Science into Weaponry

The Haber-Bosch reaction is conceptually simple and technically extraordinary. In the atmosphere, nitrogen exists as N₂ — two nitrogen atoms triple-bonded to each other. That triple bond stores roughly 945 kilojoules per mole of energy. Breaking it requires forcing the molecule to collide with a catalyst surface at extreme conditions. Haber found that iron, doped with small amounts of potassium and aluminium oxide as promoters, could do the job — but only at around 400–500°C and pressures of 150–300 atmospheres.

The Reaction

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

One molecule of nitrogen gas plus three molecules of hydrogen gas yields two molecules of ammonia. The reaction is reversible — at equilibrium, only a fraction of the gas converts to ammonia. The trick is to remove the ammonia continuously (it liquefies under pressure), shifting the equilibrium and driving the reaction forward. Carl Bosch's engineering genius was building steel reactors that could survive 200 atmospheres at 500°C for months without failure.

The hydrogen itself had to be produced — originally from water gas reactions using coal. Today, most Haber-Bosch plants use natural gas, which means the process consumes roughly 1–2% of global energy supply annually and accounts for about 1.8% of global CO₂ emissions.

The same chemical instinct — deep knowledge of nitrogen chemistry, industrial-scale thinking, and a willingness to convert laboratory findings into field deployment — drove Haber's second great project. When the First World War began in August 1914, the conflict was not going the way Germany had planned. The anticipated short war had become a grinding stalemate in the trenches. Haber, intensely patriotic in the way of assimilated German Jews who had worked hard to be seen as German first, offered his services to the military.

He proposed using chlorine gas as a weapon. Chlorine was a known industrial chemical — BASF and other German chemical companies produced it at scale. Released in sufficient concentration, it destroys lung tissue, filling airways with fluid. Soldiers in open ground could run from artillery; they could not run from a cloud drifting toward them on the wind. Haber designed the delivery system: pressurized cylinders buried along the front line, opened simultaneously to release a wall of gas downwind into Allied trenches.

On April 22, 1915, near the Belgian town of Ypres, German troops opened 5,730 cylinders containing 150 tonnes of chlorine along a 6-kilometre front. The yellowish-green cloud drifted into the Allied lines. French and Algerian troops, who had never encountered this before, had no protective equipment, no doctrine, no warning. The gas killed approximately 1,000 and injured 5,000 more. Haber was present at the front, personally overseeing the operation.

"What he was doing was a perversion of the ideals of science and a sign of barbarity, corrupting the very discipline which ought to bring new insights into life."

— Clara Immerwahr Haber, on her husband's chemical weapons work

His wife Clara was one of Germany's first female chemistry PhDs — she had earned her doctorate from the University of Breslau in 1900 at a time when women were barely tolerated in German universities. She had married Haber in 1901 and spent years subordinating her own research to assist his career. When he turned to chemical weapons, she was appalled. She argued that science had an obligation to promote life, not destroy it, and that weaponising chemistry was a fundamental betrayal of the discipline. Her protests had no effect. Haber returned from the Ypres attack on the night of May 1–2, 1915, to their home in Berlin-Dahlem, where a reception was held in his honour.

That night, Clara took his service pistol into the garden and shot herself in the heart. Their twelve-year-old son Hermann found her dying. She died early in the morning of May 2. Haber left for the Eastern Front later that day to supervise another chlorine attack on Russian positions.

Section 03

The Connections — Nationalism, Exile, and the Poison That Circled Back

The moral architecture of Haber's life resists easy resolution. He was not a cynical man, and he was not indifferent to ethics — he had a coherent, if disturbing, framework. He believed that chemical weapons would shorten the war and save German lives. He compared them favorably to artillery, which also killed at range without personal contact. "Death is death," he reportedly said, "whether caused by gas or explosion." He also believed, with total sincerity, that Germany was fighting a defensive war of national survival — and he was a German before he was anything else.

That nationalism did not survive contact with the political reality of the 1930s. Haber had converted to Christianity, served Germany in its most desperate hour, won the Nobel Prize in its name, and directed one of its premier scientific institutions — the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Physical Chemistry. He had spent his professional life demonstrating, by his very existence, that Jews could be German. When Adolf Hitler came to power in January 1933, the Nazi government's new civil service laws required the dismissal of Jews from state positions. Haber's institute was a state institution. He was ordered to dismiss his Jewish colleagues.

He refused — then resigned. He wrote to the ministry that he had "founded [his] selection of collaborators on their scientific accomplishments and character alone, and not on the basis of their national origin." He accepted that he would have to leave. The man who had helped Germany win a war — or at least not lose it sooner — was driven into exile by the regime that claimed to be Germany's highest expression.

  • Forced resignation under Nazi race laws, 1933

  • His relatives killed in Auschwitz with Zyklon B

Haber spent his final year in exile — first in Cambridge, where the scientific community was cool toward him (both because of his chemical weapons work and, among some, because of lingering anti-German sentiment), then briefly in Spain and Switzerland. He was offered a position at the Weizmann Institute in what is now Israel, a painful irony for a man who had spent his life fleeing the identity now being offered as his only refuge. He died of heart failure on January 29, 1934, in a hotel in Basel, Switzerland, aged 65, on his way to take up the position.

The deepest irony came later, and posthumously. During his career, Haber had helped found a pest-control company called Deutsche Gesellschaft für Schädlingsbekämpfung — commonly abbreviated as Degesch. The company developed a series of fumigation compounds under the Zyklon brand. Zyklon A, which Haber's researchers worked on directly, released hydrogen cyanide, an extremely effective fumigant. Zyklon B was a derivative product — hydrogen cyanide adsorbed onto pellets, with a stabilizer added to slow release and enable controlled dosing. Between 1942 and 1945, the SS used Zyklon B at Auschwitz-Birkenau and other camps to murder approximately 1.1 million people — the majority of them Jewish. Among the victims were members of Fritz Haber's own family.

The man who had poisoned thousands with chlorine at Ypres in service of Germany; whose wife died in protest of his work; who was expelled by the country he had served; who died alone in a Swiss hotel — had also, through the commercial ventures his chemistry enabled, provided the specific instrument used to murder his own people. The feedback loop closes in a way that no morality play would dare to write.

The question Haber's life poses is not whether he was a good man or a bad man — the categories dissolve under the weight of the evidence. It is the harder question: how do you weigh four billion lives against tens of thousands of deaths in gas attacks, against the murder of his own family by his own chemistry, against the wife who died in his garden? There is no unit of moral account that handles this arithmetic. Haber is history's most extreme instance of the dual-use problem — the fact that the same knowledge, the same industrial capacity, the same intelligence that can feed the world can also be pointed in the opposite direction. He did not separate these things. He was, in both senses at once.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

Clara Immerwahr — Science's Forgotten Protester

Clara was one of Germany's first female chemistry PhDs, who subordinated her own research career to support Haber's. Her suicide note was never found. German feminist groups reclaimed her in the 1980s as a martyr figure; the Fritz Haber Institute in Berlin now has an annual Clara Immerwahr lecture series — named for the woman who protested it most bitterly.

The Nitrogen Trap We Can't Escape

Haber-Bosch now consumes 1–2% of global energy and produces 1.8% of global CO₂ emissions annually. Without it, roughly half the human race cannot be fed. We are locked in: scaling back synthetic nitrogen fertilizer without first developing alternatives would cause famines affecting billions. Haber's gift is also an obligation with no obvious exit.

The Degesch Connection and Zyklon B

Haber directly worked on Zyklon A (hydrogen cyanide-based fumigant) as part of his pest-control research. Degesch, the company that commercialised these compounds, was partly owned by IG Farben — which was itself built on BASF, the firm that first industrialised his ammonia process. The institutional chain linking Haber's chemistry to the Holocaust is direct, though he died before Zyklon B was weaponised.

The Nobel Scandal

The 1918 Nobel Prize for Haber caused an international furore — the announcement came months after the Armistice, when Allied governments were still trying to prosecute German war criminals. British and French scientists publicly refused to recognise the award. Haber never delivered his Nobel lecture in person. The prize committee has never apologised or withdrawn it.

His Son Hermann's Fate

Hermann Haber, who found his mother dying as a twelve-year-old, emigrated to the United States. Unable to escape the shadow of his father's legacy, he died by suicide in 1946. He had spent his life between two impossible inheritances: the man who fed the world and the man who gassed it — and who Clara Immerwahr died to protest.
