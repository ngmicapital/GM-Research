PACE Act Would Plug Crypto and Fintech Firms Directly Into Fed Payment Rails — Bipartisan Bill Threatens Bank Toll Bridge on FedNow, Fedwire, FedACH
"Rep. Kim's PACE Act would create an optional federal supervisory framework for nonbank payment service providers administered by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, streamlining federal registration for 'qualified payment companies' while requiring 1:1 reserves, risk and recordkeeping standards." [paraphrased — official press release] — Office of Rep. Young Kim (R-CA), 21 April 2026

Representatives Young Kim (R-CA) and Sam Liccardo (D-CA) on 21 April introduced the Payments Access and Consumer Efficiency (PACE) Act — a bipartisan bill that would, for the first time in the modern banking era, permit qualified nonbank payment providers to obtain direct access to Federal Reserve payment rails: Fedwire, FedNow, and FedACH. The measure has drawn rapid endorsement from the Financial Technology Association, the Blockchain Association, The Digital Chamber, and the Crypto Council for Innovation.

The bill creates an optional federal supervisory framework administered by the OCC for "qualified payment companies." Eligible firms must hold customer funds 1:1 in reserve, meet minimum capital, risk, and recordkeeping standards, and submit to OCC examination. Crucially, the bill streamlines federal registration in a way that could eliminate the patchwork of 49 state money-transmitter licences for participating firms — a regulatory cliff the industry has fought for two decades.

The economic stakes are concrete. Nonbank payment providers currently access Fed rails only via correspondent bank relationships, which industry advocates say layer markups of up to 100 times the Fed's per-item fee. Stripping that toll would reset the cost structure for stablecoin issuers, neo-banks, and crypto on/off-ramps overnight. Ripple and Circle are the named beneficiaries in industry analyses, but Coinbase, Kraken, Robinhood, Block, and Stripe all stand to gain.

The bill has been referred to the House Financial Services Committee, where Chair French Hill — already shepherding the CLARITY Act — now controls a parallel infrastructure bill that legacy banks will fight as aggressively as they fought the GENIUS Act stablecoin yield ban. ABA and Bank Policy Institute opposition is a near-certainty.

📎 Historical precedent: The Federal Reserve's 2021 master account guidance and Custodia Bank's 2024 D.C. Circuit loss codified the central-bank policy that nonbanks cannot access the rails. PACE inverts that posture by statute.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

This is the most consequential payments bill of the 119th Congress for crypto. The legal mechanism — opt-in OCC supervision in lieu of state money-transmitter licensing — mirrors how the OCC's special-purpose national bank charter was meant to work before NYDFS killed it in Lacewell v. OCC (2nd Cir. 2021). The PACE Act sidesteps that case by limiting the carve-out to payments activity, not lending. Winners: Circle, Ripple, Paxos, USDC issuers, and any stablecoin issuer that obtains a GENIUS Act PPSI permit — they become functionally bank-equivalent for settlement. Losers: correspondent banks (Customers Bank, Cross River, Bank of Nevada) whose entire business model is selling Fed access at 100× markup. Watch for state AG opposition led by NY and California Treasurer's Office on consumer-protection grounds.

🔄 Contrarian Take

PACE looks bipartisan on paper — but Senate Banking Democrats just held their nose to vote out the GENIUS Act and have signalled they will not stack a second crypto-friendly payments bill in the same session. The bill's odds of clearing the Senate before the August recess are sub-30%. More likely path: provisions get folded into a year-end omnibus or quietly grafted onto the CLARITY Act conference report. The press release victory is the cap-table value, not the legislation itself.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Stablecoin issuers (Circle, Paxos, Ripple/RLUSD): direct Fedwire access erodes the rationale for partner-bank relationships and slashes settlement costs. Crypto exchanges (Coinbase, Kraken, Gemini): 24/7 USD settlement without bank intermediaries; competitive moat against Robinhood collapses. Custodian banks (BNY, State Street, Northern Trust): tokenisation revenue at risk if RWA settlement bypasses them entirely. Correspondent banks (Customers, Cross River, Western Alliance): direct existential threat — their crypto-payments revenue line evaporates. Fintech (Block, Stripe, Wise, Robinhood): margin expansion of 30-80bps on the payments leg.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish CRCL, COIN, HOOD, SQ, stablecoin TVL infrastructure tokens.

🔴 Bearish Customers Bancorp (CUBI), Cross River parent (private), regional banks with crypto-payments exposure.

⚪ Neutral JPM, BAC, C — large diversified banks insulated by scale.

Hong Kong SFC Authorises 24/7 Secondary Trading of Tokenised Funds on Licensed VATPs — Regulated Stablecoins Cleared as Settlement Currency, Money Market Funds First
"The new circular permits 24/7 secondary trading on SFC-licensed virtual asset trading platforms and authorises the use of regulated stablecoins issued under the Stablecoins Ordinance, as well as tokenised deposits, to facilitate round-the-clock settlement." [paraphrased — SFC Press Release 26PR59, original quote unavailable in published English summary] — Hong Kong SFC, Circular dated 20 April 2026

The Hong Kong Securities and Futures Commission on 20 April issued a circular establishing a pilot regulatory framework permitting secondary trading of SFC-authorised tokenised investment products on licensed virtual asset trading platforms (VATPs). It is the first time any Asia-Pacific regulator has formally licensed 24/7 trading of tokenised mutual funds with regulated stablecoin settlement — and it lands a full 14 months before MiCA's tokenised-securities regime is even contemplated to clarify equivalent activity in the EU.

Tokenised money market funds are the inaugural eligible product. The SFC will monitor their performance before expanding to bond funds, equity funds, ETFs, and alternative investment funds. Settlement may be effected in (a) regulated fiat-referenced stablecoins issued under licence per the Stablecoins Ordinance, or (b) tokenised bank deposits — closing the on-chain/off-chain settlement gap that has held back institutional tokenisation projects globally.

As of March 2026, Hong Kong has 13 SFC-authorised tokenised products with AUM of HK$10.7 billion — a roughly sevenfold increase year-over-year. Major banks including HSBC, Standard Chartered, ICBC Asia, BOC HK, and asset managers including ChinaAMC, Harvest Global, and Bosera are in active pilot. The framework also gives Hong Kong a clear early-mover advantage in the regional race against Singapore's MAS sandbox (announced 24 April) and Japan's reclassified FIEA crypto regime.

One critical caveat: the HKMA's promised first stablecoin licences under the Stablecoins Ordinance — originally targeted for March 2026 — still have not been issued. Until they are, only USDT/USDC equivalents that obtain the licence can be used as settlement, creating a chicken-and-egg compliance gap that the SFC framework cannot resolve unilaterally.

📎 Historical precedent: Project Guardian (MAS, 2022) and Project Mariana (BIS, 2023) demonstrated tokenised-deposit settlement in regulatory sandboxes — Hong Kong is the first jurisdiction to licence it for retail-distributed product.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

This is APAC's regulatory-arbitrage moat moment. Hong Kong now offers what no other major financial centre offers: a fully licensed venue for 24/7 trading of tokenised UCITS-equivalent funds with stablecoin settlement, anchored to a Type 1/Type 7 SFC licence and HKMA AML supervision. Winners: HashKey Exchange, OSL, OSL Digital Securities (the three SFC-licensed VATPs), HSBC's Orion platform, and any global asset manager that lists on these venues first. Losers: Singapore MAS — which has expanded its sandbox but not licensed retail secondary trading; the EU's MiFID II/DLT Pilot regime, which sunsets in 2026 with no clear replacement. Critical legal question: whether SFC will accept overseas-domiciled tokenised funds (e.g., Cayman, Luxembourg) as "authorised" under Section 104 of the SFO, or restrict to HK-domiciled vehicles only.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The framework is a phantom until HKMA issues actual stablecoin licences. With March missed and no public timeline for May or June, retail VATPs will end up trading tokenised MMFs with USD-settled bank-rail settlement — not stablecoins — for at least Q2 and possibly Q3 2026. Headline win, on-the-ground reality: the pilot operates without its core feature. The first HKMA stablecoin licence date is the only headline that matters now.

🏗️ Industry Implications

VATPs (HashKey, OSL, OSL Digital Securities): AUM and fee revenue inflection — these venues become the only licensed retail tokenisation platforms in Asia. Banks (HSBC Orion, Standard Chartered, ICBC Asia): the tokenised-deposit settlement option preserves their settlement franchise against stablecoin disintermediation. Asset managers (ChinaAMC, Bosera, Harvest, BlackRock, Franklin Templeton): on-chain MMF distribution at retail scale; expect Asia-domiciled BUIDL/BENJI competitors. Stablecoin aspirants (Circle, Tether, Paxos, Animoca-backed HKDR): race to be first HKMA licensee. Singapore (MAS): material competitive risk on the institutional product launch race.

🟢 Bullish HSBC (HSBA.L / 0005.HK), HashKey ecosystem tokens, BlackRock (BLK), Franklin Templeton (BEN), tokenised-MMF infrastructure plays.

UPDATE: EU AI Act Digital Omnibus Trilogue Day 2 Today (28 April) — Political Agreement Targeted on Council/Parliament Convergence to Push High-Risk Compliance to December 2027
"Political agreement on the AI Omnibus could be reached as early as 28 April 2026, with formal adoption expected by July — just ahead of the 2 August 2026 deadline for the implementation of the EU AI Act's obligations for high-risk AI systems." [paraphrased — institutional source] — Ropes & Gray legal alert, AI Omnibus trilogue tracker, April 2026

Today's second trilogue meeting on the EU's AI Act Digital Omnibus is the inflection point. Both the Council (mandate adopted 13 March 2026) and the European Parliament (full position adopted 26 March 2026 by 569 votes) have converged on fixed postponement dates: 2 December 2027 for stand-alone high-risk AI systems under Annex III, and 2 August 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products under Annex I. If the Belgian-led negotiating team locks the convergence today, the original 2 August 2026 deadline for high-risk obligations is dead.

Beyond the timeline, Parliament has added a new prohibition under Article 5: AI systems that generate, alter, or manipulate sexually explicit images of identifiable persons without consent — the so-called "nudifier" ban. This was absent from the Commission's original proposal and represents the only material expansion of prohibited practices in the Omnibus package.

If agreement is reached today, formal adoption follows: Parliament endorsement in May, Council endorsement in June, publication in the Official Journal in July — all just ahead of the 2 August 2026 statutory deadline. If talks fail today, the August deadline stands and the entire high-risk regime activates on schedule, triggering a cliff-edge for Annex III deployers (HR/recruitment AI, biometric systems, critical infrastructure AI) that have publicly stated they are not compliance-ready.

EDPB, EDPS, and civil-society groups (Article 19, EDRi, Algorithm Watch) have warned the postponement amounts to "regulatory backsliding" on rules negotiated less than three years ago. Industry — particularly the German auto sector and French strategic-AI champions (Mistral, Hugging Face) — has lobbied hard for the delay.

📎 Historical precedent: The GDPR's two-year implementation runway (2016 adoption → 2018 application) was never extended; the AI Act's 18-month phased schedule is now being reopened mid-implementation — first instance for an EU horizontal regulation.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

This is the most consequential AI policy outcome of 2026 — bigger than any single Member State law. Winners if agreement reached: every multinational deploying high-risk AI gets a 16-month breathing room; Mistral, Aleph Alpha, and EU GPAI providers get to scale before harmonised standards lock in; UK, Singapore, and US get to "wait and see" what the EU's GPAI Code of Practice rev 2 looks like. Losers: SMEs that already invested in 2026 readiness; supervisory authorities (the UK ICO, the French CNIL) who built capacity for the 2026 deadline. Critical pre-emption hook: under the Council text, the postponement is conditional on the Commission publishing harmonised standards before December 2027 — if that fails, the original 2026 deadline snaps back. Compliance teams should treat the August 2026 deadline as still legally binding until the Official Journal publishes the amending regulation.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The convergence is not as solid as it looks. Parliament's nudifier ban is a sticking point — Council representatives from Germany and Hungary have raised proportionality concerns that could push trilogue into a third meeting in May. Postponement is the easy win; expanding prohibited practices is the harder one. Today may produce a partial deal on timeline only, with Article 5 deferred to subsequent meetings. The August 2026 cliff-edge risk does not disappear today.

Trump's State-AI Preemption Executive Order Has Already Missed Three March Deadlines — FTC Consumer-Protection Guidance and Commerce Department State Law Audit Both AWOL
"Three provisions due on March 11 have not been completed or publicly announced. The FTC was supposed to issue guidance on AI consumer protection law, and the Commerce Department was due to review and publish an evaluation of state AI laws and flag 'onerous' ones." [paraphrased — Axios reporting] — Axios, "Trump's missed AI deadlines," 24 April 2026

The 11 December 2025 Trump executive order titled Eliminating State Law Obstruction of National Artificial Intelligence Policy set out a federal preemption agenda with hard March 2026 deadlines for the FTC, the Commerce Department, and DOJ. As Axios reported on 24 April, three of the most consequential of those deadlines have lapsed without action:

(1) FTC AI consumer-protection guidance — meant to set a federal floor that would preempt the Colorado AI Act, New York's RAISE Act, and California's generative-AI procurement rules. Without it, the federal preemption argument is rhetorical, not legal.

(2) Commerce Department state-AI-law evaluation — meant to publicly flag "onerous" state laws as candidates for federal-funding withholding. Without the published list, agencies have no operational basis to condition grants.

(3) DOJ guidance on funding conditionality — the legal mechanism for actually withholding federal funds from states with disfavoured AI laws.

The combined effect: Colorado's AI Act takes effect 30 June 2026, New York's RAISE Act has been live since 19 March, and California's generative-AI procurement order is operative — and the federal counter-preemption infrastructure that was supposed to displace them is missing. State AGs are reading the silence as a green light.

OSTP marked the one-year anniversary of Trump's AI Education for America's Youth EO on 24 April — the political optics suggest the administration is pivoting to softer, less litigable AI initiatives while the harder preemption agenda quietly stalls inside agency general-counsel offices grappling with the constitutional commandeering doctrine (Murphy v. NCAA) and Spending Clause limits (NFIB v. Sebelius).

📎 Historical precedent: The Biden 2023 AI EO had its own missed-deadline pattern (NIST RMF, OSTP Bill of Rights). Federal AI EO timelines have a roughly 60% slip rate across two administrations.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Winners: state AGs (Phil Weiser in Colorado, Letitia James in NY, Rob Bonta in CA) — they now have an unobstructed runway to enforce state AI laws through summer 2026; plaintiffs' bar bringing private right of action under Colorado AI Act. Losers: AI deployers that had baked federal preemption into their compliance posture and skipped state-law readiness; large tech trade associations (CCIA, Chamber of Progress) that lobbied for the EO. Critical legal trajectory: any DOJ funding-conditionality move now faces both a procedural infirmity (no underlying agency record) and a substantive infirmity (commandeering). Plan for state AI laws to be the operative law of the land through at least Q4 2026.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The "missed deadline" frame is a Beltway preoccupation. The actual binding constraint on state AI law enforcement is private litigation appetite, not federal preemption — and very few plaintiffs have brought Colorado AI Act cases in the eight weeks since enactment. Even with federal preemption inert, the on-the-ground enforcement environment for AI deployers may be softer than headlines suggest until the first big damages case lands. "South Korea has postponed its Digital Asset Basic Law until later in 2026 as regulators remain divided over stablecoin oversight authority. The Financial Services Commission and the Bank of Korea continue to clash over control of stablecoin reserves and enforcement responsibilities." [paraphrased — crypto.news reporting] — crypto.news, "South Korea postpones digital asset law to 2026," April 2026

South Korea's ruling Democratic Party's Digital Asset Basic Act (DABA) — the comprehensive successor to the Virtual Asset User Protection Act (VAUPA, in force since July 2024) — has been formally delayed beyond its original early-2026 target. The sticking point is the same one that has bedevilled global stablecoin frameworks: which regulator owns reserve composition, audit, and revocation authority. The Financial Services Commission (FSC) wants the file under its securities-regulator remit; the Bank of Korea (BOK) argues monetary-policy implications make it a central-bank competence.

The delay matters because Korea is one of the world's top three retail crypto markets by volume, with KRW-stablecoin pilot proposals from Kakao Pay, Naver Financial, and Shinhan Bank waiting on the legal framework. Global stablecoin issuers — including Circle (KRW-pegged USDC variants under discussion) and Tether (KRWT speculation) — also stand idle. Meanwhile, the existing VAUPA continues in force, regulating exchanges (Upbit, Bithumb, Coinone, Korbit) but not native stablecoin issuance.

In February 2026, the ruling party also proposed mandatory asset-disclosure rules for crypto influencers — amending the Capital Markets Act and VAUPA. That measure faces a similar slow-walk in committee.

⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Korea's delay creates regional regulatory arbitrage that Hong Kong (Story #2) and Singapore (MAS sandbox, Apr 24) are actively exploiting. Winners: Korean exchanges that benefit from VAUPA continuity without DABA's stricter conduct rules; HK VATPs that absorb Korean institutional tokenisation flows. Losers: Korean fintechs ready to issue KRW stablecoins; Korean retail investors continuing to use offshore platforms that VAUPA's enforcement gaps tolerate. The FSC vs. BOK dispute is structurally similar to the US Treasury-vs.-FRB dispute that delayed dollar stablecoin frameworks for two years pre-GENIUS — expect similar duration.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Korean exchanges (Upbit, Bithumb, Coinone, Korbit): status quo extended. Korean fintechs (Kakao Pay, Toss, Naver Financial): KRW-stablecoin product launches blocked. Global stablecoin issuers: Korean market access deferred. HK VATPs and Singapore PSA-licensed firms: capture Korean institutional flows in the interim.

India's Crypto Discussion Paper Shelved Again as RBI Blocks Publication — Fifth Public Delay in Two Years; Implementation Falls to Tax and FATCA/CRS Levers
"There is a genuine impasse within the Indian government, with the Finance Ministry and SEBI more open to exploring regulation and the RBI firmly resisting any move that could legitimise the sector." [paraphrased — Reuters/Crypto Times reporting, April 2026] — Crypto Times, "India's Crypto Policy Delayed Again as RBI Blocks Discussion Paper," 1 April 2026

The Indian government's long-promised crypto-policy discussion paper has been shelved for at least the fifth time in under two years. The RBI's stance — that regulating crypto would grant it "legitimacy" and risk systemic exposure — continues to override the Finance Ministry's and SEBI's interest in a comprehensive framework. India remains the world's largest unregulated crypto retail market and the third-largest by user count.

In the absence of comprehensive legislation, India is implementing crypto policy through tax and information-reporting levers: the Central Board of Direct Taxes' 5 March 2026 notification reclassified crypto-assets as financial assets under India's FATCA/CRS reporting framework, retroactive to 1 January 2026. Indian crypto exchanges and wallet providers must now share detailed transaction data with Indian tax authorities and, via CRS, with foreign tax authorities.

India has separately committed to implementing the OECD's Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF) by April 2027, requiring exchanges and wallet providers to collect and annually report transaction data, wallet addresses, user identities, and fair market valuations across borders.

⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

India is taxing what it refuses to regulate. The CBDT/CARF strategy creates the worst of both worlds for users: heavy reporting burden, no legal protection, no clear commercial framework. Winners: Indian VC and tech founders setting up Cayman/Singapore/Dubai parents to operate Indian distribution from offshore; the RBI in entrenching its position. Losers: Indian retail investors with no legal recourse; Indian crypto entrepreneurs unable to raise institutional capital onshore. Watch for a 30% TDS-rate cut in Budget 2027 as the first directional softening signal — without it, the policy posture holds.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Indian exchanges (CoinDCX, WazirX, Mudrex): tax/reporting compliance burden rises; market-share pressure from offshore platforms. Offshore platforms (Binance, Bybit, OKX): Indian volume continues to migrate. Indian fintechs (Razorpay, Cred, Phonepe): stablecoin and crypto-payment integration deferred indefinitely. OECD/G20 partners: India's CARF commitment locks in cross-border data flows by 2027.
