The Man Who Fed Half the World

Fritz Haber — chemist, weapons director, Nobel laureate, exile — 1868–1934

Fritz Haber invented the process that feeds half the world's population — then personally directed the first large-scale chemical weapons attack in history. He died a broken exile in 1934, and the pesticide chemistry he pioneered was later used to murder members of his own family in Nazi gas chambers.

TL;DR

Fritz Haber synthesized ammonia from atmospheric nitrogen in 1909, unlocking a process that now enables food production for roughly half of humanity. The same man directed Germany's chemical weapons program in World War I, watched his wife shoot herself in the garden in protest, and died in exile eight months after Hitler took power — a few months before the Nazi regime repurposed his pesticide research into Zyklon B.

Section 01

The Story

By 1900, the world was quietly running out of food. Not immediately — the famines hadn't started yet — but the arithmetic was inescapable. The human population was growing faster than the land could be coaxed into feeding it, and the reason came down to a single element: nitrogen. Plants need it for proteins, for DNA, for growth itself. And the world's supply of usable nitrogen was finite.

Fixed nitrogen — the kind plants can absorb — came primarily from guano deposits on South American islands and from Chilean saltpeter mines. Both were depleting. Demographers of the era were warning that a population crash was a matter of decades, not centuries. The Nobel laureate William Crookes gave a famous 1898 address warning that wheat production would fail to meet demand within a generation. He called on chemists to "fix" atmospheric nitrogen or face civilizational catastrophe. Nitrogen, after all, makes up 78 percent of the air. It was everywhere. And almost none of it was usable by anything alive.

Fritz Haber was born in 1868 in Breslau, in what is now Wroclaw, Poland, to a prosperous Jewish merchant family. He converted to Christianity as a young man, as many German Jews did in the era of emancipation, seeking full access to academic and professional life. By 1909, working at the University of Karlsruhe, he had found the answer to Crookes's challenge. Under extreme pressure and high temperature, using osmium as a catalyst, he combined atmospheric nitrogen with hydrogen to produce ammonia — a molecule plants could use. For the first time in history, humans could manufacture soil fertility from thin air.

The Industrial Scale-Up

Carl Bosch at the chemical firm BASF spent four years translating Haber's bench chemistry into an industrial process, replacing the scarce osmium catalyst with iron, and building reactors that could withstand pressures of 150–300 atmospheres. The first full-scale plant opened at Oppau, Germany, in September 1913. Within a year, the same technology was producing explosives for a world war.

When World War I broke out in 1914, Haber volunteered his chemistry to the German war effort with complete conviction. He believed deeply in Germany — it was his country, the country that had received him, that had made him a professor and eventually a laureate. He argued that shortening the war through decisive chemical weapons would, on balance, save more lives than it cost. On April 22, 1915, under his personal direction, 160 tonnes of chlorine gas were released from cylinders dug into the German trenches near Ypres. A yellow-green cloud drifted southwest across four miles of Allied line, held by French and Algerian troops who had never seen anything like it. The defenses collapsed. A six-and-a-half-kilometre hole opened in the line.

Clara Haber called her husband's weapons work "a perversion of the ideals of science" and "a sign of barbarity, corrupting the very discipline which ought to bring new insights into life."

Clara Immerwahr — the first woman to earn a doctorate in chemistry from Breslau University, and Haber's wife — had pleaded with him not to deploy the gas. The night of May 1–2, 1915, after a celebratory dinner at their home marking his promotion to captain, she walked into the garden with his Army pistol and shot herself in the heart. Their twelve-year-old son Hermann ran to her and held her as she died. Haber left for the Eastern Front the next morning.

He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for 1918. The announcement provoked outrage across the Allied world. Some scientists called him a war criminal. He appeared on an early Allied list of individuals to be prosecuted for war crimes, though his name was later removed. He accepted the prize, and continued his work. He never fully understood why the world could not share his utilitarian calculus.

Section 02

The Mechanism

To understand why Haber's discovery was genuinely civilizational, you have to understand why nitrogen was the bottleneck. Atmospheric nitrogen — N2 — is held together by a triple bond, one of the strongest in chemistry. It is chemically inert under almost all natural conditions. The only biological systems that can break it are specialized bacteria in soil and root nodules, working slowly. Lightning does it too, in tiny amounts. Everything else in the nitrogen cycle flows downstream from these two slow trickles.

The Haber-Bosch process forces the reaction directly: N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3. To break that triple bond without bacteria, you need brute force — temperatures of 400–500 degrees Celsius, pressures 150 to 300 times atmospheric, and a catalyst (iron with chemical promoters, in the industrial version) to lower the energy barrier enough to make the reaction viable at scale. The resulting ammonia is the feedstock for virtually all synthetic nitrogen fertilizer. Spread on fields, it breaks down into ammonium and nitrate ions that plant roots absorb directly.

The Population Arithmetic

Without Haber-Bosch, the maximum sustainable global food supply supports approximately 3.5–4 billion people. The world currently holds more than 8 billion. The gap — roughly 4 billion lives — exists because of a reaction discovered in a German university laboratory in 1909.

The dual-use nature of the chemistry was not incidental — it was structural. The same nitrogen-fixing process that makes ammonia for fertilizer also makes ammonia for nitric acid, which makes nitrate explosives. When the British naval blockade cut off Germany's supply of Chilean saltpeter in 1914, the Haber-Bosch plants pivoted to ammunition production almost seamlessly. Without that capacity, Germany would likely have run out of explosives by 1916. The war, historians have argued, was extended by years partly because Haber had already solved the nitrogen problem.

The chlorine gas weapon Haber developed operated on a different chemistry but the same moral logic: scale, concentration, and control over the delivery mechanism. Haber understood that gas dispersed by wind was less precise than artillery, but he believed precision was overrated as a measure of humaneness. He was wrong, in ways that became visible to almost everyone except him.

Roughly half the nitrogen atoms in a living human body were produced by the Haber-Bosch process. We are, in a direct chemical sense, partly made from his discovery.
Section 03

The Connections

The loyalty paradox. Haber spent his adult life performing Germanness with unusual intensity — converting, serving, inventing weapons, declining opportunities abroad. After WWI he attempted to extract gold from seawater to help Germany pay its ruinous war reparations, failing only because the ocean contained far less gold than early estimates suggested. When Hitler came to power in 1933 and the Nazis dismissed Jewish staff from the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute, Haber resigned his directorship in protest. He was, notably, protesting the dismissal of others — his own service record had temporarily exempted him. Germany did not protect him. He left for Cambridge, then Switzerland, and died of heart failure in a Basel hotel on January 29, 1934.

The Zyklon B shadow. Haber's institute had developed Zyklon A, a cyanide-based pest-control agent, during the 1920s. After his death, a reformulated version, Zyklon B — with the warning odorant removed — became the primary killing agent used in the gas chambers at Auschwitz and other extermination camps. The precise line of descent from Haber's research to the Holocaust's mechanism is contested by historians, but members of his extended family died in those camps. The irony is absolute: the man whose chemistry enabled food for billions, whose wife died opposing weapons, whose nationality was weaponized against him, had his own research form part of the industrial infrastructure of the genocide targeting his people.

The planetary boundary. Haber-Bosch now produces more than 150 million tonnes of ammonia per year. The synthetic nitrogen that runs off agricultural land has created more than 400 ocean dead zones globally, disrupted freshwater systems, and is one of the nine planetary boundaries that Earth scientists say humanity has already crossed. The discovery that rescued humanity from one existential constraint has generated a new one on a different timescale.

The Green Revolution debt. When Norman Borlaug's high-yield wheat and rice varieties saved hundreds of millions from famine across Asia and South America in the 1960s, they worked because of synthetic nitrogen. The new cultivars were bred to be fertilizer-responsive — without Haber-Bosch ammonia they would have produced no more grain than their predecessors. Borlaug won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970. Haber won the Nobel Chemistry Prize in 1918. They are two chapters of the same story.

The Energy Cost

The Haber-Bosch process currently consumes approximately 1–2% of the world's total energy supply and generates roughly 1.4% of global CO2 emissions. It is one of the single largest industrial energy uses on Earth. Finding a low-energy biological equivalent — a room-temperature nitrogen fixation catalyst — is considered one of the most valuable unsolved problems in chemistry.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

⚗️
Clara Immerwahr

The first woman to earn a chemistry doctorate from Breslau, she published on thermodynamics and translated Haber's work into English. For decades she was a historical footnote. Feminist historians recovered her story in the 1990s; she is now memorialized with an award for scientists who refuse to participate in weapons work.

🎉
The Poisoned Nobel

Haber remains the most morally contested Nobel laureate in the prize's history. The 1918 award provoked formal protests from Allied scientists. He accepted it in 1919 at a ceremony in Stockholm. The committee's position was that the fertilizer achievement stood independently of his wartime conduct — a distinction that has never stopped feeling insufficient.

🌎
The Nitrogen Boundary

The Stockholm Resilience Centre's nine planetary boundaries framework identifies biogeochemical flows — including synthetic nitrogen — as one of the boundaries already in the danger zone. The agricultural runoff from Haber-Bosch fertilizer has created dead zones at the mouths of most of the world's major rivers.

🧪
The Osmium Problem

Haber's original catalyst was osmium — one of the rarest elements on Earth, denser than lead, costing thousands per ounce. Carl Bosch spent years finding that cheap iron, with small amounts of alumina and potassium oxide as promoters, worked nearly as well. The search for better catalysts continues; a room-temperature biological mimic would transform global agriculture and energy use.

📒
The Gold in the Sea

After WWI, Haber secretly attempted to extract gold from seawater to fund Germany's 132-billion-mark war reparations. Early analyses had suggested the ocean held vast amounts. Years of secret sampling showed they were off by a factor of 1,000. He abandoned the project in 1928. The episode is a rare case of Haber's chemistry failing him at precisely the moment he needed it most.
