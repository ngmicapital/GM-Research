The Secret Cartel That Made Your Lightbulbs Die on Schedule

In 1925, seven of the world's largest lightbulb companies met in Geneva and agreed to deliberately engineer their products to burn out faster — then fined any member whose bulbs lasted too long

In 1925, seven of the world's largest lightbulb manufacturers formed a secret cartel in Geneva and deliberately re-engineered their products to fail faster — setting a hard ceiling of 1,000 hours and imposing financial penalties on members whose bulbs lasted longer. The Phoebus Cartel was the first documented case of industrial planned obsolescence, and it invented a business strategy that still governs the products you carry in your pocket today.

TL;DR

The Phoebus Cartel was the first documented case of industrial planned obsolescence, and it invented a business strategy that still governs the products you carry in your pocket today.

Section 01

The Story

On the evening of 23 December 1924, representatives of the world's most powerful electrical companies gathered in Geneva. Sitting around the table were executives from Osram of Germany, Philips of the Netherlands, International General Electric from the United States, Associated Electrical Industries from Britain, Tungsram of Hungary, Compagnie des Lampes from France, ELIN of Austria, and Tokyo Electric from Japan. Together, these companies controlled the majority of the world's lightbulb market. What they agreed to that evening would become the most documented case of deliberate industrial sabotage in commercial history.

They incorporated a Swiss holding company — Phœbus S.A. Compagnie Industrielle pour le Développement de l'Éclairage — on 15 January 1925, headquartered in Geneva. Phoebus, named for the Greek god of light, would serve as the cartel's administrative centre, co-ordinating market quotas, setting technical standards, and operating a central testing laboratory in Switzerland. To join, every member surrendered a portion of its autonomy: pricing, production volumes, territorial markets, and above all the performance specifications of its bulbs. On that last point, the cartel was explicit and ruthless. The standard lifespan of a household incandescent bulb was to be set at one thousand hours. No more.

This was not a natural engineering limit. At the time the cartel was formed, leading manufacturers were already producing bulbs that lasted considerably longer — premium models could achieve 2,500 hours, and some engineering teams believed 3,500 or even 5,000 hours was technically feasible with the tungsten filament technology then available. The cartel did not discover that 1,000 hours was the natural maximum. It chose 1,000 hours because shorter-lived bulbs meant more replacement purchases, more revenue, and a permanently captive market. Durability was, in the language of the cartel's internal documents, a problem to be engineered away.

The cartel's testing laboratory did not check whether bulbs worked. It checked whether they lasted too long — and fined members accordingly.

The enforcement mechanism was detailed to the point of being almost comical. Member companies were required to submit bulb samples to the Swiss central testing laboratory. If a company's bulbs averaged more than 1,005 hours, penalties were assessed on a graduated scale. Bulbs averaging 1,000 to 1,500 hours triggered fines of 5 to 20 Swiss francs per 1,000 units sold. For bulbs averaging over 1,750 hours, the fine rose to 20 Swiss francs. For bulbs that lasted over 3,000 hours — three times the permitted maximum — the penalty was 200 Swiss francs per 1,000 units. In fiscal year 1929 alone, total penalties collected across all cartel members reached 304,000 Swiss francs. The cartel was fining its own members for making products that worked too well.

These internal documents remained largely unknown to the outside world until 2014, when German media historian Markus Krajewski published an investigation in IEEE Spectrum titled "The Great Lightbulb Conspiracy." Krajewski had obtained access to cartel records and subjected them to systematic analysis — for the first time, the mechanics of Phoebus could be traced not through speculation but through the cartel's own paperwork. The picture that emerged was precisely as cynical as the worst suspicions had suggested. The cartel tracked average bulb lifespan across member factories with actuarial precision. In 1926, the average was already down to around 1,800 hours. By 1933–34, it had been engineered to 1,205 hours. The target of 1,000 was within reach.

The cartel was originally structured to run for thirty years, from 1925 to 1955. It did not survive that long. When the Second World War broke out in 1939, co-ordination between companies in countries now at war with each other became impossible. Phoebus effectively ceased operations in 1939 and was formally nullified in 1940. The thirty-year plan had lasted fifteen years. It had nonetheless done its work.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The technical question at the heart of the Phoebus story is: how do you deliberately make a lightbulb burn out faster without it being obvious that you're doing so? The answer is not as crude as simply using thinner wire. Tungsten filaments fail through a process called thermal creep — at high operating temperatures, the crystalline structure of the metal slowly deforms and eventually fractures. The temperature at which a filament operates determines both its light output (hotter filaments produce whiter, brighter light) and its lifespan (hotter filaments fail faster). The relationship is steep and non-linear: running a tungsten filament just 5 to 10 percent hotter than its rated thermal tolerance can cut its lifespan in half.

The Phoebus technical committee — staffed by engineers from member companies who shared research under cartel agreements — spent years systematically adjusting the thermal operating regime of standard household bulbs upward. They modified filament composition, gas mixtures inside the bulb, and the calibration of operating voltage, converging through iterative testing on specifications that reliably produced the 1,000-hour outcome. The process was not one dramatic engineering decision but a slow, methodical squeeze, spread across years, documented in meeting minutes and testing reports that Krajewski found preserved in archive boxes.

The Lifespan–Efficiency Trade-off Was Real — But the Cartel Exploited It

A genuinely important nuance: brighter, more efficient bulbs do burn out faster — the physics is real. The cartel used this truth as cover. A filament optimised for maximum lumen output at minimum energy consumption will run hotter and fail faster than one optimised for longevity. What Phoebus did was set the trade-off point by commercial decree rather than consumer preference — choosing maximum brightness that happened to produce a thousand-hour lifespan, when the engineering could have delivered the same brightness at a point that lasted far longer.

The most striking rebuttal to the cartel's implicit claim that 1,000 hours was technically inevitable sits in a fire station in Livermore, California. The Centennial Light — a carbon-filament bulb manufactured by the Shelby Electric Company of Shelby, Ohio, in the late 1890s — has been burning continuously at the Livermore-Pleasanton Fire Department since 1901. As of 2024, it holds the Guinness World Record for the longest-burning lightbulb, with approximately 123 years of operation. It has been turned off only a handful of times, including once when the fire station moved in 1976 — the bulb survived the relocation.

The comparison between the Centennial Light and Phoebus-era tungsten bulbs is not strictly apples-to-apples: the Shelby bulb uses an earlier carbon filament technology, not tungsten, and runs at very low wattage — now equivalent to about four watts, significantly dimmed from its original output. But the Centennial Light proves the underlying principle: that with different engineering choices, a bulb can last indefinitely. The cartel chose not to make those choices, and then fined its members if they accidentally got too close to them.

By the late 1940s, US antitrust scrutiny had caught up with GE's domestic practices. In United States v. General Electric Co. (82 F. Supp. 753, D.N.J. 1949), a federal court found GE had violated the Sherman Antitrust Act through its patent-licensing arrangements that effectively fixed bulb prices across the industry. The civil antitrust decree issued in November 1949 targeted GE's domestic operations specifically; the international Phoebus structure had already dissolved in the war. No criminal conviction with fines in the modern sense resulted, but the case stands as the most direct legal consequence of the Phoebus era in the United States.

Section 03

The Connections

The invention of a business strategy. The Phoebus Cartel is the origin point of what would later be named "planned obsolescence" — the deliberate engineering of finite product lifespans to sustain replacement demand. The term itself was not coined until 1954, when American industrial designer Brooks Stevens used it at a design conference, arguing that it was not a scandal but a legitimate and valuable commercial strategy. Stevens defined it as "instilling in the buyer the desire to own something a little newer, a little better, a little sooner than is necessary." What Stevens described as desire, Phoebus had operationalised as engineering. The cartel pre-dated the concept's naming by thirty years and proved it could work.

Apple and the descent of the idea. In 2017, an iOS update from Apple was found to be deliberately slowing down older iPhones — a practice Apple later confirmed, framing it as battery health management to prevent unexpected shutdowns on aging devices. Critics called it what it looked like: an engineered incentive to upgrade. Apple settled a US class-action lawsuit for up to $500 million. In France, the consumer watchdog DGCCRF launched a criminal investigation; Apple paid approximately €25 million to resolve it. France had become, in 2015, the first country in the world to explicitly criminalise planned obsolescence — defining it as "the use of techniques by which the person responsible for placing a product on the market aims, by any means, to deliberately shorten the lifespan of a product in order to increase its replacement rate." Penalties include up to two years in prison and fines of up to 5 percent of annual revenues. The law is a direct descendant of the problem Phoebus institutionalised a century earlier.

Printers and the chip problem. The lightbulb cartel fined members for making bulbs that lasted too long. Printer manufacturers solved the same problem with firmware. HP, Epson, and Canon have each faced lawsuits alleging that smart chips embedded in ink cartridges disable them at arbitrary page counts — even when significant ink remains — using programmed expiry dates rather than actual ink depletion as the trigger. The mechanism is different from Phoebus in that it involves digital rather than physical engineering, but the incentive structure is identical: the product's functional life is set by commercial decision, not by the exhaustion of its actual utility. Class-action suits on this issue have been active in US federal courts.

The right-to-repair counterpressure. The policy response to modern planned obsolescence has coalesced around right-to-repair legislation. The European Union's Ecodesign Regulation, which began rolling out in 2021, now requires manufacturers of certain product categories to supply spare parts for a minimum of seven to ten years after the product's last sale and to make those parts accessible to independent repairers. The UK passed its own right-to-repair rules in 2021 covering washing machines, dishwashers, and televisions. US federal legislation remains stalled, though the FTC issued a policy statement in 2021 supporting right-to-repair and several states have passed their own laws. None of these measures directly targets the Phoebus strategy at the design stage; they address repairability rather than durability. But they represent the first significant regulatory pushback against the business model the cartel invented.

The bulb that escaped the system. There is a coda to the Phoebus story that its founders could not have anticipated. The incandescent tungsten bulb — the product Phoebus spent fifteen years tuning for planned failure — is now largely obsolete, replaced by LED technology. A standard LED bulb today carries a rated lifespan of 15,000 to 25,000 hours, fifteen to twenty-five times the Phoebus limit. The economics of LEDs make the old cartel strategy self-defeating: the margins are in the bulb itself, not the replacement rate. Whether the same logic will apply to the next generation of consumer electronics — or whether new forms of the Phoebus strategy will emerge — is the live question.

The Great Lightbulb Conspiracy (Krajewski, 2014)
Markus Krajewski's IEEE Spectrum investigation is the definitive account of Phoebus — the only work based directly on cartel documents. Everything else, including Wikipedia's article, traces back to it. The original PDF is freely accessible and runs to about 3,000 words.

The Centennial Light, Livermore CA
The world's longest-burning lightbulb has been on since 1901 at the Livermore-Pleasanton Fire Department and has its own website (centennialbulb.org) and a 24-hour webcam. Carbon-filament, made by Shelby Electric, now burning at roughly 4-watt equivalent. It has outlasted everyone who installed it by about a century.
🏭
Alfred Sloan and Annual Model Changes
GM chief Alfred Sloan independently developed the consumer version of planned obsolescence in the 1920s–30s: annual styling changes to cars that made perfectly functional vehicles feel outdated. Sloan framed it as "the constant upgrading of products" — Brooks Stevens gave the practice its name in 1954, largely in Sloan's tradition.
📱
Apple Batterygate (2017)
The most documented modern case: Apple's iOS 10.2.1 update throttled CPU performance on iPhones with older batteries. Apple called it a stability feature; regulators in France called it planned obsolescence and opened a criminal probe. The $500M US settlement and €25M French fine are the largest penalties the strategy has ever generated.
🔧
EU Ecodesign Right-to-Repair
The EU's 2021 Ecodesign Regulation requires manufacturers of major appliances to supply spare parts for 7–10 years post-sale, accessible to independent repairers. It's the most substantive regulatory counter to planned obsolescence yet enacted — though it targets repairability, not designed-in failure at the outset.
