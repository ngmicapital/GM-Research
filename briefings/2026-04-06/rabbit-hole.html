Rabbit Hole

The Fish That
Forgot How to Bleed

Nature 6 April 2026 · Sydney, Australia
TL;DR

Every vertebrate on Earth uses hemoglobin — the iron-rich protein that makes blood red and carries oxygen to tissues. Without it, a vertebrate asphyxiates within minutes of any real exertion. Except one family: the Antarctic icefish. All 16 species of Channichthyidae have no hemoglobin and no red blood cells. Their blood is clear. Their hearts are white. They carry roughly 10% of the oxygen capacity of their nearest relatives and survive on plasma alone — dissolved gas in watery fluid, the way a pond holds air on a cold morning. The loss happened once, roughly 8–9 million years ago, triggered by mobile "jumping genes" that deleted the hemoglobin clusters from the genome. The fish compensated with hearts four to five times larger relative to body size, blood volume four times greater, capillaries three times wider, and skin they breathe through. They are the living proof that one of vertebrate biology's most fundamental rules can be broken — under the right conditions, and with enough evolutionary time to rebuild around the absence.

Section 01

The Story

In 1927, Norwegian biologist Ditlef Rustad hauled a strange fish onto the deck of a research vessel near Bouvet Island in the South Atlantic. When it bled, the blood that ran was not red. It was milky, thin, almost translucent — the colour of clouded water. Rustad filed his report, called the creature the "white crocodile fish" for its elongated snout and pallid appearance, and moved on. The scientific community had no framework for what he had seen. It did not yet compute.

The moment that shook vertebrate biology came in 1954, when Norwegian biochemist Johan Ruud published a formal analysis in Nature. The fish — Chaenocephalus aceratus, the blackfin icefish — had blood that was "nearly transparent, lacking erythrocytes and hemoglobin." No red blood cells. No hemoglobin. Iron levels close to zero. Ruud had found a vertebrate that broke every rule in the textbook.

Hemoglobin is one of the most conserved molecules in vertebrate evolution — its core structure is virtually identical across humans, sharks, and zebrafish, unchanged for roughly 450 million years. The reason is brutal simplicity: dissolved oxygen in blood plasma can supply perhaps 1–3% of what an active vertebrate needs. Hemoglobin solves this by binding oxygen cooperatively and carrying 60 to 70 times more oxygen per unit volume than plasma alone. Remove it and the animal should, by every physiological principle, suffocate.

The icefish did not get the memo. There are 16 species in the family Channichthyidae, all confined to the Southern Ocean around Antarctica, and every one of them is hemoglobin-free. They are not a marginal population clinging to survival at the fringes. They and their close notothenioid relatives account for roughly 35% of Southern Ocean fish species and approximately 90% of the fish biomass in Antarctic waters. A group that by rights should not exist has come to dominate one of the harshest marine environments on the planet.

The Counterintuitive Core

The icefish do not merely survive without hemoglobin. They thrive. They have turned the loss of the molecule most fundamental to vertebrate oxygen transport into a working body plan — and in doing so have become the dominant fish lineage of an entire ocean.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The Southern Ocean is extraordinarily cold — averaging around -2°C year-round, with ice-laden water barely above the freezing point of seawater. Cold water holds significantly more dissolved oxygen than warm water. The icefish exploit this. Their oxygen transport relies entirely on gas dissolved in plasma, not bound to a carrier molecule. It is an inherently low-capacity system, but the environment supplies just enough ambient oxygen to make it viable — barely.

To compensate for blood that delivers only a fraction of normal oxygen, the icefish body was rebuilt from the circulatory system outward. The heart is four to five times larger relative to body size than in comparable red-blooded fish, and pumps roughly five times the cardiac output by weight. At rest, approximately 22% of an icefish's total metabolic energy goes purely to moving blood — compared to around 5% in temperate fish. Nearly a quarter of all the energy the animal produces, just to keep circulation running.

The blood itself was redesigned for flow. Because plasma carries no cells, it is far less viscous than normal vertebrate blood. Icefish carry roughly four times more blood by body weight than their red-blooded relatives — but because it flows so easily, the energy cost per litre pumped remains manageable. Their blood vessels are also wider: capillaries run at two to three times the diameter of typical vertebrate capillaries, maximising surface area for oxygen diffusion into tissues. Researchers examining icefish eye capillaries described them as a "dense, tangled mess like a plate of spaghetti" — structure sacrificed for throughput.

There is a final, stranger adaptation: icefish are scaleless, and they breathe through their skin. Cutaneous respiration — oxygen diffusing directly through the body surface into the bloodstream — provides a measurable supplement to whatever their gills and plasma can manage. The animal has become, in part, a living membrane.

In Plain English

Imagine losing a car's engine and compensating by fitting an enormous battery, removing all non-essential weight, widening every road in the city, and then adding solar panels to the roof. The car still moves — just differently, at a cost, optimised for a world in which engines are no longer available. The icefish is that car.

Six of the 16 icefish species have gone further: they have also lost myoglobin, the oxygen-storing protein that gives heart and muscle tissue their red-brown colour in most vertebrates. Their hearts are literally white. This myoglobin loss happened through at least four separate mutational events across the family — meaning natural selection found the same solution independently, multiple times. The loss apparently reduces oxygen demand in resting cardiac muscle enough that the tradeoff is worth it, given how efficiently icefish heart cells compensate with increased mitochondrial density.

The genetics of how the hemoglobin loss occurred were pieced together over decades. A landmark 2006 paper in Molecular Biology and Evolution used one exceptional species — Neopagetopsis ionah, which retained a nonfunctional "genomic fossil" of the original hemoglobin gene cluster — to reconstruct the order of deletions. A 2025 paper in Genome Biology and Evolution identified the likely trigger: transposable elements — mobile DNA sequences, sometimes called jumping genes, that can cut and paste themselves into new positions in the genome. At both hemoglobin gene clusters, transposable element sequences are found precisely at the deletion boundaries. The researchers also found evidence of a "recent burst" of transposon activity in the icefish genome, consistent with environmental stress — extreme cold and glaciation cycles — having mobilised these genetic elements en masse roughly 8 to 9 million years ago, deleting hemoglobin in the process.

The loss, once it happened, was permanent. And because the icefish subsequently radiated into 16 species, every one of them inherited the deletion. A single genomic accident, propagated across an entire family — and the family survived anyway.

Section 03

The Connections

The antifreeze that keeps them half-frozen. The icefish story is inseparable from the broader notothenioid story, and that story begins with antifreeze. As the Southern Ocean cooled over the past 34 million years, most fish went extinct. The notothenioids survived because their common ancestor evolved antifreeze glycoproteins (AFGPs) — proteins that bind to incoming ice crystals and physically block their growth. The proteins evolved from a pancreatic digestive enzyme, repurposed through gene duplication and amplification into something structurally entirely new. They circulate at concentrations of roughly 30–35 mg/mL and depress the fish's actual freezing point from about -1°C to around -2.5°C to -3.5°C — a critical survival buffer in water that sits at -1.9°C. Without the AFGPs, there would have been no icefish lineage to lose hemoglobin. One improbable adaptation cleared the runway for the other.

They carry ice inside them. The antifreeze proteins are almost too effective. Because they block ice crystal growth irreversibly, they also inhibit melting. Internal ice crystals that form in icefish bodies can persist at temperatures up to 1°C above the equilibrium melting point — meaning the fish carry microscopic internal ice year-round. They cannot fully thaw during the brief Antarctic summer. They are partially frozen animals, surviving because the ice crystals are too small to pierce cell membranes, and because the antifreeze proteins keep those crystals from growing into something lethal.

A model for human disease. H. William Detrich III at Northeastern University sequenced the full blackfin icefish genome in 2019 and found something medically interesting: several genes associated with human blood disorders had been lost or altered. A 2024 paper identified the loss of FAAP20, a gene associated with Fanconi anemia — a rare human syndrome causing progressive bone marrow failure and severe anemia. Icefish are, in a sense, living animals that have "solved" a version of this condition through evolution. They also reduced their bone density over approximately 4 million years, accumulating lipids instead of minerals to achieve neutral buoyancy — a process structurally analogous to human osteoporosis. The NIH funded a $2.48 million grant to study icefish bone genetics as a model for understanding osteopenia in humans.

Evolution found a second path. In January 2026, Detrich's lab published a study in Current Biology on the Asian noodlefish (family Salangidae) — small, translucent, warm-water fish from East Asia that have independently evolved white blood through entirely different genetic mechanisms. They lost the myoglobin gene in a single ancestral event, and their hemoglobin genes were inactivated by accumulated smaller mutations rather than outright deletion. They survive because they are neotenic (permanently juvenile in form), thin, scaleless, and short-lived — enabling skin-based respiration without red blood cells. Detrich's summary: "There's more than one way for fish to lose the production of myoglobin, hemoglobin and red blood cells." The implication is that hemoglobin loss may be more evolutionarily accessible than anyone assumed. The "impossible" happened twice.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

Genetics Jumping Genes and the Hemoglobin Deletion

Transposable elements — mobile DNA sequences that can copy or excise themselves — appear to have triggered both hemoglobin cluster deletions in the icefish lineage. The 2025 Genome Biology and Evolution paper suggests extreme environmental stress may mobilise transposons and accelerate evolutionary change, a finding with implications for how genomes respond to climate shocks.

Biochemistry Antifreeze Proteins: A Digestive Enzyme Repurposed

The antifreeze glycoproteins that allow notothenioids to survive in -2°C seawater evolved from a pancreatic trypsinogen gene. A 9-nucleotide coding element was amplified into a repetitive protein structure with entirely new function. It is one of biology's most dramatic examples of a molecule being seized and repurposed wholesale for a new job.

Medicine Living With Catastrophic Anaemia

Icefish oxygen-carrying capacity is roughly 10% of their red-blooded relatives — a level that would be fatal in any mammal. NIH-funded research is mining the icefish genome for insights into human anaemia and osteoporosis: the fish evolved reduced bone density alongside white blood, accumulating fat lipids for buoyancy in place of mineral. The same genetic pathways appear to operate in human skeletal disease.

Climate The Most Vulnerable Fish in the Ocean

Icefish physiology is calibrated to within a few degrees. Because dissolved oxygen falls as water warms, even a modest rise in Southern Ocean temperatures reduces the ambient supply their plasma-only system depends on. Researchers at Northeastern University have identified icefish as significantly more thermally sensitive than red-blooded notothenioids — a canary in the Antarctic ecosystem.

Evolution Convergent Loss: The Asian Noodlefish

Published in Current Biology in January 2026, research on Asian noodlefish confirmed they independently evolved white blood — not through gene cluster deletions like icefish, but through accumulated smaller mutations. Two entirely separate fish lineages, on opposite sides of the planet, lost the same ancient molecule through different genetic routes. The "impossible" happened twice.
