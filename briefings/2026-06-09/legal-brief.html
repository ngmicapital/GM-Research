CLARITY Act Floor Crunch: FISA Delay Compresses the July 4 Window as Over 200 Firms Urge Immediate Senate Vote

"The CLARITY Act will establish a framework for crypto markets, clarify regulatory responsibilities, create workable registration pathways, and maintain protections for software developers — we urge Senate leadership to schedule a vote without further delay." Joint industry letter signed by Coinbase, Ripple, Circle, and 200+ organizations — June 2026 [paraphrased]

The Digital Asset Market CLARITY Act (S. Cal. No. 423) was formally placed on the Senate Legislative Calendar on June 1, 2026, following a 15–9 bipartisan Banking Committee vote on May 14. The bill — the most consequential US crypto market-structure legislation since the GENIUS Act — would give the CFTC exclusive spot-market jurisdiction over digital commodities, carve out a statutory safe harbor for non-custodial DeFi developers, and codify the SEC’s March 2026 token taxonomy into law.

Calendar placement does not guarantee floor time. Senate floor scheduling has been disrupted by the FISA Section 702 reauthorization debate, which consumed the week of June 3 after a 47–52 procedural failure. Galaxy Digital, which placed a $10 million prediction-market bet on 2026 passage, has trimmed its odds to approximately 60%; Polymarket sits at 59%. The narrow corridor before the August recess means leadership must act in the next two to three weeks or risk pushing the bill past the July 4 White House signing target that Chairman Scott has publicly endorsed.

Three comment windows relevant to the GENIUS Act stablecoin framework also close today (June 9): the FinCEN/OFAC AML rules, FDIC prudential standards, and OCC licensing standards. The confluence of a live comment deadline and the CLARITY Act scheduling fight makes this the single most active legislative and regulatory week for digital assets in 2026.

📎 Historical Precedent: The Dodd–Frank Act cleared the Senate 60–39 on July 15, 2010, after a similar summer-recess pressure campaign; leadership used a procedural pre-recess window identical to the one now available for CLARITY. The Dodd–Frank precedent cut both ways — speed produced a landmark, but also dozens of ambiguous provisions litigated for a decade.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The CLARITY Act’s DeFi safe harbor is the pivotal provision for practitioners. Non-custodial developers — those who write and publish smart contracts without holding user funds — would obtain an explicit statutory shield from SEC enforcement, ending the jurisdictional grey zone that has paralysed on-shore DeFi legal structuring since 2021. Counsel advising DeFi protocols should note that the safe harbor is activity-based, not entity-based: if a developer takes custody at any point (including for gas-fee pre-funding), the shield dissolves. The 60-vote cloture threshold remains the structural obstacle; the Democratic ethics clause targeting Trump family crypto profits remains the seven-vote gap. The Brief’s read: The floor window is real but narrow. A July 4 signing is possible if leadership cancels one recess week; a post-August-recess scenario is more likely unless the ethics clause is resolved by compromise language this week. Source: PYMNTS / CryptoTimes / Bloomberg Government.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Critics within the Democratic caucus argue that the CLARITY Act’s CFTC-first framework — endorsed by the crypto industry — is precisely the problem: it moves oversight to an agency historically less equipped for consumer-protection enforcement and hollows out the SEC’s retail-investor mandate. Senator Warren’s camp contends that “market-structure clarity” is a regulatory capture project dressed in bipartisan language, particularly given that the Trump administration holds the OCC, CFTC, and Fed simultaneously. The Brief’s rebuttal: The SEC’s own 2026–2030 Strategic Plan explicitly welcomes the CLARITY Act framework as complementary, undermining the regulatory-gap argument. The consumer-protection concern is real but can be addressed through amendment rather than delay. Source: The Defiant / Senator Warren press releases.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Coinbase National Trust Company (OCC charter: April 2, 2026), Ripple National Trust Bank (charter: December 12, 2025), and Circle’s First National Digital Currency Bank (charter: December 12, 2025) are the primary beneficiaries of CLARITY Act passage: their CFTC-commodity classification of XRP, BTC, and ETH would be locked in, ending litigation risk. CME Group, which launched 24/7 XRP futures with Ripple Prime as clearing partner, gains expanded product scope under a CFTC-centric regime. Offshore perpetual-futures venues (Deribit, Binance) face structural disadvantage as US-regulated perps become viable onshore. Source: CoinGape / The Block / CNBC.

Three GENIUS Act Rulemaking Comment Windows Close Today — FDIC Prudential, FinCEN AML, and OCC Licensing Deadlines All Land on June 9

"The FDIC is soliciting comment on a proposal that would implement certain requirements pursuant to the GENIUS Act applicable to FDIC-supervised permitted payment stablecoin issuers — comments must be received no later than June 9, 2026." Federal Register Vol. 91, Doc. 2026-06974 — FDIC Proposed Rule

Today marks the simultaneous close of three separate notice-and-comment windows that together constitute the core prudential infrastructure of the GENIUS Act stablecoin regime. The FDIC’s proposed rule — published April 10, 2026 — would require FDIC-supervised permitted payment stablecoin issuers (PPSIs) to maintain full BSA/AML and OFAC compliance programs, with FinCEN granted a 30-day pre-enforcement review right over new issuances. The OCC’s 376-page NPRM, published March 2, 2026, covers reserve asset standards, redemption mechanics, risk-management frameworks, and audit requirements. FinCEN’s parallel AML rulemaking closes the loop on Travel Rule applicability to stablecoin transfers.

Critically, the Federal Reserve — the only primary federal banking agency that has not yet issued an NPRM — remains conspicuously absent from the rulemaking queue. The GENIUS Act’s 18-month effective date (January 18, 2027) requires all federal agency rules to be final before the statute goes live. If the Fed does not publish its proposed rule within weeks, the effective date may slip or apply unevenly across Fed-supervised bank holding companies.

📎 Historical Precedent: The Durbin Amendment (Dodd–Frank §1075) triggered a similarly fragmented multi-agency rulemaking in 2010–2011; the Fed’s delay in finalising interchange-fee rules created a six-month legal vacuum that issuers exploited. The GENIUS Act’s Fed gap echoes that dynamic.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Today’s triple deadline is operationally significant for comment-letter strategy: firms that submitted to the OCC in May but not to the FDIC and FinCEN today have materially incomplete records. The FDIC rule’s pre-enforcement FinCEN review provision is novel — it creates a de facto dual-approval structure for new stablecoin launches that practitioners should flag in product-launch timelines. The Fed’s absence means bank-holding-company stablecoin subsidiaries face regulatory asymmetry until an FRB rule appears. The Brief’s read: The rulemaking infrastructure is advancing faster than legislative text, which is unusual and beneficial — but the Fed gap creates real compliance uncertainty for the largest would-be issuers (JPMorgan, Citi). Source: Federal Register / Freshfields / Morgan Lewis.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Several community bank groups argue the FDIC’s AML/CFT requirements for PPSIs are structurally identical to existing BSA obligations on deposit-taking institutions — meaning the FDIC rule adds compliance cost without adding protection, and effectively prices out state-chartered issuers below the $10 billion cap. The Brief’s rebuttal: The GENIUS Act’s explicit intent is to treat stablecoin issuers as quasi-banks for AML purposes; the duplication concern is accurate but unavoidable given that PPSIs hold reserve assets at Fed-member institutions. Source: Consumer Finance Monitor / Paul Hastings.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Tether (USDT) — which still has no Treasury “substantially similar” equivalency determination — faces the most acute risk: the foreign-issuer safe harbor under GENIUS expires July 18, 2026, creating a 39-day window. Circle (USDC) and Paxos (USDP), both OCC-chartered, are best positioned to meet the June 9 comment infrastructure. PayPal’s PYUSD faces the Fed-gap problem if it seeks bank-holding-company issuance. OCC Crypto Trust Charter Battle Escalates as Warren Deadline Passes Without Production — Nine Charters for Coinbase, Ripple, Circle and Others Challenged as Illegal

"The OCC has approved special charters for companies seeking to act like banks while evading bank rules — this is improper use of the agency’s trust charter authority and Congress never authorized it." Senator Elizabeth Warren, Senate Banking Committee Minority, May 2026 [paraphrased]

Senator Elizabeth Warren’s June 1 deadline for the OCC to produce communications regarding nine conditional crypto trust charter approvals passed without public confirmation of compliance, hardening what the crypto industry is calling “Choke Point 3.0.” The nine firms — Coinbase, Ripple, Circle, Paxos, BitGo, Fidelity, Crypto.com, Stripe, and Protego — received conditional national trust bank charter approvals between December 2025 and April 2026. Warren alleges the OCC violated the National Bank Act by approving firms conducting non-fiduciary activities including staking, lending, and stablecoin issuance.

The Digital Chamber of Commerce has formally defended the OCC’s chartering authority, citing the agency’s established power to issue limited-purpose trust charters under 12 U.S.C. §27 and interpretive letters dating to 2021. The American Bankers Association, however, has separately demanded the OCC pause all nine reviews until GENIUS Act rulemaking is complete — a position that aligns the traditional banking sector with Warren despite opposite motivations (the ABA objects to competitive threat; Warren objects to inadequate oversight).

📎 Historical Precedent: The 2018 OCC fintech charter effort was struck down by the Second Circuit in Lacewell v. OCC (2021), which held the OCC lacked authority to charter non-depository fintechs. The GENIUS Act’s new PPSI licensing pathway potentially resolves that precedent gap, but only if the statute’s rulemaking is complete — which it is not.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The legal vulnerability is narrower than Warren’s framing suggests. The OCC’s trust charter authority (12 U.S.C. §92a) is broad and the agency has issued non-depository trust charters since the 1860s. The novel issue is whether staking and stablecoin issuance — activities not traditionally associated with fiduciary administration — fall within the trust-incidental-powers doctrine. The Lacewell precedent applies to national bank charters, not limited-purpose trust charters, reducing Warren’s legal argument to a policy objection dressed in statutory language. The Brief’s read: The charters are legally defensible but politically exposed. The GENIUS Act’s final rules, once issued, will moot the authority question entirely — the fight is really about which administration controls the rulemaking narrative. Source: Warren Senate committee / Decrypt / CryptoNews.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The ABA’s demand for a pause — the traditional banking industry asking regulators to slow-roll crypto competitors — is a more commercially motivated objection than Warren’s. Large banks are structurally disadvantaged by crypto trust charters because chartered crypto firms can access Fed payment rails, FDIC messaging networks, and interbank systems without carrying the full deposit-insurance cost burden. The Brief’s rebuttal: The competitive asymmetry concern is legitimate and the ABA is right that issuing trust charters before GENIUS Act rules are final creates an unlevel playing field — but the solution is accelerating rulemaking, not pausing charters already conditionally approved. Source: CoinPedia / Digital Chamber.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Ripple’s RLUSD stablecoin expansion into Turkey and its OCC charter create a dual-track strategy: regulatory legitimacy in the US while capturing emerging-market demand. Circle’s USDC, armed with an OCC charter, is best positioned to partner with US banks under the GENIUS Act’s subsidiary-issuer pathway. Coinbase’s trust charter — the most recent approval (April 2, 2026) — is most exposed to a Warren-led Senate investigation given its timing after public lobbying. Source: CoinPedia / CryptoNews / CoinGape.

SEC’s Draft 2026–2030 Strategic Plan Centres Crypto and Formally Codifies the Retreat From Regulation-by-Enforcement

"Digital assets represent one of the most significant opportunities and challenges of our time — the Commission will provide a fit-for-purpose regulatory framework that fosters innovation while maintaining investor protection." SEC Draft Strategic Plan FY 2026–2030, published June 2, 2026 [paraphrased]

The SEC published its Draft Strategic Plan for Fiscal Years 2026–2030 on June 2, 2026, placing digital assets at the centre of a broad regulatory reset under Chairman Paul S. Atkins. The plan is open for public comment through July 2, 2026. It formally abandons the enforcement-volume metrics of the prior administration, directing staff to measure success by deterrence and market clarity rather than case counts or fine totals.

The plan’s crypto provisions build on the March 11, 2026, SEC-CFTC Memorandum of Understanding and the March 2026 token taxonomy interpretation (Release No. 33-11412), which provided the first comprehensive federal taxonomy for digital commodities, digital collectibles, stablecoins, and digital securities. The strategic plan commits to codifying jurisdictional boundaries between the SEC and CFTC — work that the CLARITY Act would accomplish legislatively if passed.

📎 Historical Precedent: The SEC’s 2003–2007 strategic plan, drafted under Chairman Donaldson after the Enron era, similarly recalibrated enforcement metrics from case volume to deterrence — but that shift preceded the 2008 crisis, in which the SEC’s lighter touch was widely blamed for inadequate oversight of structured products.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The strategic plan’s most actionable provision for practitioners is its explicit commitment to rule-based, rather than enforcement-driven, digital-asset regulation. This directly signals that the SEC will not pursue novel enforcement theories against crypto businesses in areas where the March 2026 token taxonomy has provided guidance. Practitioners should, however, note that the plan explicitly preserves anti-fraud and market-manipulation enforcement — the shift is away from “is this a security” actions, not away from insider-trading and wash-trade cases. The Brief’s read: The draft plan is the administrative formalisation of what the Crypto Task Force has been implementing since January 2025. Its real significance is downstream: it constrains future enforcement discretion and creates estoppel-like expectations that make any future administration’s regulatory reversal more legally and politically costly. Source: SEC.gov / EY / Bitcoin Magazine.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Strategic plans are non-binding policy documents. A future SEC chair can repudiate a strategic plan on day one without triggering APA notice-and-comment. The Gensler-era SEC also had a strategic plan that committed to “regulatory clarity” — then pursued enforcement-by-Wells-notice for four years. The Brief’s rebuttal: The distinction is that the Atkins plan is being implemented through actual rulemakings (token taxonomy, MOU, SEC-CFTC coordination) rather than speeches alone. Reversal would require unwinding completed regulatory instruments, not just discarding a document. Source: SEC.gov historical records / CoinDCX.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Protocol developers, exchange operators, and DeFi platforms operating in the US can now treat the SEC’s March 2026 token taxonomy as a stable legal baseline — the strategic plan commits to building on it rather than litigating around it. Traditional asset managers (BlackRock, Fidelity) who have already filed for tokenised fund structures gain the most: the plan’s tokenisation priority signals a fast lane for Rule 144A digital-security approvals. Offshore exchanges (Binance, OKX) face structural disadvantage as the US regulatory moat deepens for compliant domestic players. Source: Yahoo Finance / CoinSpaid Media / KuCoin.

EU AI Act Full-Application Clock Hits 54 Days — High-Risk System Obligations, Article 50 Transparency Rules, and €35M Penalty Regime All Activate on 2 August 2026

"The EU AI Act will be fully applicable on 2 August 2026 — high-risk AI systems must be conformity-assessed, registered, and operational with risk management, data governance, logging and human oversight from that date." European Commission AI Act Implementation Guidance, 2026

The EU AI Act enters full application on August 2, 2026 — 54 days from today. The August deadline activates obligations for Annex III high-risk AI systems across biometrics, critical infrastructure, employment, financial services, law enforcement, and border control. Simultaneously, Article 50 transparency rules require firms to disclose AI-generated content, label deepfakes, and notify users of automated decision-making in real time. The enforcement regime carries maximum fines of €35 million or 7% of global turnover — exceeding GDPR’s ceiling.

Separately, the EU’s AI Omnibus — adopted November 2025 with political agreement reached May 7, 2026 — centralises oversight of general-purpose AI (GPAI) models under the European AI Office, extends simplified requirements to small mid-caps, and expands regulatory sandbox access. Each EU member state must have designated national competent authorities and established at least one AI regulatory sandbox by August 2.

📎 Historical Precedent: GDPR’s May 2018 full-application date saw 12 national data-protection authorities unprepared to enforce; enforcement lag of 18–24 months was common. The EU AI Act faces the same member-state readiness gap — only Germany, France, and the Netherlands have fully designated national AI authorities as of June 2026.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

For financial-services practitioners, the highest-priority Annex III obligation is credit-scoring, insurance pricing, and employment-screening AI systems — all explicitly listed. Firms using algorithmic credit assessment that includes EU data subjects must have completed a conformity assessment, documented their risk management system, and registered the system in the EU AI database by August 2. Article 50 is equally actionable: any customer-facing chatbot or recommendation engine must carry a disclosure. The Brief’s read: The enforcement lag will likely repeat the GDPR pattern, but first-mover firms that invest in conformity assessments now gain a competitive advantage in EU procurement and institutional counterparty due diligence. Source: EU Commission / LegisScope / DataGuard.

🔄 Contrarian Take

US AI policy is moving in precisely the opposite direction — the White House’s March 2026 blueprint explicitly favours regulatory sandboxes and sector-specific rules over an EU-style horizontal mandate. Colorado’s repeal of its original AI Act (May 14, 2026) signals that even progressive US states are retreating from GDPR-analog AI obligations. The divergence argument holds that EU AI Act compliance creates a competitive tax on EU-based AI developers relative to US counterparts. The Brief’s rebuttal: Market-access dynamics will force US firms serving EU customers to comply regardless; the question is not whether to comply but which US compliance infrastructure can be leveraged to reduce the cost. Source: Cooley / Wilson Sonsini / Vorys.

🏗️ Industry Implications

EU-based AI infrastructure firms (SAP, Siemens, Palantir Europe) face the most immediate conformity-assessment burden. US hyperscalers (Microsoft Azure EU, Google Cloud EU) must ensure their EU-hosted AI services carry appropriate high-risk system documentation. Law firms with AI-assisted contract-review and legal-research tools deployed in the EU must assess whether those tools qualify as “employment” or “administration of justice” Annex III systems. Source: EU AI Office / Secure Privacy / Tredence.

ASIC No-Action Relief Expires in 21 Days — Crypto Platforms Serving Australian Users Must Lodge AFSL Applications by June 30 or Face Immediate Enforcement Risk

"After 30 June 2026, any firm providing financial services in relation to digital asset financial products must have lodged an AFS licence application — there will be no further extensions to this transitional relief." ASIC, Digital Asset Compliance Deadline Guidance, 2026

ASIC’s class no-action position under INFO 225 — which has allowed crypto platforms to operate without an Australian Financial Services Licence since October 2023 — expires on June 30, 2026. After that date, any firm providing financial services in relation to digital asset financial products that has not lodged a complete AFSL application faces enforcement action. Separately, firms intending to apply for a Market Licence or Clearing and Settlement facility licence must notify ASIC of intent by the same date.

The Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Act 2026 (DAF Act), which received Royal Assent on April 8, 2026, establishes the permanent licensing regime for digital asset platforms (DAPs) and tokenised custody platforms (TCPs). Full commencement is April 2027, but the June 30 deadline functions as a pre-qualification filter: only firms that have lodged AFSL applications by June 30 will be eligible for the DAP transitional registration pathway under the DAF Act.

📎 Historical Precedent: ASIC’s 2014 managed investment scheme licensing transition for retail online platforms resulted in approximately 30% of operators ceasing Australian operations rather than obtaining licences. A similar attrition rate among smaller crypto platforms would materially consolidate the Australian market.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The June 30 deadline is the most operationally urgent international compliance event for any global crypto platform with Australian users — including US-headquartered firms that have not localised their compliance stack. The AFSL application process typically takes 3–6 months for final approval, meaning firms lodging on June 30 will operate in a “lodgement pending” status for the balance of 2026. ASIC has indicated it will not take enforcement action against firms with lodged applications during the assessment period. The Brief’s read: Non-lodging platforms should begin geofencing Australian users by June 30 as a stopgap — the enforcement risk from operating unlicensed post-deadline is acute, as ASIC has already shown willingness to pursue foreign-domiciled platforms under the PSA extended territorial reach. Source: ASIC / Piper Alderman / MCO.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Some market participants argue the DAF Act’s April 2027 commencement effectively provides another year of runway, and that ASIC will de-prioritise enforcement against smaller platforms that miss the June 30 AFSL deadline if they are genuinely preparing for DAP licensing. The Brief’s rebuttal: ASIC has explicitly stated “no further extensions” and has a track record of following through; the managed-investment-scheme precedent involved enforcement action even against platforms that claimed good-faith preparation. Source: ASIC media release / Ledger Insights.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Global exchanges with Australian operations (Binance AU, Kraken AU, Coinbase International) face the most acute deadline pressure. ASIC’s stablecoin and wrapped-token relief instrument (ASIC Corporations (Stablecoin and Wrapped Token Relief) Instrument 2025/867) provides transitional relief for specific product categories, but it does not extend the AFSL lodgement deadline for platform operators. The Australian market — the world’s 9th largest by crypto trading volume — will consolidate sharply post-June 30. Source: MCO / CoinDCX / Transak.

UpdateFCA Cryptoasset Perimeter Guidance Consultation Closed June 3 — Seven Activity Categories Formally In-Scope; Policy Statement Expected This Autumn Ahead of September Authorization Gateway

"This perimeter guidance consultation complements our substantive rulebook work by clarifying which activities fall within the scope of the new UK cryptoasset regime — final policy statements will be published this summer." FCA CP26/13 Accompanying Guidance Note, April 2026 [paraphrased]

The FCA’s CP26/13 consultation on cryptoasset perimeter guidance closed on June 3, 2026. The consultation mapped seven activities into scope: issuing qualifying stablecoins in the UK; safeguarding or arranging safeguarding of qualifying cryptoassets; operating a qualifying cryptoasset trading platform; dealing as principal; dealing as agent; arranging deals; and arranging qualifying cryptoasset staking. The guidance followed the statutory instrument made in Parliament on February 4, 2026, which created the legal foundation for the UK’s full crypto regime.

The FCA has confirmed that the September 2026 authorisation gateway will open on schedule, with the full crypto regime live from October 2027. Crucially, Travers Smith has flagged that legislative changes to the regulated-activities perimeter are being made simultaneously with the FCA’s consultation — creating the risk that firms map activities to guidance that is immediately superseded by statutory amendments. The “24-hour trap” — the FCA’s proposed requirement for transaction reversal within 24 hours — remains in the draft rulebook and is drawing sharp industry criticism as operationally unworkable for international settlement chains.

📎 Historical Precedent: The FCA’s 2020–2023 crypto-asset registration regime (under the MLRs) resulted in approximately 85% of applications being refused or withdrawn. The new authorisation regime, with broader scope, may produce a similar attrition rate among smaller applicants.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The most actionable FCA guidance provision is the staking classification: arranging qualifying cryptoasset staking is explicitly a regulated activity under CP26/13. This means protocol-level liquid staking providers, liquid staking derivative issuers, and exchange-based staking-as-a-service products all require FCA authorisation. Law firms advising DeFi-adjacent products should flag this immediately. The simultaneous legislative changes noted by Travers Smith create a compliance-mapping problem: firms that have already conducted gap analyses against CP26/13 must re-run them against any amended statutory perimeter. The Brief’s read: The September gateway is a hard deadline for firms that intend to serve UK retail clients. Authorisation timelines of 12–18 months mean firms applying in September 2026 may not be authorised until late 2027 — missing the October 2027 full-regime live date. CFTC Prediction Markets Rulemaking Reaches White House OIRA Review — First Federal Framework for Political, Sports, and Economic Event Contracts Advances Toward Final Rule

"The CFTC is seeking information on statutory core principles applicable to prediction markets, the types of event contracts that may be prohibited as contrary to the public interest, and cost-benefit considerations — a fundamental rethink of a three-decade regulatory framework." CFTC ANPRM, Federal Register Vol. 91, Doc. 2026-05105, March 16, 2026

The CFTC’s prediction-markets rulemaking has reached the White House Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs (OIRA) review stage as of late May 2026, signalling that a formal notice of proposed rulemaking (NPRM) is imminent. The regulatory trajectory began in March 2026 with an Advanced Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (ANPRM) seeking comment on event contract regulations — the comment period closed April 30. Simultaneously, the CFTC formally withdrew its prior proposed rule restricting political and sports event contracts, reversing a 2023 position.

The CFTC’s March 12, 2026 staff advisory guidance for designated contract markets (DCMs) clarified current regulations applicable to prediction-market listings, providing the first formal regulatory safe harbor for platforms like Kalshi and Polymarket operating under CFTC oversight. President Trump has publicly endorsed federal control over prediction markets, creating a White House policy tailwind that will likely accelerate the NPRM timeline.

📎 Historical Precedent: The CFTC’s approval of Kalshi’s Congressional-control contracts in 2023 — after a court battle that overturned the CFTC’s own block — established that political event contracts are legally permissible under the Commodity Exchange Act absent an explicit public-interest prohibition. The ANPRM is the first regulatory effort to build a positive framework rather than litigate case-by-case.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The OIRA review stage means a formal NPRM is likely within 60–90 days. Practitioners advising prediction-market operators should begin preparing for the new NPRM’s comment window, which will be the definitive opportunity to shape the scope of permissible event contracts. Key open questions include the public-interest prohibition threshold for election-related contracts, position limits for prediction-market contracts, and whether state gaming laws can co-exist with CFTC federal preemption. The staff advisory already provides sufficient comfort for Kalshi and Polymarket to expand product scope under existing DCM authorisation. The Brief’s read: The White House backing ensures the prediction-market NPRM will not be withdrawn — this is a done deal institutionally. The legal risk shifts to state-level gaming and lottery authority challenges, not federal reversal. Source: CFTC / CoinDesk / Norton Rose.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Consumer-advocacy groups and several state attorneys general argue that unrestricted federal prediction markets on political and sporting outcomes effectively legalise sports betting without consumer protections — and that CFTC jurisdictional preemption of state gaming laws is constitutionally dubious. The Brief’s rebuttal: The Commodity Exchange Act’s broad preemption clause has survived constitutional challenges before; the state-law conflict is real but navigable through NPRM carve-outs for sports betting (which is already legalised in 38 states via PASPA repeal). Source: Crowell & Moring / SBA Office of Advocacy / Sidley Austin.
