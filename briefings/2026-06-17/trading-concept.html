Alpha — Open Interest Dynamics

Wed · 17 June 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #52 11 min read
TL;DR

Open interest is the count of derivative contracts still open — not how many traded, but how much exposure is still standing once the trading stops; it tells you whether a move is backed by fresh conviction or just positions being unwound. Read OI's direction against price's direction and a four-box decoder falls out: rising price on rising OI is real demand, rising price on falling OI is short-covering that fades, and falling price on falling OI — like BTC's −22% OI flush on 4 June — is longs being liquidated, not new sellers arriving.

§01
What It Is
Definition · the third dimension
Open interest is the number of futures or perpetual contracts still open — the market's standing exposure, not its trading activity.

Price tells you direction. Volume tells you how much changed hands. Open interest tells you something neither can: how much money is still committed to the trade after the dust settles. Ignore it and you will mistake a short-covering bounce for real demand — and stand in front of a crowded book right before it flushes.

Formally, open interest is the total number of derivative contracts — futures or perpetual swaps — that have been opened and not yet closed, expired, or settled. One new buyer matched with one new seller creates exactly one unit of OI. When an existing long and an existing short close against each other, that unit disappears. If a new trader simply takes over an existing position from someone exiting, OI does not move at all — the contract still exists, only its owner changed.

That last case is the whole point. Volume can be enormous while OI sits flat — that is positions changing hands, not new risk arriving. OI rising means net-new contracts are being created; OI falling means net contracts are being torn up. In crypto perps there is no expiry, so OI is a clean, continuous read on how much leverage is parked in the market right now.

§02
Mechanics
The four-box decoder
Pair the sign of ΔOI with the sign of the price change and four distinct stories fall out — three of them tradeable, one a trap dressed as a trend.

The signal comes from one pairing: the direction of price against the direction of open interest over the same window. Each is just a sign — up or down — so there are four combinations. This is the price/OI matrix futures traders have used since long before perps existed; John J. Murphy codified it in Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, and it maps cleanly onto crypto.

ΔOI = OI(t) − OI(t−1) Notional OI (USD) = OI (contracts) × price Price ↑ + OI ↑ → new longs, real trend [CONFIRM] Price ↑ + OI ↓ → short covering, hollow [FADE] Price ↓ + OI ↑ → new shorts, real downtrend [CONFIRM] Price ↓ + OI ↓ → long liquidation / unwind [WASHOUT]
Sign, not size, drives the read — but the larger the ΔOI, the louder the signal.
StateWhat it meansPosture
Price ↑ · OI ↑New longs paying up; fresh money entering with conviction.Trend is real — trail it, don't fade it.

Crypto adds two refinements. First, read OI in notional USD, not contracts — a 5% OI rise at $66K is more leverage than the same rise at $59K. Second, overlay funding. When OI climbs while funding sits at an extreme — BTC is at the 99th percentile of its weekly range today, roughly 7% annualised — the new money is one-sided and expensive to hold. That is the exact fuel a liquidation cascade burns, which is why this issue builds directly on the liquidation cascade (Issue #48, 13 Jun) and trapped traders (Issue #45, 10 Jun): OI is the inventory those mechanics consume.

§04
In Practice
Confluence, not a standalone
Never trade OI alone — use it to grade a move's quality: does the price action have committed money behind it, or none at all?
1

Grade the breakout

When price clears a level, check ΔOI on the break. Breakout with rising OI means participants are pressing the new range — it tends to hold. Breakout on flat or falling OI means nobody is backing it; treat it as fade-prone until OI confirms.

2

Spot the hollow bounce

A rally on falling OI is shorts buying back, not buyers arriving. It runs only until the shorts are done, then stalls. Sell strength into resistance rather than chase a move with no new demand underneath it.

3

Map the fuel

Rising notional OI at extreme funding marks a crowded, one-sided book. That OI is the liquidation fuel — the bigger it gets, the harder the eventual flush. Treat record OI + extreme funding as a risk warning, not a trend confirmation.

4

Read the washout

Falling price on collapsing OI is longs being flushed, not fresh sellers arriving. Once OI is spent, the downside fuel is gone — that is where reversals start, not where you panic-sell into the hole.

§07
Common Traps
Where OI misleads
The biggest mistake is reading rising OI as automatically bullish — OI is directionless; it only confirms whichever way price is already moving.
1
Treating OI as directional

Rising OI is neither bullish nor bearish on its own — it only says new money is committing. Always pair it with price. "OI is pumping" with no price context is noise, not a signal.

2
Watching contracts, not notional

Quoting OI in coins or contracts hides how leverage changes as price moves. Track notional USD (OI × price) and OI/MCap, or you will misjudge how crowded the book actually is — especially after a big price swing.

3
Confusing volume with OI

Huge volume can leave OI dead flat — that is positions changing hands, not new risk arriving. Trade a high-volume, flat-OI session as a fresh trend and you are trading churn.

4
Reading extreme OI as strength

Record-high OI at extreme funding is a crowded, fragile book — not a strong trend. It is the setup for a cascade, not a reason to add. That was the lesson of 4 June: peak OI marked the top, not the launchpad.

5
The "OI is predictive" overclaim — overhyped

OI is descriptive, not a crystal ball. Much exchange-published OI is end-of-period and aggregated across venues with different contract conventions, so cross-exchange "OI heatmap" products often double-count or lag. OI tells you what positioning is — not where price goes next. Anyone selling it as a leading indicator is selling hype.
