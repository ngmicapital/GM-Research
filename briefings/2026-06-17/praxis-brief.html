Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

Four sources in June 2026 — Housel, Clear, Lamade, and Mollick — converge on one truth: looking outward at rivals, odds, and approval is the root of poor decisions. The elite instinct, across investing, performance psychology, and AI work, is always to look inward at process, calibration, and judgment first.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Essay · Morgan Housel · Very Bad Advice · June 2026

The Fastest Path to Self-Destruction Is Outsourcing Your Compass

Morgan Housel's latest Collaborative Fund essay inverts the usual success playbook. Instead of asking what great investors and decision-makers do, he catalogues the beliefs that reliably produce failure — and the list reveals a common mechanism: outsourcing your internal compass to external signals.

The items that cluster at the top of his list are all variants of the same error: letting envy guide your goals; adjusting your willingness to believe something by how much you want it to be true; valuing the appearance of looking busy; prioritising defending what you already believe over learning something new; assuming all your success is due to your own hard work and all your failure is due to bad luck. Each one involves calibrating to the wrong dial.

"It's often hard to know what will bring joy but easy to spot what will bring misery. Building a house is complex; destroying one is simple. When trying to get ahead it can be helpful to flip things around, focusing on how to not fall back." — Morgan Housel, Collaborative Fund

The inversion framing matters: positive advice is contested and context-dependent. Bad advice is shockingly universal. Housel's catalogue is, at its core, a map of externally-referencing psychology — and the implicit antidote to each item is to turn the reference point inward.

Newsletter · James Clear · 3-2-1 · 4 June 2026

Average Looks Out. Elite Looks In.

James Clear's June 4 issue of the 3-2-1 newsletter put the thesis in the bluntest possible terms. Writing on what separates average performers from elite ones, he offered a single clean distinction:

"Compete against yourself. When you look outside — your rivals, your industry, your luck — there is always something to blame. When you look inside — your process, your effort, your rate of learning — there is always something to improve. Average looks out. Elite looks in."

The issue also featured lessons from Mike Deegan, head coach of the Denison University baseball team, on what it actually takes to win: "Show up every day. Work hard. Do it with joy. Care more about others than we do ourselves." Deegan's list shares the same logic — none of the four items are about rivals, rankings, or outcomes. They are all about internal conduct.

Clear's second idea in the same issue reinforced the point from another angle: "Your response to a challenge defines you more than the challenge itself." The challenge is external and uncontrollable. The response is internal and is entirely yours.

Essay · Ted Lamade · Collaborative Fund · 10 June 2026

We Outsource Our Judgment to Odds We Don't Understand — and Pay for It in Stress

Ted Lamade's Collaborative Fund piece, "Our Achilles Heel," zeroes in on a specific flavour of outward-looking that produces chronic misery: probability blindness. We calibrate our self-worth and our decisions to odds we can't actually compute.

His examples are deliberately mundane: youth travel programs charge families thousands of dollars by implying they unlock pathways to scholarships, despite long odds. Sports franchises sell season-ticket packages to fans who rationally overestimate championship likelihood. Investment managers pitch market-beating strategies despite most failing to outperform a passive index over any sustained period. In each case, people are comparing themselves to an external benchmark they have systematically miscalculated.

The opening anecdote is pointed: a student devastated by rejection from a university that admits fewer than 6% of applicants. Asked how many high schools exist in the United States, the student guessed 12,000. The real number is closer to 25,000. The student had never understood the base-rate scale of the competition — and had therefore imported enormous external stress against a benchmark he could not accurately read.

Lamade's conclusion: when you find something you genuinely love, don't let miscalibrated odds become your Achilles heel. Understand the probabilities first — then decide whether the pursuit is worth it. Many of humanity's greatest achievements began with someone who understood the odds clearly and chose to pursue the dream anyway.

Newsletter · Ethan Mollick · One Useful Thing · 4 June 2026

The Human Advantage Isn't Raw Output — It's Knowing Where Not to Outsource Judgment

Ethan Mollick's "Co-Existence and the End of Co-Intelligence" marks a shift in his thinking about AI and work, timed to accompany his forthcoming book Co-Existence. The essay argues that the co-intelligence model — human at the centre, AI as helpful collaborator — was never the long-term design intent of AI systems. The actual goal of frontier AI labs has always been "highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work."

This creates what Mollick calls the "jagged frontier": AI capability is not uniformly ahead of or behind human capability. It is uneven — dramatically strong in some domains, surprisingly weak in others. The critical human skill is not producing more output; it is knowing which side of the frontier you are on for any given task, and retaining judgment and autonomy when it matters.

The people who will build a durable human advantage are not those who use AI for everything, nor those who refuse it. They are those who can accurately distinguish where AI systems are more reliable than their own cognition — and where they are not. That is an internal calibration problem, not an external one.

The parallel with Housel and Clear is precise: the risk with AI is the same as the risk with envy, rivals, and miscalibrated odds. You can surrender your judgment wholesale to a system designed to persuade and imitate. Or you can look inward first, then decide when and how to engage the external tool.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · Housel + Clear + Lamade + Mollick

Build an Internal Compass Before You Reach for the External One

The convergence across these four sources suggests a single meta-skill: before consulting any external signal — a rival's performance, a market probability, an AI recommendation, an approval metric — establish your own position first. The error in each domain is the same: importing the external reading before you know what your own instruments say.

  • Run the inversion test weekly. Housel's method: don't ask "what would success look like?" Ask instead "which beliefs, if I held them, would reliably produce failure?" The list is faster to generate and more universally applicable. Flag any belief that requires an external reference point (envy, comparison, approval-seeking) as a candidate for removal.
  • Audit your scorecards. Clear's "average looks out" test applied practically: for any goal or project you're tracking, ask whether your primary metric is internal (your rate of learning, your effort quality, your process fidelity) or external (ranking, revenue vs. competitors, likes). External metrics aren't wrong — but they should be secondary. If they are primary, you've handed your compass to someone else.
  • Calculate before you feel. Lamade's probability exercise: when you're stressed by a rejection, a competitive threat, or a low-odds situation, pause to actually compute the base rate. How many people are competing? What does the historical distribution look like? Stress that survives accurate probability-calculation is real signal. Stress that dissolves when you run the numbers was borrowed from a miscalibrated external benchmark.
  • Map your jagged frontier. Mollick's framework applied: for your own domain of work, sketch out which tasks AI currently outperforms you on and which it doesn't. Be honest about both sides. The goal is not to feel threatened or smug — it is to know where your judgment is the scarce input, so you protect it rather than outsource it by default.

The shared underlying move: externalising your reference point is seductive because it feels like gathering data. But external signals are noisy, often miscalibrated, and designed (by competitors, by markets, by AI systems, by social media) to pull your behaviour toward theirs. Internal calibration is harder and slower — and that is precisely why it is where durable advantage lives.

Practice · James Clear · June 2026

The "Three Selves" Frame: Which Version of You Is Running the Decision?

Clear's June 11 issue introduced a useful psychological taxonomy for how to relate to time and self-judgement — what he called the "three selves" frame. It is a practical tool for internal calibration across different timeframes.

  • Be forgiving with your past self. What's done is done. No productive information comes from beating yourself up about past decisions. This is not complacency — it is clearing the noise so you can see the present clearly.
  • Be strict with your present self. Win the moment in front of you. This is where the internal compass matters most: not retrospective comparison with past outcomes, not prospective anxiety about future rankings — the quality of your current effort.
  • Be flexible with your future self. There are many paths to any meaningful outcome. Rigidly optimising toward one external benchmark (one school, one career path, one definition of success) forecloses optionality and imports the worst of external-reference psychology into your long-term planning.

The three-selves frame pairs cleanly with Mollick's jagged frontier: your past self operated with different tools than your future self will. The rigidity that locks you into a fixed external target also locks you out of adapting as the frontier shifts.

Mollick's concept from Co-Intelligence and elaborated in his June 2026 "Co-Existence" newsletter: AI capability is not a uniform curve above or below human performance. Concept

Base Rate Calibration — Ted Lamade, Collaborative Fund

Before acting on any probability-laden decision (competitive odds, investment returns, career choices), compute the base rate independently. Not the rate you've been sold, and not the one you'd like to be true — the historical distributional number. Lamade's essay "Our Achilles Heel" works through several domains where people systematically mis-import external probabilities. collabfund.com/blog.

04 On the Horizon
AI

The "Choosing to Stay Human" Trend Is Accelerating

Mollick's May 2026 essay "Choosing to Stay Human" flagged that social media is increasingly saturated with AI-generated posts, comments, and interactions. As AI content becomes ambient, the deliberate choice to produce something demonstrably human — uneven, idiosyncratic, personally accountable — may become a signal of quality rather than inefficiency. Watch for premium human-certification layers emerging in content, advice, and creative work.

Mind

Innumeracy as the Next Behavioural Finance Frontier

Lamade's probability piece sits within a growing body of research on how natural-number bias distorts health and financial decisions. The 2026 SABE Annual Conference (University of Michigan, June 23-25) will focus on exactly this intersection. Practical numeracy — understanding distributions, base rates, and the difference between ratios and raw counts — is emerging as a teachable, high-leverage cognitive skill, not just an academic curiosity.

Work

Performance Benchmarks Are Shifting From Outputs to Process Fidelity

Clear's "elite looks in" frame aligns with a broader management shift. As AI handles more measurable outputs (content volume, code generation, research synthesis), internal process quality — judgment, taste, consistency of effort, intellectual honesty — becomes harder to replicate and easier to lose if you stop tracking it. Expect performance frameworks in knowledge work to start incorporating process-quality metrics alongside traditional output KPIs.

Finance

The "Lifestyle Compounding" Problem in Personal Finance

The Morgan Housel quote surfaced in Clear's June 11 newsletter captures a tension that personal finance mostly ignores: "If I need half as much money to be happy while your lifestyle compounds as fast as your assets, I'm better off than you are." As markets remain volatile and real return expectations moderate, the question of what you're actually trying to accumulate toward is becoming financially material — not just philosophical. Expect more advisers to build explicit lifestyle-calibration work into the planning process.
