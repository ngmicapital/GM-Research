Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

When AI can produce expert-level work on demand, the scarce skill is judging whether the output is good enough, and that judgment cannot be handed off along with the task. Ethan Mollick's Fable 5.2 tests show broad capability with a gap in self-assessment: the model shipped a joyless course and a bloated rulebook, and Mollick had to catch both himself. Morgan Housel's argument that a good bullshit detector is among the most valuable modern skills describes the same gap from the reader's side, and Pat O'Connell's weekly read of the same debate lands in the same place. The practical move is to write the quality bar before asking for the work, then filter every persuasive input against it.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Newsletter · Ethan Mollick · “Fable 5.2 and Jagged AGI” · Nov 9, 2026

AI can now do expert-level work across many domains, but it still cannot reliably tell when its own output falls short

Mollick put the new Claude Fable 5.2 through tasks far outside coding: a hardware hack that yielded six working games, a business plan screened from 22 ideas, a reconstructed Auden syllabus, and a 182-page entrepreneurship paper. On most individual tasks he judged the results to meet his own bar of work as good as most human experts. The failures he found were not the obvious made-up facts of earlier models. The model produced a course that was accurate but joyless, and it buried a creative project under a rulebook that grew to 204 sections and 87,500 words.

What matters is who caught these problems. The critic and fact-checker agents the model deployed hunted for errors, but none of them asked whether the work was too long, too repetitive, or dull. Mollick had to notice those things and fix them himself. He frames the gap plainly: knowing when your own work is not good enough is a large part of what makes someone an expert, and it is the part the model does least reliably.

The frontier of AI capability is broad. The frontier of AI self-assessment is far narrower, and that gap is where human judgment still does the work.

Essay · Morgan Housel · “Little Rules About Big Things” · Collaborative Fund, undated

When output becomes cheap, the scarce asset is the filter that separates good work from confident noise

Housel's list of rules about big things contains a line that reads as a direct answer to Mollick's problem: “Few things are as valuable in the modern world as a good bullshit detector.” He writes about markets and personal money, not AI, but the logic transfers. When a plausible answer costs almost nothing to produce, the people who keep their footing are the ones who can spot an answer that is plausible yet wrong, unfinished, or padded.

Housel also describes how strongly people crave certainty, which explains why the filter is so easy to skip. A polished draft from a model, or a confident analyst's chart, delivers the same relief as an answer that has actually been checked. The discipline is to treat fluency as a reason to look harder, not as evidence of quality.

Convergence · Mollick, Housel and The & Report #46 · Nov 9 to Nov 14, 2026

The bottleneck moves to whoever sets the standard, because a vague request produces work nobody can judge

Mollick admits that some of the rulebook problem started with his own request to “polish” existing worlds. The agents read that as a demand for blind-vote improvement and then built process around it. The lesson is not that the model misbehaved. An underspecified target gives the executor room to optimise for the wrong thing. The & Report's summary of his early-access notes compresses the point into one line: the model can do the work, but deciding whether it is good still falls to you.

Housel's bullshit detector and Mollick's specification problem are the same skill seen from two sides. One is the reader's ability to reject weak output. The other is the author's ability to define good before the work begins. Both depend on a quality bar that exists in writing before anything is produced.

Producing work gets cheaper every month. Defining what good looks like and checking against it is the expensive part, and it cannot be delegated along with the task.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · drawn from Mollick

Write the quality bar in one paragraph before you ask for any AI output

The fixes Mollick needed took minutes, but he only made them because he was watching for failures the model could not see in its own work. A written standard turns that watching into a checklist you can run on every output, whether it came from a model, a colleague, or your own draft.

  • Define done in writing. Name the audience, the length, the form, and the one thing the output must do well before you start.
  • List the failures you already dislike. Mollick's cases were joyless, repetitive, and overbuilt. Put those words in the brief so the checker has to test for them.
  • Ask for a critique against your bar, then read the result yourself. A second pass can surface problems, but the final judgment stays with you.
  • Stop when the output grows faster than its value. A rulebook that gets longer each round is a sign the process is drifting, not improving.

The standard is the real work. Everything after it is execution.

Practice · drawn from Housel

Run every persuasive input through a bullshit check before it shapes a decision

Housel's point about the scarcity of a good filter applies to trading ideas, market commentary, and the steady flow of confident analysis. The check is not cynicism. It is a short routine that asks what would have to be true for a claim to hold, and what would show it was wrong.

  • Separate fluency from evidence. A clean chart or an articulate post earns a second look, not a position.
  • Find the one number that would break the thesis. Write it down before you commit capital or time.
  • Check whether the author gains if you agree. Note any incentive that sits behind the claim.
  • Log the claims you accepted that later failed. A monthly review of those misses sharpens the filter faster than reading more.

Primary Source

Ethan Mollick, “Fable 5.2 and Jagged AGI”

The core text for this issue. Mollick sets out a working definition of AGI, runs the model through hardware hacking, business planning, research, and world-building, and records exactly where the work fell short. The sections on the rulebook and on self-assessment are worth reading slowly.

Book

Ethan Mollick, Co-Existence: The Next Phase of AI

A field guide to the in-between years when machines are sometimes, but not always, smarter than the people using them. It extends the jagged-capability argument into how individuals and organisations should live with that unevenness. Its predecessor, Co-Intelligence, is the natural starting point if you have not read it.

Primary Source

Morgan Housel, “Little Rules About Big Things”

A short Collaborative Fund list of rules about money, risk, and judgment. Each line is compact enough to test against your own decisions. It is the source of the bullshit-detector line that anchors this issue's second card.

Book

Morgan Housel, The Psychology of Money

The standing reference for how behaviour, not arithmetic, drives most financial outcomes. Read it as a companion to the filter habit: most of the errors it describes come from accepting a confident story without asking what it leaves out.

A compact weekly read that picks up the same jagged-capability debate and frames it around the cost of a finished task rather than the price of a token. It is a quick way to check how the Mollick argument is landing with working practitioners.

04 On the Horizon
AI

Jagged progress will stay jagged for a while, and the self-assessment gap becomes the metric to watch

Mollick closes by wondering how long this phase of uneven capability will last. If models keep gaining breadth faster than they gain the ability to judge their own output, the useful benchmark shifts from what a model can produce to how reliably it flags its own failures.

Work

Expert roles shift from producing work toward specifying and reviewing it

If the tasks a model can do grow longer and more varied, the valuable part of a professional's job moves upstream into defining the brief and downstream into checking the result. That is the same bottleneck the convergence card identifies: whoever sets the standard holds the leverage.

Mind

Filtering becomes a trainable habit rather than a personality trait

Housel's point that a good bullshit detector is so valuable suggests the skill will be rewarded more as the cost of plausible output falls. Readers who build a routine for checking fluency against evidence, and who keep a log of their own misses, will be better placed as the flow of confident content keeps rising.
