TL;DR — Driving Thesis

Anthropic has moved from filing to a set IPO price range of $70 to $78 a share, with pricing expected on 18 November and Nasdaq trading the day after. Thinking Machines also released the open weights for its 594B-parameter Inkling 2 Small under an Apache 2.0 license, and llama.cpp reached its first official 1.0 release.

Two things to act on: first, treat the Anthropic IPO terms (825 million Class A shares at $70 to $78, pricing around 18 November, Nasdaq trading as ANT on 19 November) as the date to re-check your AI-equity exposure, and read the final price before the open. Second, if you run self-hosted inference, pull the Inkling 2 Small weights into a private eval harness before changing any production workload, because the 31 Intelligence Index score is vendor-reported. Three to monitor: whether Bittensor subnets actually opt into the new Gamma payments, whether Venice's data-centre milestone lands in November, and whether follow-up research confirms the MCP tool-poisoning rates.

Model Landscape

The open-weights tier moved again: Thinking Machines has released the weights for its 594B Inkling 2 Small under Apache 2.0, which makes it the most permissively licensed model of its size on the table.

Inkling 2 Small goes to open weights

Thinking Machines Lab posted the Inkling 2 Small weights on Hugging Face after a period of closed testing. The model is a mixture-of-experts design with 594B total parameters and roughly 17B active per token. The company reports an Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index score of 31 and says it tops open-weights rankings on DesignArena. It also lists day-one support for SGLang, vLLM and Unsloth. The model was first shown on 5 November with a promise of weights after safety testing, so the release arrived about nine days later.

What it means: a 594B open model under Apache terms changes the self-hosting calculation more than any single benchmark does. Teams that have been paying for frontier API access now have a credible private option. The roughly 394 GB file size makes this a cluster question rather than a laptop one. Treat the Index score as a vendor-reported figure until independent evaluators rerun it.

⚡ Update: Gemini 4 Hydrogen adds a speed and reasoning-level dial

Google's Gemini 4 Hydrogen was covered in yesterday's issue, so this is only the builder detail. Google says it completes tasks 2.5 times faster than Gemini 3.8 Flash and exposes four reasoning levels, which means a developer can pick a reasoning budget per request instead of per model tier. It is rolling out in Search and is also reachable through the Gemini API, AI Studio, the Gemini app and Antigravity.

Claude Ecosystem

Anthropic's headlines this week belong to the IPO, but the product line kept moving: the Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 release describes agents that coordinate and hand work to one another.

Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2: the delegation pitch

Anthropic's 9 November product release for Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2 leads with coding and multi-step reasoning, then gives robotics control as a use case. The release also gives explicit examples of models that coordinate with each other and delegate subtasks across agents [paraphrased]. That is the more interesting part. The unit being sold is shifting from a single chat turn to a supervised team of model calls.

For anyone building on the API, the practical consequence is operational. Per-agent token budgets, logged handoffs and a clear owner for each delegated step become necessary once models are allowed to hand work to other models. The Fable 5.2 ranking story from 13 November covered the leaderboard position; this release is the reason that position matters for multi-agent builds.

Claude Code and SDK: nothing confirmed this window

I did not confirm a new Claude Code or Agent SDK release in the last 24 hours, so none is claimed here. Watch the Anthropic release notes for any SDK change that accompanies the Mythos delegation features, since those would need matching client-side support.

Decentralized AI

Decentralized AI this week was about infrastructure plumbing and visibility, not price: Bittensor added opt-in subnet payments, Venice kept cutting token issuance, and an AI benchmark index added five crypto AI projects to its watchlist.

Bittensor ships wallet-based subnet payments

The Openτensor Foundation opened Gamma Payments for subnets on 11 November. Users pay for subnet services with wallet-based credits, but each subnet has to opt in before it accepts them. On 6 November the foundation also added three post-quantum signing methods covering transfers, staking and validator operations, though that protection only becomes effective after a mainnet upgrade. The opt-in design means adoption is a per-subnet decision to watch, not a network-wide switch.

Venice keeps tightening supply

Venice lowered annual VVV emissions from 2.5 million to 2 million, effective 1 October, and routes all new emissions to stakers. Cumulative burns are above 33.7 million VVV, and the company describes the direction as net deflationary. A calendar aggregator lists a Data Center Online milestone for November 2026, but I could not confirm it from a primary Venice source, so treat it as unverified.

Visibility, not adoption: the AI watchlist

An AI benchmarking index revised its watchlist on 11 November to include Bittensor, Render, Akash, FET and Virtuals Protocol. The coverage itself notes this adds visibility rather than evidence of adoption. Akash has no fresh milestone in this window; its October proposal to leave its own Cosmos chain remains the item to track.

Open-Source Pulse

The open-source tier had a strong day: a 594B open-weights release landed alongside llama.cpp reaching its first official 1.0.

llama.cpp and ggml reach version 1.0

llama.cpp, the standard software for running models on personal computers, and its ggml foundation reached official version 1.0 on 13 November. A stable 1.0 matters most to the downstream wrappers and local-inference front ends that depend on it, because they can now pin to a versioned runtime instead of tracking a moving target.

Inkling 2 Small on Hugging Face

The Apache 2.0 weights for Inkling 2 Small are the story here, covered in Model Landscape above. The open-source angle to watch is quantised builds: once community GGUF conversions appear, the model becomes practical on far smaller hardware than the 394 GB original implies.

Perplexity's Decider v1.3

Perplexity released Decider v1.3 on 12 November, a model that classifies the type of incoming question. It is a small release, but routers like this are increasingly how agent stacks decide which downstream model to call, so it is worth noting for anyone building query routing.

Deals & Regulation

Anthropic's IPO has moved from filing to a fixed price band, making this the most consequential AI corporate event on the calendar this week.

⚡ Update: Anthropic sets a $70 to $78 range, pricing on 18 November

Anthropic launched its IPO roadshow on 10 November with an expected price of $70.00 to $78.00 per share. The offering is 825 million Class A shares, of which about 801 million come from the company and about 23.6 million from existing stockholders. The company will not receive proceeds from the selling holders' shares. The stock is set to list on the Nasdaq Global Select Market as ANT, with final pricing expected on or about 18 November and trading on 19 November. This follows the coverage of the roadshow opening in the 13 and 14 November issues; the new information is the fixed range and timetable.

The roadshow deck, as reported, is 57 pages and mentions compute 49 times, which tells you the pitch is capacity. It also sets a long-term adjusted operating margin target above 35%. Reported third-quarter 2026 revenue is about $16.96 billion, a twelvefold increase on 2025, and the adjusted margin of about 15% sits against a GAAP figure near -3%. That gap between adjusted and GAAP results is the number investors will press on at pricing.

Pricing is the next test

Final pricing will show whether the top of the $70 to $78 band holds. A price at the top would support the broader AI-infrastructure complex that Nvidia, Broadcom and Alphabet trade alongside. A price at the bottom would become the skeptics' reference point for the sector.

Workflow & Tools

Workflow news this window is about trust boundaries: the tool-connection layer is where the new risk sits, and the protocol underneath it has not versioned since late 2025.

MCP specification: no new version confirmed

The most recent Model Context Protocol specification I could confirm is dated 25 November 2025. A 2026 roadmap from the maintainers said no new version had been cut since, and I found nothing newer in this search. The practical advice is to pin MCP server versions and review tool descriptions as you would a dependency, because the protocol itself is not moving fast enough to rely on for updates.

Gemini 4 Hydrogen in developer surfaces

Hydrogen is available in the Gemini API, AI Studio and Antigravity, so it can serve as a cheap test model for agent loops before committing a production budget. Pair it with the reasoning-level setting to measure how much extra reasoning changes task success on your own tool calls.

Self-hosting inference stacks

Inkling 2 Small ships with SGLang, vLLM and Unsloth support, which means the usual serving stacks work without custom adapters. If you are evaluating it, benchmark both serving engines on your own prompt lengths before choosing one.

Research Radar

One paper worth tracking this week measures how often attacks succeed through the tool-connection layer that MCP creates.

The preprint MCP Tool Poisoning in Agentic AI Frameworks: A Cross-Framework Measurement Study, submitted on 2 November and accepted to the AISec '26 workshop, reports an overall attack success rate of 22.9% when attacker-controlled content in tool descriptions, schemas and responses is passed into model context. Nine core frameworks cluster between 23% and 26%, which the authors read as a property of the model rather than of any one framework. They also found that no framework implements an intentional defence. The study measured real tool execution, which makes the numbers more concrete than a threat model, but it is a single preprint and the defence side is still open.
