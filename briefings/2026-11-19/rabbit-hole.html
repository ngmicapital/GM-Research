Why About 30% of Real Numbers Start With 1 — and How Worn Pages Caught Fraudsters

A pattern noticed in grubby logarithm tables in 1881 became a law that now flags fabricated invoices, ballots and bot accounts.

About 30 percent of the numbers in many real-world datasets begin with the digit 1, while fewer than 5 percent begin with 9, and the pattern was first spotted in 1881 because the early pages of a logarithm table were grubbier than the rest. The reason is scale: data that spans many orders of magnitude has a leading-digit shape fixed by logarithms, which is why the same law now exposes invented numbers in audits and fake accounts on social platforms.

Section 01

The Story

Gather numbers from almost any real collection, such as river surface areas, city populations, molecular weights or the street numbers printed in a magazine, and the leading digits refuse to be evenly spread. Roughly 30 percent begin with 1, while under 5 percent begin with 9. If every digit were equally likely, each would take up about 11.1 percent. Nothing in ordinary life seems to favour a 1, which is what makes the pattern feel wrong the first time you check it.

The first person to see it was Simon Newcomb, an American astronomer who worked with logarithm tables, the bound reference volumes scientists used before calculators to multiply and divide by looking up logs. In 1881 he noticed that the early pages, covering numbers that begin with 1, were far more thumbed than the back of the book. He reasoned that people looked up numbers starting with 1 much more often than the rest, and he worked out the percentages in a two-page note. That note was then largely overlooked.

the pages for numbers beginning with 1 were “so much more worn than the others.”
— Simon Newcomb, “Note on the Frequency of Use of the Different Digits in Natural Numbers,” American Journal of Mathematics, 1881

Fifty-seven years later Frank Benford, a physicist at General Electric’s research laboratory, arrived at the same result independently. Instead of relying on a worn book, he assembled a much larger test, and his 1938 paper “The Law of Anomalous Numbers” is the one that gave the pattern its popular name. Because the paper came later and was more systematic, the law is usually attached to Benford rather than to the astronomer who saw it first.

The dataset

20,229 observations drawn from 20 groups. Benford’s 1938 collection included surface areas of rivers, populations of cities, physical constants, molecular weights, death rates, baseball statistics and numbers clipped from newspapers. The leading-digit shape recurred across those groups, and the common thread was that each spanned several orders of magnitude rather than sitting in one narrow band.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The law states that the probability a number begins with digit d is log₁₀(1 + 1/d). For d = 1 that gives log₁₀ 2, or 30.1 percent. For d = 9 it gives log₁₀(10/9), or 4.6 percent. The logarithm is the key. On a logarithmic scale, the stretch from 1 to 2 covers about 0.301 of a decade, while the stretch from 9 to 10 covers only about 0.046. If a quantity is spread evenly across that log scale, numbers starting with 1 occupy a much larger slice of it.

Why would real data be spread evenly across the log scale? The answer lies in scale invariance. A law that is unchanged when you multiply every value by a constant has to take the logarithmic form, because the leading digits must look the same whether you measure in metres or in kilometres. Mathematicians point out a subtlety here: the only scale-invariant density over all positive numbers cannot be normalised into a probability distribution. The true claim is about the significant digits of the data, not about the whole shape of the distribution.

A derivation of Benford's Law or the First-Digit Phenomenon is given assuming only base-invariance of the underlying law.
— Theodore P. Hill, “Base-Invariance Implies Benford’s Law,” Proceedings of the American Mathematical Society, 1995

Hill’s 1995 work supplied the explanation that made the law stop feeling like a coincidence. Suppose you choose many distributions at random in a way that favours no particular scale, draw a sample from each, and pool everything together. The leading digits of the pooled sample converge to Benford’s distribution, even though no individual distribution needs to obey the law. That is the mechanism that matters for real data, which is a mixture of many processes with no preferred units.

The law also has clear failure conditions, and they are where the practical value lies. Assigned numbers break it: US telephone numbers are built from area codes and prefixes that restrict their values, so only the last four digits behave like random draws. Hard minimums and maximums break it, and so does a dataset that covers only one or two orders of magnitude. An auditor therefore has to check that the data is unrestricted and genuinely spread across scales before reading anything into a deviation.

Section 03

The Connections

Why fabricators get caught. People who invent numbers do not follow the logarithmic shape. They tend to overuse certain digits, avoid others and cluster values near round figures. Auditors exploit this, and Mark Nigrini’s forensic work built a set of tests around it. The 1995 review by Hill cites a 1995 report in which the Brooklyn District Attorney’s office used a Benford goodness-of-fit test to help uncover a fraud ring involving seven companies. The caveat is important: the test flags anomalies for follow-up and does not prove that anyone committed fraud.

Why the obvious explanation fails. Newcomb’s own account was about human habit: people looked up small numbers more often. That story fits a book of tables, but it cannot explain why river areas, physical constants and baseball statistics share the same shape. Benford described the law as reaching deeper than the number system itself. The 1995 derivations made that mystique unnecessary, since the shape follows from scale-neutrality alone. The lesson is that a pattern can be real and still be less mysterious than its early explanations suggested.

The irony of the name. The discovery is credited to the later and more systematic paper, so the law carries Benford’s name while the astronomer who saw the worn pages first is remembered mainly through footnotes. Some historical accounts argue that Benford’s paper restated Newcomb’s formula without citing it. That attribution question is contested, so it is worth reading both papers rather than taking either account on trust. The coda is that the most ordinary evidence in the world, a grubby book, was the first clue to a law that outlived the book.

The live dimension: markets and platforms. Physicists have shown that scale-invariant distributions emerge from multiplicative stochastic processes, and the dynamics of stock prices are a standard example. That makes returns and trade sizes a natural place to look for the pattern, but a trader should treat any deviation as a question rather than a verdict, because price floors, circuit breakers and quoted tick sizes all restrict the range of values. On social platforms, Scientific American reports that follower counts for most users fit the pattern while artificial accounts depart from it, which makes the law a cheap first screen for bot activity.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

📖
Newcomb’s two-page note

The entire original argument fits in a couple of pages. Reading it shows how much of the law was visible in 1881 from a single observation about wear.

📐
Hill’s 1995 derivations

Theodore Hill’s base-invariance paper and his statistical derivation are the proofs that explain why the law appears in mixed, unit-free data. They are short enough to follow with a pencil.

🧾
Forensic accounting with Nigrini

Mark Nigrini’s applied work shows how first-digit, first-two-digit and second-order tests are used on ledgers, and where the tests give false alarms.

☎️
Where the law breaks

Assigned numbers such as telephone prefixes, bounded measurements and narrow ranges are the sub-rabbit-hole. Working out why each one fails is the fastest way to understand the mechanism.
