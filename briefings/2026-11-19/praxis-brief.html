Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

Housel on risk and Mollick on AI converge on one idea: the failure that hurts you is usually the one you did not forecast, so the job shifts from predicting it to building margin and testing for it by hand. Housel's essays ask readers to flip the goal and work out how they would fall back, and to learn from people who endured risk rather than those who seemed to conquer it. Mollick's jagged-frontier framing says AI capability is uneven, which means an average score tells you little about where a given tool will fail. Together they point to one practice: design for surprise, and keep a log of the failures you actually observe.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Essay · Morgan Housel · “Little Rules About Big Things” · Aug 13, 2025

The risk that hurts you is the one you did not see coming, so forecasting the biggest risk is the wrong job

Housel's list of hard-won rules contains a cluster that reads as one argument. The risks that do the most damage are the ones people fail to see, assume only happen to other people, or ignore, and a small surprise often hurts more than a large risk that has been in the news for months. He also reframes the question itself. Asking which risks are biggest is like asking what you expect to be surprised by, because the dangerous material sits in what your imagination cannot reach. The practical upshot is that risk management means counting how many things can go wrong before they actually do, not picking the one that will.

The same essay is really about margin. An ordinary investor can absorb a few mistakes and keep going, while a careless one cannot recover, so the goal is to stay in the game rather than to be the sharpest forecaster. That is a survival argument, not a prediction argument.

Housel's risk rules and Mollick's jagged-frontier warning come at the same problem from opposite ends: when you cannot forecast where failure will land, the work shifts from predicting it to building room to absorb it and testing for it directly.

Essay · Morgan Housel · “Very Bad Advice” · Jul 24, 2025

Flip the goal: plan by asking how you would fall back, not how you would get ahead

Housel opens with an observation that building a house is complex while destroying one is simple, and argues that in most areas of life the same asymmetry holds. His practical move follows from it. When trying to get ahead, it can help to flip the question and focus on how not to fall back. He pairs this with a blunt point about competition: rivals can probably innovate and execute as well as you can, so any new edge you feel proud of should be tempered by the fact that people who want to win just as badly are probably close behind.

This is the same move as the margin argument above, applied to strategy. Inversion does not tell you what success looks like. It tells you where the failure path runs, and that is the path worth mapping first, because it is the one you can actually see from where you stand.

Newsletter · Ethan Mollick · “Fable 5.2 and Jagged AGI” · Nov 9, 2026

AI capability is jagged, so no average score tells you where a tool will fail, and only hands-on tests do

Mollick's latest post carries the subtitle that AI is general, often expert, and sometimes jagged. The jagged-frontier framing is his, according to the conversation Eric Topol published with him on Nov 10, where Topol notes the term has since been adopted widely. The point is that capability is uneven across tasks rather than rising smoothly, so a reassuring benchmark average says little about the specific job in front of you. Mollick describes his own work as a research-based view on what AI means for work and education, and he tests models on real tasks rather than relying on headline claims.

Read next to Housel, this is a risk-management problem. If failure is unevenly distributed and hard to forecast, the reliable method is to enumerate where it actually shows up, which means running the tool against work whose right answer you already know and recording the misses.

Both writers arrive at the same discipline: stop trusting the average, map the failure paths you can observe, and size your exposure to the ones you cannot.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · drawn from Housel's risk rules

Write the surprise list before you write the forecast

Most plans are built around the outcome you expect. This practice reverses the order: before you commit to a forecast, list the ways the plan could break, including the ones your gut says are preposterous, because those are the ones most likely to be discounted and ignored.

  • Name five failure paths. For the next decision you are making, write five specific ways it could go wrong, not just the one you fear most.
  • Flag the dismissed ones. Circle any path you rejected as unlikely or silly. These are the candidates Housel warns about, so give them a second look.
  • Size the cushion, not the bet. For each path, write the margin that would let you survive it, such as cash, time, or a fallback option.
  • Re-run the list on a schedule. Revisit it when conditions change, because the surprise list goes stale faster than the forecast does.
Practice · drawn from Housel's inversion and Mollick's jagged-frontier test

Invert the goal, then test any AI tool on work you already understand

Two moves from this issue fit together. First, flip the question to how you would fall back. Second, because AI capability is uneven, test the tool against tasks where you already know what good output looks like, and keep a record of where it fails.

  • Write the fall-back line. In one sentence, describe how this project would fail badly and what you would do then.
  • Pick a known-answer task. Choose a piece of work from your own field where you can judge the output without help.
  • Log the misses. Record where the tool fails, not just where it impresses you, and note the type of error.
  • Set the handover rule. Use the log to decide which tasks you hand over and which stay with you, then review it after the next model update.

The failure log is the practical form of margin: it turns an unpredictable tool into a map of where you can and cannot rely on it.

04 On the Horizon
Finance

Margin becomes the scarce asset when forecasts keep missing

If the damaging risks are the ones nobody is watching, the advantage shifts to people who hold cash, time, and options that let them survive a surprise. That is the margin argument from Housel's essays, and it points to a reordering of priorities in personal money decisions: resilience first, return second.

AI

Uneven capability will keep producing surprises in both directions

The jagged frontier implies that a tool can be excellent on one task and unreliable on a neighbouring one. Expect more cases where the model is trusted in the wrong place, which makes an ongoing failure log more valuable than any single benchmark, and makes the test-first habit a durable skill rather than a stopgap.

Work

Pre-mortems move from a niche habit to a standard planning step

Teams that ask first how a project could fail, and then write down the fall-back line, will catch the discounted risks that usually arrive unannounced. The inversion move from Housel's essay is cheap to adopt and tends to surface problems before they reach the budget.
