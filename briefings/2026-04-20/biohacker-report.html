Biohacker Report

Health Intelligence Briefing

20 APRIL 2026  ·  SYDNEY, AUSTRALIA
TL;DR — Driving Thesis

Two landmark updates dropped in April 2026 that should change how you train and eat. The ACSM just rewrote resistance training science for the first time in 17 years: volume is king, rep range and load are mostly irrelevant for hypertrophy, and 10 sets per muscle group per week is the evidence-backed target — meaning many PPL lifters are under-voluminising specific groups. Meanwhile, U.S. federal guidelines have officially raised the protein RDA to 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day, vindicating what the evidence-based community has been saying for a decade. The third signal worth locking in: cold plunging immediately after heavy resistance training is increasingly shown to blunt muscle protein synthesis — stop combining them if hypertrophy is the goal.

Section 01

Longevity & Biomarkers

The Science of Aging Is Changing Its Framework Expert Opinion

The dominant view in longevity research — attack one pathway, fix one hallmark — is giving way to something more nuanced and harder to sell supplements around. The 2nd World Congress on Targeting Longevity (Berlin, 8–9 April 2026), organised by the World Mitochondria Society and International Society of Microbiota, framed the state of play clearly: aging is best understood as a loss of coordination between biological systems — metabolism, immunity, mitochondria, and the gut microbiome — rather than a failure of any single mechanism. Restoring dialogue between these systems may be more powerful than targeting any one marker.

🔬 In Plain English Mitochondria are the power generators inside every cell — they turn food and oxygen into usable energy. When mitochondrial signalling breaks down, the whole cell notices: inflammation increases, repair slows, and energy output drops. They're also central messengers that talk to the immune system and gut — which is why a "whole systems" view of aging now makes more scientific sense than fixing one thing at a time.
🔬 In Plain English The gut microbiome is the community of trillions of bacteria living in your intestines. Research now shows that how your gut bugs communicate with your brain (the gut-brain axis) and immune system significantly shapes how fast you age. It matters because disrupting this communication — via antibiotics, poor diet, or chronic stress — accelerates aging markers even in otherwise healthy people.
The practical takeaway from this paradigm shift is not to run more labs or buy more supplements — it's that interventions hitting multiple systems simultaneously (resistance training + sleep + quality nutrition + zone 2 cardio) outperform any single-pathway hack. The research community is finally building the scientific scaffolding around what practitioners already know works.

Creatine Gets a Mood Signal — With Caveats Meta

A new systematic review and meta-analysis published in the British Journal of Nutrition (Eckert et al., 2025, 11 trials, n=1,093) found that creatine supplementation produces a small-to-moderate effect on depressive symptoms — and more notably, a 3.6× higher odds of remission (OR 3.60; 95% CI 1.76–7.56) across three trials. The effect on depression scores was modest: a standardised mean difference of -0.34, equivalent to roughly 2 points on the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale.

🔬 In Plain English Standardised mean difference (SMD) is a stat used to compare results across different studies. An SMD of -0.34 is considered "small" — real, but not dramatic. An odds ratio (OR) of 3.6 for remission sounds impressive, but remission rates were low across all trials, so the absolute numbers behind the ratio matter more than the headline figure.
⚠ Hype Watch
The authors themselves rate this evidence as "very low quality" under GRADE standards. Sample sizes are small, heterogeneity is high, and the "3.6x recovery" figure comes from only 3 trials. Creatine is not an antidepressant. If you're already supplementing for performance, this is a free benefit worth tracking — but it's not a reason to start creatine for mood alone.

VO2max Still the Best Longevity Biomarker — But the Training Route Is Contested Meta

A 2025 narrative review (updated April 2026) examined the Zone 2 training hype against hard data and found the claim that Zone 2 is superior to higher intensities for mitochondrial adaptation and VO2max improvement is not supported. At realistic weekly volumes (1–4 hours), work above the lactate threshold yields larger VO2max and mitochondrial signalling improvements per minute than Zone 2 alone.

🔬 In Plain English VO2max is your body's maximum oxygen-processing capacity — it's the single best predictor of long-term health and longevity in the literature, stronger than BMI, cholesterol, or blood pressure. Higher VO2max = your heart and muscles are more efficient. It drops about 10% per decade after 30 if you don't train it.
🔬 In Plain English Lactate threshold (LT1) is the exercise intensity where your body starts producing lactic acid faster than it can clear it. Zone 2 training sits just below this threshold — comfortable but purposeful. Training above LT1 (intervals, tempo efforts) stresses the aerobic system harder and produces faster VO2max gains, especially when total training hours are limited.
For a time-constrained lifter doing PPL, the implication is clear: if you're doing cardio twice a week, one session of Zone 2 (aerobic base, low stress) plus one session of higher-intensity intervals will outperform two Zone 2 sessions for VO2max improvement. Zone 2's biggest evidence advantage is reduced recovery cost, not superior adaptation.
⚡ Implication The highest-leverage longevity action right now is hitting ≥10 sets/muscle group/week in the gym and adding one higher-intensity cardio session per week — these two behaviours address muscle, VO2max, and mitochondrial health simultaneously.
Section 02

Recovery & Sleep

Daily Exercise — Not Weekly Volume — Is the Sleep Quality Driver Observational

Research from the University of Texas at Austin (2025) found that exercise frequency, not total weekly volume, is the strongest predictor of sleep quality — particularly deep, restorative slow-wave sleep. Daily movement, even light activity, consistently outperformed equivalent-volume but less-frequent training for subjective and objective sleep markers. The mechanism is circadian: daily activity reinforces the body's internal clock in a way that 2–3 intense sessions per week doesn't.

🔬 In Plain English Slow-wave sleep (also called deep sleep) is the deepest stage of non-REM sleep — this is when your body repairs tissue, consolidates memories, and flushes metabolic waste from the brain. It's the most physically restorative phase. Losing it is one of the first and most damaging effects of poor sleep habits or training mismanagement.

Fundamentals: Reading Your Whoop HRV — What's Signal, What's Noise

HRV (heart rate variability) is the gap between consecutive heartbeats — measured in milliseconds. A higher HRV means your nervous system is in a parasympathetic (rest-and-recover) state. A lower HRV means sympathetic dominance — stress, inflammation, or under-recovery. Whoop tracks your HRV during the final 5-minute window of sleep, which is more stable than single-point readings.

🔬 In Plain English HRV (heart rate variability) is not your heart rate — it's the tiny millisecond-level variation between beats. A heart that beats with irregular spacing is actually healthier than a metronome-perfect one, because that variation shows the nervous system is actively adapting to demands. Low HRV = nervous system is fatigued and not adapting. High HRV = recovered and ready to absorb training stress.
For lifters, three rules for HRV interpretation: (1) Trend matters more than the number. A 7-day downward HRV trend despite adequate sleep means something is wrong — training load, nutrition, or stress. (2) Compare yourself to yourself. Population averages are useless. Your personal 30-day baseline is the only benchmark that counts. (3) Context stacks. A single low HRV after a big training day, alcohol, or poor sleep is normal and not actionable. It's when it persists across 3+ normal nights that it becomes a training signal — consider a deload or extra recovery day.

When to push through a low HRV: Short-term lows (1–2 days) after hard sessions, travel, or a poor night — train but modulate intensity. When to back off: 3+ consecutive days below your personal baseline, especially combined with low recovery scores, disrupted sleep, or performance regression. A full deload or active recovery day is the correct response, not extra coffee and a PR attempt.

⚡ Implication Add a daily 20-minute walk on rest days — not for fitness gains, but to keep the circadian signal consistent and protect sleep architecture without adding recovery load.
Section 03

Nutrition & Fuel

⚡ Federal Protein RDA Just Got Rewritten Expert Opinion

The 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans (released January 2026) officially raised the recommended daily protein intake from the longstanding 0.8 g/kg body weight to a new range of 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day. For a 80 kg male this means the new official floor is 96 g/day — and the upper end of the evidence-based range for active individuals pushing hypertrophy sits at 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day (i.e., 128–176 g/day).

This is not a breaking research finding — the evidence for higher protein has been overwhelming for years. What changed is that the regulatory framework has now caught up. The old 0.8 g/kg figure was designed to prevent deficiency in sedentary populations, not to optimise muscle protein synthesis for people who lift. Expert Opinion

🔬 In Plain English Muscle protein synthesis (MPS) is the process your body uses to build and repair muscle fibres after training. It requires a continuous supply of amino acids from dietary protein. When you eat below 1.6 g/kg/day as an active person, MPS is consistently limited — meaning the training stimulus is there but the raw materials to respond to it aren't. This is the most common nutritional bottleneck for people who train seriously.
The practical upshot: if you're at 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day (common for people who don't actively track), you're almost certainly leaving hypertrophy on the table. The research-supported target for a male doing PPL is 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day, with at least 0.4 g/kg per meal to maximally stimulate MPS in that sitting.
📋 Quick Reference — Hitting 160g Protein at 80 kg
  • Breakfast: 5 eggs + 200g Greek yoghurt (full-fat) = ~45g protein
  • Lunch: 200g chicken breast + 100g cooked lentils = ~55g protein
  • Post-training: 40g whey isolate shake = ~35g protein
  • Dinner: 200g salmon fillet + 150g cottage cheese = ~55g protein
  • Total: ~190g — 2.4 g/kg at 80 kg. Achievable without supplements if meat is at each meal.

Creatine Brain Dosing — What the 2025–26 Data Actually Supports Meta

Beyond the standard 3–5 g/day for muscle performance, research presented at SupplySide Global 2025 and subsequently published recommends 10–20 g/day for cognitive and bone health benefits. The cognitive mechanism is increased cerebral phosphocreatine availability — essentially giving the brain more energy buffer during stress, sleep deprivation, or cognitive load. The depression remission data (Eckert et al., 2025) comes in at the 5–10 g range across trials.

🔬 In Plain English Phosphocreatine is the stored form of creatine in muscle and brain cells — it's the quickest energy source available for high-intensity work, replenishing ATP (the cell's energy currency) faster than any other system. In the brain, more phosphocreatine means faster recovery from mental exertion and better resilience under cognitive stress like sleep deprivation or high-stakes decision-making.
The current evidence supports: 3–5 g/day is sufficient for muscle performance. 10+ g/day may add cognitive and mood benefits but lacks large RCT support — it's a plausible, low-risk extension worth considering if budget allows and GI tolerance is fine (higher doses can cause GI upset in some people). Creatine monohydrate remains the gold standard — no reason to pay for fancier forms.
⚡ Implication Audit daily protein against the 1.6 g/kg floor — most people tracking loosely are under it. Nail this before spending money on any other supplement.
Section 04

Protocols

Sauna Protocol: The Evidence-Based Deep Dive RCT Observational

Sauna has the strongest non-exercise longevity evidence base of any single protocol. The data is Finnish-population-heavy but consistent: frequent use (4+ sessions/week) vs once-weekly reduces hypertension risk by 47% and sudden cardiac death risk by 63% [Observational, Laukkanen et al.]. A 2026 study published in Temperature (Tandfonline) confirmed that acute Finnish sauna heat induces stronger cardiovascular adaptation than equivalent passive heating, with 3 × 10-minute exposures at 80–100°C as the standard protocol.

The sleep signal is the most actionable new finding: one experimental protocol found that a sauna session increased slow-wave (deep) sleep by 70% in the first two hours post-sleep onset, and by 45% across the first six hours. Waking time also decreased significantly. Mechanism is well-understood — body temperature must drop to initiate and maintain deep sleep, and the post-sauna temperature drop is both faster and steeper than normal, creating an ideal entry into deep sleep architecture.

🔬 In Plain English Cardiovascular adaptation in the context of sauna means your heart is getting a workout similar to moderate aerobic exercise — cardiac output increases, blood vessels dilate, and the heart pumps more blood. Over time, this trains the heart muscle and blood vessel walls in ways that reduce long-term heart disease risk. Finnish population data backing this is some of the most robust observational evidence in longevity research.
Optimal protocol for a PPL lifter:
— Timing: 30–60 minutes after training (not immediately post-session), or on rest days in the evening (2–3 hours before bed for maximal sleep benefit)
— Exposure: 3 rounds × 10 minutes at 80–100°C, with 5-minute cool-down between rounds (room temp — do not cold plunge between rounds if combining with a lifting day, see below)
— Frequency: 3–4 sessions/week for cardiovascular benefits; even 1–2 sessions/week has measurable effects on sleep quality
— Hydration: 500–750 mL water around each session; replace electrolytes (sodium + potassium) if doing back-to-back sessions
⚠ Cold Plunge Immediately After Lifting — Stop Doing This
Emerging research is consistent: cold water immersion immediately post-resistance training blunts muscle protein synthesis and may reduce hypertrophy gains. The mechanism is vasoconstriction reducing nutrient delivery to muscle during the anabolic window. If you're doing both, separate them: lift → wait 4+ hours → cold plunge. Or cold plunge on non-lifting days. Cold exposure is valuable for recovery from DOMS and soreness — just not in the 2 hours after heavy sets. Mechanistic

BPC-157: Status Check April 2026 Mechanistic

BPC-157 remains one of the most-discussed peptides in training communities — and also one of the most evidence-thin in terms of human data. As of April 2026: only three human studies exist, all from the same small research group, all without placebo controls. No Phase 2 or Phase 3 trials have been completed. No registered clinical trials are actively recruiting on ClinicalTrials.gov.

The FDA banned compounding pharmacies from supplying BPC-157 in 2023. It continues to circulate via gray-market wellness clinics and online channels. Animal model data (primarily Predrag Sikiric's lab, Croatia) shows consistent tendon, ligament, and GI healing effects — the mechanisms are plausible and the rodent results are impressive. The problem is that most drugs that work in rodents fail in humans.

🔬 In Plain English Peptides are short chains of amino acids — smaller than proteins, larger than individual amino acids. They act as signalling molecules in the body. BPC-157 ("body protection compound") is a 15-amino-acid sequence derived from a protein found in stomach fluid. In animal studies, it accelerates tissue repair, reduces inflammation, and protects gut lining — but "accelerates tissue repair in rodents" has a long track record of not translating to humans.
⚠ Hype Watch
One STAT News medical expert's verdict: BPC-157 "should not be used by humans until proper human studies are conducted." This is the correct position. The hype-to-evidence ratio remains the highest of any mainstream peptide. Australia's TGA classifies it as a Schedule 4 substance — it's not legal to possess without a prescription. If you have a specific injury and want to trial it, consult a sports medicine physician, not a wellness clinic. [Practitioner Reported]
⚡ Implication Add one sauna session this week specifically 2–3 hours before bed and track deep sleep on Whoop. The sleep data is the fastest feedback loop for gauging individual response.
Section 05

Movement & Load

⚡ ACSM 2026: First Resistance Training Guidelines Update in 17 Years Meta

The American College of Sports Medicine Position Stand (Currier et al., led by Stuart M. Phillips, PhD, McMaster University) published its first major resistance training update since 2009, synthesising 137 systematic reviews across 30,000+ participants. The headline findings reshape how hypertrophy programming should be approached:

Volume is the primary driver of hypertrophy. The evidence-based target is ≥10 sets per muscle group per week, with a minimum effective dose of 4 sets/week and diminishing returns above 20 sets/week. Previous guidelines had placed heavy emphasis on rep ranges and specific load percentages — this update effectively sidelines both as secondary variables.

🔬 In Plain English 1RM (one-repetition maximum) is the most weight you can lift for a single rep with full technique. It's used as a reference point to set training loads — e.g., "work at 70–80% of 1RM." The ACSM's new finding that load (30–100% of 1RM) doesn't consistently impact hypertrophy when volume is equated means: whether you use heavy or light weights, what matters is doing enough total sets with sufficient effort.
🔬 In Plain English Eccentric training refers to the "lowering" phase of an exercise — when the muscle lengthens under load (e.g., lowering the bar in a bench press, or the descent in a squat). Eccentric-focused or eccentric-overloaded training produces greater muscle damage and therefore greater hypertrophy signals per set than standard lifting. It's the most under-utilised technique in general lifting populations.
Key action items from the ACSM Position Stand for a PPL lifter:
  • Audit your weekly volume per muscle. Chest getting 10 sets? Rear delts? Biceps? Many PPL programs hit quads and chest well but lag on posterior chain, rear delts, and arms.
  • Slow your eccentrics. 2–3 second lowering phase on all compound movements is the simplest way to implement eccentric emphasis without special equipment.
  • Load flexibility. If a session calls for heavy bench and you're beat up — drop to 60–70% and do more reps. Volume is the target variable, not weight on the bar.
  • Training frequency to failure is not necessary. The ACSM confirms "training to failure and complex periodization are not necessary for general adult health and fitness." Proximity to failure matters, but chasing failure every set is unnecessary and increases injury risk.

✓ What to Change

  • Count weekly sets per muscle group — hit ≥10
  • Add a 2–3 sec eccentric to all compound lifts
  • Stop obsessing over rep range — 6s and 15s both work
  • Flex load based on recovery — volume is the constant
  • Train each major muscle group at least twice weekly

✗ What NOT to Change

  • PPL structure itself — it's evidence-backed for frequency
  • Compound lifts — they remain the best volume-efficient exercises
  • Progressive overload — still the foundational principle
  • Full range of motion — ACSM confirms ROM superiority
  • Consistency — "best program is the one you'll actually do"
⚡ Implication Do a one-session audit: count how many sets you're doing per muscle group weekly. Any muscle group under 10 sets is your weak link — add one additional working set per session until you hit the threshold.
