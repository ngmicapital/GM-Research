Alpha — Spoofing & Iceberg Orders

Monday, 16 November 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue TBD 11 min read
TL;DR

Spoofing and icebergs are the two ways the visible order book misleads you, and they point in opposite directions: a spoof is size that never meant to trade, while an iceberg is size that always does, just shown in slices. This issue builds on the 15 November Absorption issue, which covered size holding against aggression; today asks whether that size was real in the first place. Practical takeaway: judge any large resting order by what it does when price touches it. A level that refills under aggression is real, and a level that vanishes on approach never was.

§01
What It Is
Two ways the visible book misleads
A spoof is large resting size placed to be cancelled before it trades, while an iceberg is real size shown in small visible slices.

Spoofing and icebergs are the two ways the visible order book misleads, and they mislead in opposite directions. My position: the book is a claim about intent, not a record of it. A spoof displays size that was never meant to trade, placed to bend your behaviour and then pulled as price closes in. An iceberg hides size that fully intends to trade, showing only a small slice and refilling it as each slice is consumed. Both leave the same footprint on a single snapshot, which is why reading either one in isolation is dangerous.

The distinction is legal as well as mechanical. Spoofing means placing orders with no intention to execute, and it is illegal manipulation on regulated venues. An iceberg is a standard order type that conceals displayed size. Crypto venues are more lightly regulated, so spoof-style behaviour is a constant background presence on centralised books, and any large resting order there deserves provisional status until the tape confirms it. This is the natural follow-on to the 15 November Absorption issue (Absorption builds on size holding against aggression; today asks whether that size was real to begin with).

§02
Mechanics
Refill ratio and pull rate
The book gives you one snapshot, so the signal comes from comparing how displayed size behaves against executed volume at the same price.

The mechanism is a three-step test run on the same price level. First, measure how long a displayed order has sat and how its size changes as price approaches. Second, compare that displayed size with the volume actually executed at the level on the tape. Third, classify the level: absorbing aggression (real and possibly hidden) or evaporating before contact (fake).

Refill ratio = executed volume at price ÷ displayed size at price, over the same window
A ratio well above 1 with the level still standing points to hidden size. A ratio near zero with the displayed size pulled before contact points to an order that was never meant to fill.
Pull rate = size cancelled within 2 ticks of the level ÷ total size posted there
A high pull rate on large, round-number orders is the spoof signature. Round numbers are a heuristic, not proof, so weight the pull rate above the number itself.

The practical problem is that the two look identical on the book for one snapshot. Only the sequence over time separates them. That is why a single screenshot of a wall tells you almost nothing, and why the refill test has to run across several minutes of prints.

Visual
Pulled wall versus refilling slice
A pulled ask wall that never traded sits beside a bid level that refills after each slice is hit, showing how the two patterns diverge.
Left: a displayed wall that disappears before any fill, which is the spoof pattern. Right: a visible slice that keeps refilling at one price as aggressive sells arrive, which is the iceberg pattern.

Read the two panels as a test, not a verdict. On the left, the size never met price, so the level never existed as support or resistance. On the right, the level is doing work: each refill is absorbing aggression. Price behaviour at the level is what separates them, and the table below turns that into observations you can log while watching the tape.

Some guides use a rough rule of thumb: a large order that has sat for only a few minutes and disappears on approach is suspect. Treat that as a starting heuristic for attention, not a standard the venue enforces.

§04
In Practice
Three tests for a disputed level
A level earns trust only when price tests it and it holds, so treat every large resting order as provisional until the tape confirms it.
1

Test the wall before you trust it

Note the size and the time it first appeared. A bid or offer that survives several minutes of trading and then fills is usually real, while one that appears and vanishes near price is a candidate spoof.

2

Count refills, not displayed size

Watch one price for repeat prints of the same chunk. Refilling at a single level under aggressive selling is the iceberg signature, and it matters far more than the number printed on the screen.

3

Let price confirm the book

A level counts only after price tests it and holds or breaks. The book is the hypothesis and the tape is the evidence, so do not react to walls that price has not reached yet.

Worked example (illustrative, not a trade call)
SetupLong on a bid zone near 64,800 after refills confirmed under selling
Entry64,800
Stop64,380, below the refilled zone: 420 risk per BTC
Target66,000: 1,200 reward per BTC

Timeframe fit: read the book on 1–5m for the refill and pull test, confirm the level on 15m–1h structure, and take bias from the 4H.

§07
Common Traps
Where book-reading goes wrong
Calling every big wall a spoof, or every refill a whale, which turns a probability tool into a story.
1
Reading any large wall as real size

Most displayed walls far from price are provisional. Wait for contact, then judge whether the level refills or gets pulled.

2
Calling every iceberg a whale

Icebergs are a standard execution tool used by many participants. Repeated refills tell you someone is working size at a level, not who that someone is.

3
Trading the pull itself

A vanishing wall is a reason to re-check the level, not a standalone entry. Price can still reverse without the wall, and the pull may simply be a routine cancel.

4
Ignoring cross-venue differences

Thinner venues show more fake-looking behaviour. A level that appears on one book but not in aggregated depth is weak evidence and should be treated as such.

!
Overhyped: whale-wall reading as a standalone edge

Much online content presents spoof detection as a repeatable edge. The research literature notes that detecting spoofing in live markets is very hard and that scientific coverage of the topic is thin. Use book-reading as a filter for where to look, not as a signal you can trade on its own.
