The Keyboard Built to Stop Jams, Not to Slow You Down

The QWERTY myth, the 1873 typewriter that made it, and why a tidy villain story outlived the evidence

The most famous keyboard in the world was supposedly designed to slow you down, and the story is almost entirely a myth. The real fault was mechanical, the fix was geometric, and the layout survived because switching away from it would cost more than it could ever save.

TL;DR

The QWERTY keyboard is supposed to be the textbook case of a deliberately slowed-down design: a clever inventor scrambled the letters so that typists would not jam their machines. The mechanical problem was real, since neighbouring typebars tangled when struck in quick succession, but separating common letter pairs was a fix for jams, not a handicap on the typist. The story persisted because it was tidy, and because the economics that really explain its survival, switching costs and lock-in, are far less memorable than a villain.

Section 01

The Story

In the early 1870s a Milwaukee newspaper publisher, Christopher Latham Sholes, and his collaborators were trying to build a machine that could put letters on paper faster than a hand could write them. Their early prototypes did not use the layout you are looking at now. The keys were ordered in an alphabetical scheme, and the machine jammed whenever the operator hit certain keys in quick succession. The keyboard that eventually shipped was rearranged to fix that problem, and the arrangement that resulted looks arbitrary to modern eyes, which is exactly why a more dramatic explanation took hold.

The popular version holds that the rearrangement was a deliberate handicap. Slow the typist down and the bars stop tangling. Versions of this account turn up in textbooks and popular writing alike, and economists have long used the keyboard as their favourite example of a market that locked in an inferior product. The difficulty is that the historical record does not support the part about slowing people down. The stated goal was to stop collisions, and whatever slowing occurred was a side effect of a different design aim.

The story of Dvorak’s superiority is a myth or, perhaps more properly, a hoax.
— Stan Liebowitz and Stephen E. Margolis, “Typing Errors,” Reason, 1996

Commercial life began when the design reached E. Remington and Sons. Production started on 1 March 1873 in Ilion, New York, and the machine went on sale as the Sholes & Glidden Type Writer in 1874. It printed capitals only, and sales were slow to start before picking up. Roughly 5,000 units were sold between 1874 and 1878, when Remington introduced a model with a shift key for upper and lower case. Early sales were modest, but the keyboard was copied and taught, so the arrangement outlived the machine that first carried it.

The paper that broke the story

“The Fable of the Keys,” by economists Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis, appeared in the Journal of Law and Economics in April 1990. Its target was the claim that the Dvorak layout had been demonstrated to be far faster. The authors argued that the first evidence for that claim was extremely thin, and that the historical account of QWERTY had been retold in a way that favoured a neat conclusion.

The keyboard is still on your desk, though not for the reasons the myth gives. In 1936 August Dvorak and William Dealey were granted a US patent for an alternative layout that placed the most-used letters on the home row. Some popular accounts date the patent to 1932, so it is worth checking the specific record before quoting a year. Either way, the rival layout arrived decades after QWERTY had become the default in offices, and it never displaced it.

Section 02

The Mechanism

An early typewriter had no electronics. Each key pushed a steel typebar upward, and the bar carried a raised character up to strike a sheet of paper pressed against a rubber roller. The bars did not strike at separate points. They converged on one spot in front of the platen, so two bars released within a few milliseconds of each other could collide there and stall the whole action. The failure was geometric. It depended on which bars were moving together, not on how fast a person could type overall.

Sholes’s remedy was to put the letters that often come one after another on opposite sides of the machine. English is full of very common pairs, such as th, he, and er, and in the earlier alphabetical arrangement many of them sat next to each other. Moving the pairs apart meant that the bars for consecutive letters were less likely to be moving at the same moment. The fix was real and it worked on the jam problem, which is why the scrambled order can be traced to a specific engineering purpose.

The fault in one chain

Adjacent typebars fire close together, they meet at the striking point, and the machine jams. Separating frequently paired letters reduces that collision. Separation can incidentally slow the hands, because they travel further between common letters. The myth comes from sliding from “reduce jams” to “handicap the typist,” which are two distinct design goals.

This is where the myth gains its half-truth. Keeping common pairs apart can make some sequences of keystrokes take longer, so a deliberate speed penalty is not absurd on paper. But a designer optimising for fewer jams is not the same as a designer optimising for slower typing, and the historical sources do not show the latter as the aim. The original goal was mechanical reliability. Once the speed tradeoff was understood, the layout was already in too many offices to be revised.

Economists then looked at whether the keyboard itself could be ranked against its rivals. That is harder than it sounds. Early comparison trials compared retrained typists with different starting points, and the QWERTY group’s initial scores were measured in a way that disadvantaged them. The authors of “The Fable of the Keys” argued that the claimed Dvorak advantage shrinks once the measurement is made fair. So the honest answer on speed is that the evidence for a large gain is weak, not that Dvorak has been shown to be worse.

Section 03

The Connections

Lock-in, not failure. Economists use the keyboard as the favourite case of a standard that won for the wrong reasons. Once millions of people had learned one layout and offices had bought machines to match, the cost of switching included retraining, lost productivity, and replacing equipment. Liebowitz and Margolis turned the usual argument around. If the alternative was not clearly better by enough to cover those switching costs, then staying with QWERTY is not market failure. It is the market behaving sensibly.

Why the villain survives. A clever inventor deliberately hobbling us is a far better story than a tangle of metal bars and an accounting of switching costs. Neat explanations get retold, and the retelling tends to cite earlier retellings rather than the original trials. The competing explanations are less dramatic: separated letter pairs, a machine that was already selling, and a population that had already learned the keys. None of that is as satisfying as a conspiracy, but it accounts for the record much better.

The ironic coda. The most-cited evidence for the Dvorak layout came from a US Navy study in the 1940s, and the man supervising it was a Navy time-and-motion expert named August Dvorak, the layout’s own inventor. Liebowitz and Margolis judged the study badly flawed. They did not claim the layout is worse. Fast alphanumeric typists do use it, and operating systems now ship with it as a setting, so the real question is how large the gain is compared with the cost of switching. That gap, not the keyboard itself, is the subject of the whole debate.

The live version: standards and AI. Markets and software follow the same shape. Protocols, file formats, and interfaces lock in early because coordination is shared and expensive to undo. A trader choosing a broker API, a data vendor, or an AI tool is buying into a standard, and the cost of leaving becomes part of the price, even if nobody writes it on the invoice. The useful question is not whether a tool is best in absolute terms, but whether it is better enough to justify the switching cost. QWERTY, which can now be changed in a settings menu, still shows how little a menu change can move a habit built over a century and a half.
