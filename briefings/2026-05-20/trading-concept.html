Alpha — Initial Balance & Range Extension

Wednesday · 20 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #26 11-min read
TL;DR

Initial Balance is the price range carved out in the first hour of the session — the day's reference scaffold, set by short-term participants before larger timeframes show their hand. Range Extension is any auction beyond that range after the IB closes; it is the cleanest first-print signal that a longer-timeframe trader has joined the auction, and the width of the IB itself tells you in advance how likely that extension is — narrow base, easy to overwhelm; wide base, the day will probably hold the extremes.

§01
What It Is
The first hour · the base of the lamp · the day's reference frame
The Initial Balance is the high and low printed during the first hour of a defined session — and every move outside it carries information about which timeframe just took control.

Steidlmayer built Market Profile around a simple observation: the first hour of a trading session is structurally different from every hour that follows. It's when the locals — the short-term participants who arrive at the opening bell — find the price range where two-sided trade can actually occur. Two thirty-minute TPO brackets — period A and period B — produce a range, and that range is the Initial Balance: the base on which the rest of the day is constructed.

Dalton's metaphor in Mind Over Markets is exact and worth keeping: the IB is the base of a lamp. "If the initial balance is narrow, the odds are greater that the base will be upset and range extension will occur; days that establish a wider base provide more support and the initial balance is more likely to maintain the extremes for the day." The base is fragile or sturdy, and the rest of the day is a stress test of that base.

Range Extension is what happens when the base is upset. Any auction beyond the IB high or IB low — in period C or later — is by definition a range extension. It is the moment a longer-timeframe participant looked at the locally-established price range and said: I disagree, that's mispriced, I'll move it. The arrival of that participant is the signal. Whether it sticks is the trade.

In crypto the underlying mechanic is identical, but the choice of session matters. With 24/7 markets there is no single canonical opening bell — most desks use one of three references: NY cash open (13:30 UTC / 9:30 ET), London open (07:00 UTC / 08:00 GMT), or a custom day-anchor (00:00 UTC + 60 min). Pick one and be consistent. The level you computed yesterday from a NY-anchored IB is incomparable to a London-anchored IB today.

§02
Mechanics
A + B periods · range extension rules · six day types
Lock the IB at the end of the first hour, watch what happens at IBH and IBL — that single transition decides whether you are trading trend, rotation, or fade.

The computation is mechanical. Mark the highest and lowest traded prices between minute 0 and minute 60 of your chosen session. Freeze them. From minute 61 onward, every print is either inside the IB (rotation, fade candidate) or outside it (range extension, breakout candidate). The IBH and IBL become magnet levels — on a clean trend day, price will leave them and never return; on a rotation day, price will keep returning to mid-IB and chopping around it.

Initial Balance: IBH = max(High[t]) for t ∈ [open, open+60min] IBL = min(Low[t]) for t ∈ [open, open+60min] IB_width = IBH − IBL Range Extension event (upward): ∃ t > open+60min : High[t] > IBH → first such t = RE_up_time Range Extension event (downward): ∃ t > open+60min : Low[t] < IBL → first such t = RE_down_time Dalton width heuristic: IB_width / ATR(20d) < 0.30 → narrow IB → trend-day / DD-day odds rise IB_width / ATR(20d) > 0.65 → wide IB → rotational day odds rise Range-extension projection (informal target): RE_target_up ≈ IBH + IB_width RE_target_down ≈ IBL − IB_width
Note: the ATR ratio threshold is a heuristic widely used by Profile traders — not a Dalton-published formula. The 1× IB-width projection is the standard "first" measured-move target; some desks use 1.5× or 2× IB-width for trend-day campaigns. Always sanity-check against the prior day's value area and any prior-session HVNs/LVNs.

The reason Dalton (and Steidlmayer before him) cared so deeply about IB width is that it telegraphs the day's character before you have to commit capital. A narrow IB means the locals couldn't find two-sided trade — the auction stayed compressed because nobody disagreed enough to push it. That compression is fuel. When a higher-timeframe participant finally does disagree, the move is violent because there is no resting liquidity to absorb it. A wide IB is the opposite signal: it means the auction has already found both extremes, range-extension attempts will run into prior reference, and the day will probably balance.

The literature codifies six day types — each defined by IB shape and range-extension behaviour:

Day typeIB shapeRange Extension
Non-TrendVery narrowAbsent both sides — pre-event, low participation, no auction follow-through
NormalWideMinimal or one-sided small — bell-curve profile, ~50–60% of days
Normal VariationMediumSingle-sided, >2× IB width — institutional timeframe arrives later in the session
TrendNarrowContinuous one-directional — long-timeframe in control from open, no rotation
Double DistributionNarrowOne side, then separated single prints, then a new distribution at the extension
NeutralMediumBoth sides break out — extreme close at one end (event) or centre close (balance)

Notice the pattern: narrow IB shows up three times in this taxonomy — Non-Trend, Trend, and Double Distribution. The IB itself doesn't decide which of those three you got; the behaviour at the IB boundary does. A narrow IB that gets aggressively broken with follow-through volume in period C is the opening tell for a Trend Day. The same narrow IB that gets broken and reclaimed is a Non-Trend Day. The same narrow IB that breaks one way, runs, then reverses through the other side is Double Distribution. You don't predict the type — you read the boundary reaction.

§04
In Practice
Read width first · trade the boundary · confluence with prior session
Width tells you what kind of day to prepare for; the first touch of the IB boundary in period C tells you whether to take the trade.

The IB is not a signal you trade in isolation — it's a frame you trade against. Three things matter, in order: (1) the width of the IB relative to the asset's normal range, (2) the location of the IB relative to prior-session reference (yesterday's value area, prior-day high/low, weekly POC), and (3) what happens at the IBH or IBL on the first re-test from outside.

1

Read the width before period C — it's a forecast, not just a measurement

Compute IB-width / 20-day ATR before period C opens. Below 0.30 — narrow — you are looking for breakout follow-through. Above 0.65 — wide — you are looking for failed breakouts and rotations back through mid-IB. This single ratio biases every decision for the next four hours.

2

Demand follow-through, not just the break

The TradePro Academy heuristic: if period C breaks IBH/IBL and closes outside, the directional trend continues with ~70–75% historical probability. If period C breaks but fails to close outside, you trade the reversal toward the opposite IB extreme. The close, not the wick, is what matters.

3

Stack confluence with prior-session reference

An IB break that lines up with yesterday's value-area edge, prior-day high/low, or a weekly POC is a far higher-quality signal than a break into open air. A break into a void (an LVN above) is a momentum trade; a break into a wall (an HVN above) is a magnet trade — different setups, opposite stop logic.

4

Use the 1× IB-width projection as your first take-profit anchor

For a confirmed range extension, the standard first measured-move target is IBH + IB_width (up) or IBL − IB_width (down). It's where short-covering or stop runs tend to exhaust and the market gives the day's first retracement. Don't anchor your whole trade to it — but it's a natural place to take partials.

§07
Common Traps
Where IB gets misapplied, misread, or oversold
The single biggest mistake is treating every IB break as a tradable signal — most aren't, and the ratio of false breaks rises with IB width.
1
Treating every IB break as a breakout trade

Wide IBs produce far more failed breaks than clean extensions — the wider the base, the better the lamp stands. Trading every IBH poke long is how desks burn their morning P&L. Demand period-close confirmation outside, not just a wick through.

2
Using mismatched session anchors across days

If yesterday's IB was NY-anchored and today's is "00:00 UTC anchored", the levels you carry forward are noise — different participant pool, different liquidity profile. Pick one anchor for the entire week minimum.

3
Ignoring prior-session context

An IB break into a prior-session HVN (high-volume node) is a different trade from a break into an LVN (low-volume void). The first stalls and reverses; the second extends violently. The IB level alone doesn't tell you which — the surrounding profile does.

4
Forcing the day-type label in real time

You don't predict a Trend Day vs Double Distribution vs Normal — you read the reactions sequentially. Many traders pre-commit to a label after period C and then refuse to re-classify when period E contradicts. The taxonomy is a post-hoc filing system, not a pre-trade forecast.

5
The "IB indicator" overhype

Plenty of TradingView "Initial Balance" indicators have gone viral on X with bold promises about win rate. The level is correct; the marketing is not. IB is a frame, not a system — it tells you what kind of day you are likely in, but it does not generate entries on its own. Anyone selling it as a standalone strategy is selling the lamp without the bulb.
