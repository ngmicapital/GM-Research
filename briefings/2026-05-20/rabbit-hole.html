We Have Put 230 Million People to Sleep This Year Without Knowing Why

General anesthesia is one of medicine's most important inventions and one of its deepest unsolved mysteries. Since 1846 we have used it in billions of operations. We still have no agreed scientific explanation for how it erases consciousness at the molecular level — and every theory that tries to explain it runs directly into the hardest problem in all of science.

General anesthesia has been administered more than 230 million times this year alone, yet scientists still have no agreed explanation for why it works at the molecular level — the 125-year-old rule that predicted anesthetic potency was shattered by exceptions no lipid or protein theory can fully explain. The deepest competing theory ties the mystery directly to the unsolved problem of consciousness itself, and plants — which have no brain and no neurons — respond to anesthetics just as we do.

TL;DR

On October 16, 1846, a dentist named William Morton walked into the operating theatre at Massachusetts General Hospital and demonstrated, before a skeptical audience of surgeons, that a patient could be made unconscious with ether vapor and then cut open without screaming. The surgeon John Collins Warren removed a tumor from the patient's jaw, and when the man woke up, he reported feeling nothing. Warren turned to his audience and said: "Gentlemen, this is no humbug." Within a year, ether anesthesia had spread to hospitals across Europe. Within a decade, it had transformed surgery from a race against a patient's endurance into something approaching a craft. What nobody told Warren — because nobody knew — was that the mechanism by which ether erased consciousness was a complete mystery and would remain so for the next 180 years.

Section 01

The Story

Before October 1846, surgery was a violent act committed on a fully conscious person. Speed was survival: the best surgeons could amputate a leg in under a minute, not because precision was impossible but because every additional second increased the chance of the patient dying from shock. Patients were restrained with leather straps. Biting down on a wooden rod was not a metaphor. Surgeons prided themselves on working through screaming. The psychological toll on both patient and surgeon was enormous, and some physicians refused to operate at all on anything but the most immediately life-threatening conditions, because the suffering required seemed disproportionate to the benefit.

Morton's demonstration changed this within months. The transition was so rapid that historians sometimes describe 1846 as the single most important year in the history of medicine. Ether, and then chloroform (introduced in 1847), allowed surgeons to take their time, explore cavities, tie vessels, sew tissue — to do things that would have been impossible on a conscious patient. Modern surgery, including all abdominal, thoracic, neurological, and cardiac procedures, is essentially unthinkable without anesthesia. The 230 million or more procedures performed under general anesthesia each year represent one of the most widely applied medical interventions in history.

We have administered anesthesia billions of times over 180 years. We have refined dosing, developed safer agents, built monitoring systems. What we have never done is explain, at the molecular level, why it works.

And yet the mechanism — the precise molecular explanation for why certain inhaled or injected compounds can reliably and reversibly abolish consciousness — remains, to this day, scientifically contested. In 1899, two pharmacologists named Hans Meyer and Ernest Overton independently noticed something remarkable: the anesthetic potency of any compound was almost perfectly correlated with how well it dissolved in fat. This became the Meyer-Overton rule, and for decades it seemed like the beginning of an explanation. It was not. It turned out to be a clue that pointed in every direction at once, and the attempts to follow it led researchers into the deepest unsolved problem in all of science.

The mystery is not academic. Approximately 1 to 2 patients in every 1,000 experience "awareness under anesthesia" — they remain conscious during surgery, often unable to move because of the paralytic drugs administered alongside the anesthetic, and some feel pain. Of those who experience awareness with recall, studies have found that more than half meet clinical criteria for post-traumatic stress disorder. And the reason these failures happen — the reason anesthesiologists cannot perfectly predict who will go under and who will not — is precisely because the mechanism is not understood well enough to measure its depth with certainty.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The Meyer-Overton rule states that the more fat-soluble a molecule, the more potent an anesthetic it will be. When Meyer and Overton plotted the anesthetic potency of dozens of structurally unrelated compounds — from noble gases like xenon to alcohols to ethers to hydrocarbons — against their oil-water partition coefficients, the correlation was extraordinary: spanning nearly six orders of magnitude, the relationship held almost perfectly. This suggested that all anesthetics were doing the same thing, regardless of their chemical structure: dissolving into the fatty lipid membranes of neurons and somehow disrupting their function.

The lipid theory dominated for most of the twentieth century. But it had a fatal problem, identified in the 1980s: the cutoff effect. As you extend the carbon chain of a fatty alcohol or alkane, potency climbs in lockstep with the Meyer-Overton prediction — until it abruptly stops, at a chain length of around 10 to 12 carbons, and longer molecules simply cease to anesthetize at all. A compound with 14 carbons may be vastly more fat-soluble than one with 8, but it has zero anesthetic effect. If potency were simply a function of how much of the drug dissolves into membranes, the cutoff should not exist. Something else was setting a size limit. Something with a specific shape, a specific binding site — something more like a protein receptor than a lipid pool.

The Enantiomer Problem

An even more direct challenge to the lipid theory comes from enantiomers — mirror-image molecules that are chemically identical except for spatial orientation, the way a left hand and a right hand are identical except for handedness. Many anesthetics exist as pairs of enantiomers with identical fat solubility but measurably different anesthetic potency. The S-enantiomer of ketamine is roughly three to four times more potent as an anesthetic than its R-mirror image. If lipid dissolution were the mechanism, both should be equally potent. The fact that they are not means a protein is involved — because proteins have shape-specific binding sites that discriminate between mirror images. Lipids do not.

The current mainstream view is a protein theory: anesthetics bind to specific sites within ion channel proteins in the brain — particularly the GABA-A receptor (which opens in response to inhibitory signals) and the NMDA receptor (which responds to excitatory ones). By binding to GABA-A, anesthetics make it easier for inhibitory signals to fire; by blocking NMDA, they dampen excitatory activity. The net result is a suppression of neuronal communication that, at sufficient concentrations, extinguishes consciousness. This is consistent with the cutoff effect (the binding pocket has a physical size limit) and with the enantiomer data (the pocket has a specific shape). It is the explanation currently taught in medical schools, and it has generated real clinical advances in designing more targeted agents.

But the protein theory has its own problems. Different classes of anesthetic — volatile agents like isoflurane, intravenous agents like propofol, dissociative agents like ketamine, noble gases like xenon — appear to act on overlapping but distinct sets of receptors. There is no single mechanism that unifies them. And the deeper question — why manipulating these ion channels results specifically in the abolition of consciousness, rather than merely reducing neural activity — remains unanswered. Which brings the mystery to its most unusual frontier.

In the 1990s, physicist Sir Roger Penrose and anesthesiologist Stuart Hameroff proposed that consciousness arises from quantum mechanical processes in protein structures called microtubules, which form the internal scaffolding of neurons. Their Orchestrated Objective Reduction (Orch-OR) theory holds that conscious moments are produced when quantum superpositions within microtubules collapse in a biologically orchestrated way. Hameroff noted that many anesthetics bind to hydrophobic pockets within tubulin — the protein that makes up microtubules — and proposed that they work by disrupting these quantum processes. The theory remains highly controversial; critics argue that the brain is too warm and too wet for quantum coherence to survive long enough to matter. But it has resisted definitive disproof, and it offers the only account of anesthesia that directly addresses the question of why consciousness specifically disappears.

Section 03

The Connections

Plants go under too — and they have no neurons. In 1875, Charles Darwin noted that the tentacles of sundew plants stopped responding to stimulation when exposed to chloroform. The observation was filed away as a curiosity. Over a century later, researchers returned to it and found that it generalizes: Mimosa pudica (the "sensitive plant," which folds its leaves when touched) stops folding under ether or halothane. Venus flytraps — which snap shut in response to action potentials generated by trigger hairs — lose the ability to fire those action potentials when exposed to diethyl ether, and recover within minutes when the anesthetic is removed. Pea tendrils stop twisting. Sundew traps stop moving. The anesthetics that knock out an animal nervous system also halt electrical signaling in plants that have never evolved a single neuron. Whatever these compounds are doing, they are doing it to something more fundamental than the brain structures we have identified as the seat of consciousness.

The hard problem of consciousness — and why it is not just philosophy. In 1995, philosopher David Chalmers formalized what he called the "hard problem of consciousness": explaining not just which brain processes are correlated with conscious experience, but why information processing in a physical system feels like anything at all. The "easy problems" — explaining how the brain integrates sensory data, generates attention, produces reports of internal states — are solvable in principle by conventional neuroscience. The hard problem is not. And the reason the anesthesia mystery is so durable is precisely that it is entangled with the hard problem: to fully explain why a drug eliminates consciousness, you first need a theory of what consciousness is and where in the physical system it resides. We do not have that theory. The anesthesia data is, in an important sense, the clearest empirical constraint we have on whatever the answer turns out to be.

Measuring the unmeasurable — the depth of unconsciousness. Because the mechanism is unknown, there is no reliable way to directly measure the depth of anesthesia. The main clinical tool is the Bispectral Index (BIS) monitor, which analyses electroencephalogram (EEG) signals from electrodes placed on the forehead and produces a single number between 0 (no brain activity) and 100 (fully awake). But BIS was calibrated empirically — by correlating its output with clinical signs of consciousness in thousands of patients — rather than derived from any mechanistic theory of how anesthesia works. Randomised controlled trials have produced mixed results on whether BIS monitoring actually reduces awareness rates. For some agents, particularly ketamine, BIS is known to give misleading readings. Anesthesiologists still rely primarily on physiological signs — blood pressure, heart rate, tear production, movement — and careful titration of drug doses. These are proxies for consciousness, not measures of it.

The awareness patients — consciousness without control. Of the roughly 0.1 to 0.2 percent of patients who experience awareness under general anesthesia, the most disturbing cases involve paralytic drugs administered alongside the anesthetic. Modern anesthesia typically includes a neuromuscular blocking agent to prevent movement during surgery; if the anesthetic component is insufficient but the paralytic is not, the patient is conscious but cannot move, speak, or signal distress. They can hear, feel, and remember. The psychological aftermath is severe: follow-up studies find PTSD rates exceeding 50 percent among this group. The awareness problem cannot be solved by better monitoring alone — it requires understanding the mechanism well enough to guarantee unconsciousness rather than merely inducing its usual physiological correlates.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

🏛
The Ether Dome, Preserved
The operating theatre at Massachusetts General Hospital where Morton demonstrated ether anesthesia on October 16, 1846 — now known as the Ether Dome — is still intact and open for viewing. The original surgical chair, the ether inhaler Morton used, and the amphitheatre seating for medical students are preserved. The date, October 16, is observed as "World Anesthesia Day" each year. Morton went on to spend the rest of his life fighting a bitter patent dispute over the discovery and died penniless in 1868.
🧬
Chirality: The Mirror-Image Test
The enantiomer phenomenon applies beyond anesthesia — it is one of the primary tools used to probe drug-receptor interactions in all of pharmacology. S-ketamine (marketed as esketamine/Spravato) has been approved for treatment-resistant depression at doses far below its anesthetic threshold, while R-ketamine shows a different psychoactive profile. The FDA approved esketamine in 2019, making it the first genuinely novel antidepressant mechanism in decades. The chiral asymmetry that reveals something about the anesthesia mechanism also opened a new avenue in psychiatric treatment.
🧠
Hameroff and the Quantum Brain
Stuart Hameroff's lab at the University of Arizona maintains an active research programme on Orch-OR. His 2014 paper with Penrose in Physics of Life Reviews, "Consciousness in the Universe: A Review of the Orch OR Theory," updated the theory to address quantum biology objections. Recent experimental work on quantum coherence in biological systems — including photosynthesis and bird navigation via quantum entanglement in cryptochrome proteins — has made the idea of quantum effects in warm biological systems less dismissible than it seemed in the 1990s, though Orch-OR remains highly speculative.
🌱
Plants Under Anesthesia
The 2018 Annals of Botany paper "Anaesthetics Stop Diverse Plant Organ Movements, Affect Endocytic Vesicle Recycling and ROS Homeostasis, and Block Action Potentials in Venus Flytraps" by Yokawa et al. is the most comprehensive modern review of plant anesthesia responses. The authors suggest the mechanism involves disruption of lipid raft dynamics and endosomal vesicle recycling in plant cells — analogous to processes in neurons — which may explain why the same compounds affect such evolutionarily distant organisms through what may be a conserved cellular mechanism predating the nervous system itself.
💬
The Hard Problem of Consciousness
David Chalmers' 1995 paper "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" in the Journal of Consciousness Studies and his 1996 book The Conscious Mind are the standard entry points. For a more recent and accessible treatment, Anil Seth's 2021 book Being You argues that consciousness is a controlled hallucination constructed by the brain — a framework that has direct implications for how anesthesia research should be designed. The 2023 Templeton Foundation-funded Adversarial Collaboration on consciousness tested predictions of competing theories and found mixed results, underlining how far from resolution the field remains.
