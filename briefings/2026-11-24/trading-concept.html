Alpha — Sweep Orders

Tuesday, 24 November 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue TBC 11 min read
TL;DR

A sweep order is a single marketable order that eats several levels of the book in one burst, and it matters because it shows urgency: someone paid slippage to be filled now. It tells you who is in a hurry, not which way price will go, so read the void it leaves and the next three prints before you assign a side. BTC is testing that read right now, with the $61,365 four-month low already lost and price near $60,400.

§01
What It Is
One order that stops waiting
A sweep order is one marketable order that consumes several book levels in a single burst, signalling urgency rather than direction.

A sweep order is the moment a marketable order stops waiting. Rather than resting at one price, it crosses the spread and keeps consuming whatever sits on the other side of the book until its quantity is gone. The fact that matters is not raw size; it is the aggression. The participant accepted slippage to be filled now, and that choice is the information.

On the tape it reads as a burst of same-direction prints stepping to successively higher or lower prices, landing inside a very short window. A single print tells you nothing. Traders reconstruct the sweep by merging that burst into one event with a total size, a price range and a direction. Keep two things apart: this is an execution style, not the Smart Money Concepts “liquidity sweep”, where price pokes past a swing high or low to trigger stops. Same word, different event, and mixing them up is the first mistake most people make.

This issue builds on Liquidity Voids (21 Nov 2026), which covered what a thin book does when a large order walks through it, and on Orderbook Depth & Spread (18 Nov 2026). The sweep is the trigger; the void is what it leaves behind.

§02
Mechanics
How the walk fills and what it costs
It walks the resting book level by level, and the average fill and the void it leaves show how hurried it was.

A limit order book stacks resting size at each price. Only so much sits at the best bid, so a marketable sell larger than that size must keep matching against the next bid, then the next, until it is complete. Every level it touches prints a trade at that level's price. The ordered sequence of those prints is the sweep, and the book itself changes with every fill.

The mechanic runs in four steps. First, the order arrives marketable and crosses the spread. Second, the matching engine fills at the best price, then steps to the next one. Third, the book updates after each fill, so the depth you saw a moment earlier is gone. Fourth, if the order finishes on a new level, the levels it skipped form the void that the next move has to cross.

Average fill = Σ (qtyi × pricei) ÷ Σ qtyi
Sum over every level the sweep touched. For a sell sweep, slippage = best bid − average fill.

Worked: a 16-unit market sell hits 7 units at 61,200, 6 at 61,150 and 3 at 61,100. The average fill is 61,162.5, so the sweep paid 37.5 of slippage against the best bid. The cost is part of the signal: a seller who pays 37.5 to exit now is in a hurry, and a hurried seller is what you are looking for.

§04
In Practice
THREE READS ON THE PRINT
A sweep is evidence of urgency, so trade it only with what price does on the next few prints.
1

Judge the sweep by the next three prints

A sweep into a level that fails to move price is absorption: passive size is taking the aggression. A sweep that gets through and keeps stepping is initiative, and it usually leaves a void behind.

2

Map the void before you trade the retest

The band the book skipped is where the next move meets the least resistance. Price returning into that band with same-direction sweeps is continuation; price returning and stalling there is the failed-void read.

3

Size the sweep against the instrument, not your feeling

A 16-unit sweep means little on a thin alt book and a great deal on a deep BTC book. Compare it to typical print size on the same feed, or the threshold you set will be wrong for the next instrument.

§07
Common Traps
FOUR WAYS TO MISREAD IT
Treating every sweep as a directional signal, when many are hedges, exits or rebalancing.
1
Calling every sweep directional

Sweeps carry urgency, and urgency sometimes comes from informed traders, but plenty are hedges, exits or rebalancing. Require follow-through in price and a void or absorption context before you assign a side.

2
Mixing up two different sweeps

The execution sweep is a marketable order walking the book. The Smart Money liquidity sweep is price running stops past a swing. Name which one you mean in your journal, or your backtests will blend two unrelated events.

3
Trusting a chart-based sweep count

Chart tools often approximate sweeps from volume delta or volume spikes. Real detection needs sub-second timestamps and a reliable aggressor side, which consolidated feeds blur. Know whether your feed is tick-level before you count anything.

4
Reading a swept level as gone

Books refill. A level emptied by a sweep can be restocked within the same second, and depth updates can lag the prints. Compare depth several seconds before and after the event, not a single snapshot.

!
Overhyped: “sweep detection” indicators sold as signals

Many packaged sweep scanners are a volume filter with a new name, and the research on urgency is probabilistic at best. Unless you have tested the sweep count out of sample on your own feed against plain volume spikes, treat it as a log, not an edge.
