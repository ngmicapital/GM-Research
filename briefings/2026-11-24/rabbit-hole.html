The Moon Is Leaving, and Every Day Runs a Little Longer

Lunar laser ranging, tidal friction, and why a 1969 mirror still tells us how fast the Earth–Moon pair is drifting apart

The Moon is moving away from Earth at about 3.8 centimetres a year, and we know that figure to the millimetre because Apollo astronauts left mirrors on its surface. The same tidal drag that pushes the Moon outward is taking spin out of the Earth, so the day gets longer over the centuries.

The mechanism is textbook physics, but the way it was measured, the way the naive extrapolation fails, and the way the instruments are now degrading make it a much stranger story than the number alone.

Section 01

The Story

Ask most people how far away the Moon is and they will say about 384,000 kilometres, as if that were a settled number. It is an average, and the average is creeping upward. Every year the Moon sits a few centimetres farther from us than it did the year before, and the rate has been pinned down with unusual precision for more than half a century.

The measurement is almost absurdly simple in outline. On 20 July 1969, Apollo 11 astronauts set a panel of 100 fused-silica corner cubes on the lunar surface, a square about 46 centimetres across. Less than two weeks later, a team at Lick Observatory fired a laser at it and detected a return on 1 August 1969. Each pulse goes out, bounces off the panel, and comes back, and halving the round-trip time multiplied by the speed of light gives the distance. Repeated over decades, those pulses reveal the Moon’s slow exit from Earth’s neighbourhood.

The surprise is in the direction of travel. Gravity usually pulls bodies together, so a moon in a tidal relationship with its planet might be expected to sink inward over time. What the ranging record shows is the opposite: the Moon is gaining distance, and the Earth is the one losing something to pay for it. The two changes are tied together by bookkeeping, not by any mysterious push.

The hardware has also been a source of trouble. When the APOLLO station at Apache Point began its campaign in 2006, the returning signal was roughly a factor of ten weaker than careful link budgets predicted. The leading explanation is dust settling on the reflector faces over four decades on a surface with no weather to clean them, which means the most famous clock in lunar science is slowly going dim.

We also have strong evidence that the Moon has a liquid core, and laser ranging has allowed us to determine with great accuracy the rate at which the Moon is gradually receding from the Earth.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The engine is the tidal bulge. The Moon’s gravity pulls the oceans, and the solid Earth too, into a pair of bulges, one facing the Moon and one on the far side. If the Earth did not rotate, those bulges would sit in a fixed line with the Moon. But the Earth spins in about a day while the Moon takes roughly 27.3 days to orbit, so the planet turns the bulge forward of the Moon. The bulge is then slightly ahead of the line joining the two bodies, and that offset pulls the Moon forward along its orbit, a little like a hand pushing a swing at the right moment.

The forward pull is only half the story, because the bulge is dragged across the ocean floors and continental shelves as the planet turns, and that friction turns rotational energy into heat. The Earth loses spin, the day lengthens by a few milliseconds per century, and the angular momentum it sheds does not vanish. Conservation of angular momentum says the total must be preserved, so the Moon’s orbit absorbs what the Earth gives up. Tidal dissipation is therefore the bridge between the slowing day and the receding Moon, and the two numbers are measurements of the same transfer seen from different ends.

Here is the counterintuitive piece. For a near-circular orbit, adding energy makes it larger, but it also makes the orbital speed smaller. The Moon gains energy and angular momentum, climbs to a wider path, and then travels that path more slowly than before. A pushed satellite behaves the same way: it rises in altitude while slowing down. This is why the Moon’s recession is a slow and gentle process rather than a runaway, and why the day-length change is tiny compared with the tidal energy involved.

The ranging record

Williams and colleagues analysed 20,138 ranges collected between March 1970 and September 2015 and estimated the recession at 38.30 ± 0.09 millimetres per year. Using the orbital parameters that come from that fit, the corresponding lengthening of the day works out to about 2.4 milliseconds per century.

The obvious extrapolation is also the most dangerous. Run today’s rate backwards and the Moon would have been in contact with the Earth around 1.5 billion years ago, which is far too young for a body that scientists date to about 4 billion years. The fix is not to abandon the mechanism but to accept that the rate has not been constant. Geology supplies the missing record: layered sedimentary rocks called tidal rhythmites, and certain fossil corals, preserve daily and seasonal growth bands, and models built from those layers put the Earth–Moon distance 1.4 billion years ago at roughly 340,900 kilometres, with a day of about 18.7 hours.

Section 03

The Connections

Clocks in rock. The Earth has been keeping an archive of its own spin for a very long time. Ancient coral skeletons and tidal rhythmites are essentially paleo-calendars, and when a geologist counts the layers in a stone, the count gives a day length from hundreds of millions of years ago. That makes the Moon’s recession a question of palaeontology as much as astronomy. Competing explanations for changing day length exist too. Winds, shifts in the molten core, and the exchange of angular momentum between the solid Earth and the atmosphere all nudge the day by small amounts from year to year, so the tidal signal only emerges as a long trend that has to be separated from that noise.

Why the straight line breaks. The backwards extrapolation to a Moon touching the Earth 1.5 billion years ago is a classic error: take a rate measured over fifty years and treat it as a constant law. Wikipedia’s summary of the lunar-distance literature notes that the rate may have changed recently, which is exactly the kind of caveat that gets stripped out when a clean number travels through headlines. The same trap appears in any model fitted to a short window. A tidy trend line can be accurate about the recent past and badly wrong about the deep past, and the gap between those two is where the real science lives.

The instrument is dimming. There is an irony here that a markets reader will recognise. The most precise clock in the Earth–Moon system is a passive piece of hardware installed in 1969 and never serviced, and it has been losing signal to dust while the thing it measures keeps moving. The APOLLO station now averages about 260 measurements a year with a median statistical uncertainty under 3 millimetres, so the data are better than ever even as the target gets dimmer. A long time series is only as trustworthy as the calibration of the instrument that produced it, and most benchmarks and indices have the same hidden dependency on their plumbing.

The live dimension: time itself. The slowing day is not an academic footnote for anyone running timestamped systems. Civil time is anchored to the rotation of the planet, and because the day is drifting longer, timekeeping standards have had to add occasional leap seconds to keep clocks aligned with the sky. Those leap seconds have a history of causing software and network trouble when systems handle the extra second badly. For traders and infrastructure engineers, the lesson is that a tiny, predictable drift in a foundational reference can still produce outages if nobody models it. The same goes for AI systems trained on long records and asked to extrapolate beyond them, which is the Moon’s 1.5-billion-year mistake in a new costume.
