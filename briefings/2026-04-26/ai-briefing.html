TL;DR — Driving Thesis

Capital is voting for AI coding agents and Nvidia alternatives — and the regulatory backdrop is getting messier.

Cognition (maker of Devin) is in talks at $25B, Cerebras filed for a mid-May IPO at ~$35B, and Tencent + Alibaba are circling DeepSeek's first outside round — three deals that, taken together, say the next leg of AI investment is being routed away from generic foundation-model bets and into specific bottlenecks: agentic software engineering and inference silicon. On the policy side, the White House just ejected an Anthropic alum from the Commerce AI standards role within days of his hiring, while Trump publicly floated a possible Anthropic–DoD deal — same week, opposite signals. For dc, the practical takeaway is narrow: if you're paying for Cursor- or Devin-class assistants, prices will keep moving (up for capability, down for compute as Cerebras and Akash chase Nvidia margins), and the AI-token sector is finally getting institutional rails (Grayscale GTAO S-1 amendment moves Bittensor closer to a real ETF). Everything else this weekend is consolidation — no frontier model release on the public side since GPT-5.5 and Opus 4.7.

Model Landscape

§02 Claude Ecosystem

Claude Ecosystem

Anthropic is being pulled in two directions in Washington this week — White House ejected one of its alums from a Commerce AI role, while Trump publicly floated a possible Anthropic–DoD deal.

White House forces out Collin Burns from Center for AI Standards role

[Regulatory] Politics Anthropic alumni

The Department of Commerce hired Anthropic researcher Collin Burns last week to lead the new Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) — the federal body that will run capability evaluations and set baseline assurance standards. ⚡ Implication for dc: No direct product impact, but the direction of travel matters — Anthropic's commercial future has a meaningful U.S. government component (DoD, intelligence community, federal civilian) and the political risk is now bidirectional. §07 Research Radar

Research Radar

The most useful paper this week is the Andreoletti transformer-forecast-collapse proof — for anyone building return-prediction models, this is a hard-stop signal that scaling transformer expressivity is counterproductive.

Andreoletti (2026) — Forecast collapse of transformer models under squared loss

[Research] arXiv Quant ML

Andreoletti (2026) presents a formal proof that for financial time series with low signal-to-noise ratios, increasing transformer expressivity raises prediction error rather than lowering it — and that simpler linear models (and naive baselines) consistently outperform complex transformers like PatchTST in return-predictive tasks under standard squared-loss training. The proof shows the failure mode is not a hyperparameter problem; it's structural to the loss surface and the SNR regime.

⚡ Why it matters in 6 months: Quant teams across the street have been quietly burning compute on transformer-based return forecasters since 2023. This paper is the formal version of what desks have been observing empirically — they don't beat OLS+GLM on out-of-sample returns. Expect a wave of "we're going back to simpler models" signal in academic quant publications and possibly some quiet repositioning at multi-strats. For dc directly: not actionable, but a useful prior — when an AI-trading-tool vendor pitches "transformer-based return prediction," ask for SNR-corrected backtests.

Tufts neuro-symbolic VLA — outperforms neural-only VLAs on long-horizon manipulation

[Research] Robotics Hybrid AI

Tufts published a neuro-symbolic Visual-Language-Action system that combines statistical pattern recognition with explicit symbolic reasoning — significantly outperforms pure-neural VLA baselines on structured long-horizon manipulation tasks, with materially lower energy consumption. Same direction of travel as the AI Scientist-v2 work covered in Issue #29: hybrid architectures are now more often the right choice than scaled-up monoliths for any task with explicit structure.

⚡ Why it matters in 6 months: Robotics labs and embodied-AI players (Figure, 1X, Boston Dynamics) will increasingly publish hybrid systems — pure end-to-end VLA models will be reframed as a research curiosity rather than a deployment path. Watch for embodied-AI funding rounds to follow this architectural shift.
§08 Technicals

Technicals

Inference cost is breaking — Quantix 3-bit, Attention Matching 50× KV reduction, and Cerebras IPO-scale wafer compute are all pulling the same direction at once.

Quantix — 3-bit quantised inference, 4.82× kernel speedup over FP16 cuBLAS

[Research] Inference

Quantix (ACM TOIT publication this month) ships a 3-bit non-uniform quantisation scheme for high-throughput batched inference: 4.82× average kernel-level speedup over FP16 cuBLAS, end-to-end speedups of up to 11.46× over prior SOTA on NVIDIA L40 GPUs. Crucially, this is weight quantisation (separate from TurboQuant's KV-cache attack) and the two compose multiplicatively on the same inference pass.

Attention Matching — 50× KV-cache compression with negligible quality loss

[Research] Memory

The Attention Matching technique compresses the KV cache up to 50× while keeping benchmark accuracy close to original. This is the most aggressive number reported in the literature so far for KV cache compression — TurboQuant did 6× without retraining, Attention Matching pushes to 50× by absorbing a small calibration step. If it integrates into vLLM (the team has signalled they want to), 1M-token context becomes practical on a single 80GB H100 instead of needing a multi-GPU shard.

Cerebras IPO scale — wafer-scale economics, 750MW in OpenAI deal

[Update] Hardware

The Cerebras S-1 confirms what the OpenAI deal suggested: wafer-scale integration (the WSE-3 chip is essentially a single silicon wafer with no inter-die interconnect penalty) is finally cost-competitive at the scale a frontier lab needs. 750MW of dedicated compute over three years, $20B+ total contract value, with $1B in OpenAI capex toward Cerebras data centres. The new architectural pattern that matters for cost: SRAM-on-wafer sidesteps the HBM3/HBM4 memory bandwidth bottleneck that throttles GPU clusters on long-context inference.

🔬 Plain-English Explainer

Three different teams just made AI inference dramatically cheaper, and they did it in three different places at once. Quantix made the model itself smaller (think: zipping a file before opening it — the model takes less GPU memory and runs faster). Attention Matching shrank the model's "scratchpad memory" — the temporary notes the model keeps about your current conversation, which actually take up more GPU memory than the model weights for long sessions. And Cerebras built a chip that's an entire silicon wafer instead of dicing it into smaller chips, which removes a major performance bottleneck.

The practical result is that the cost to run a model like Claude or GPT in 6 months will likely be 5–10× cheaper than today, even without any new model release. For traders, this matters because the AI-token sector (TAO, RENDER, AKT) is partially pricing the assumption that compute is scarce. As compute gets less scarce, the "decentralised compute" thesis still works — but it gets squeezed by the AWS/Cerebras axis on price. Stay long utility tokens, not narrative tokens.

⚡ Implication in 3–6 months: The 5–10× cost reduction shows up as either better margins for hyperscalers (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google) or as price cuts to attract usage. Historically the answer is "both, in sequence" — quiet margin capture first, then a pricing announcement when a competitor moves. Watch Opus 4.7 / Sonnet 4.6 / GPT-5.5 pricing for cuts in the May–July window.
