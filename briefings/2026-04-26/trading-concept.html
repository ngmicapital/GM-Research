Alpha — Absorption

Sunday · 26 April 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #4 11-min read
TL;DR

Absorption is what happens when massive aggressive volume slams into a price level and price refuses to move — passive limit orders are silently eating every market order that hits them. It is Wyckoff's "effort vs result" inverted: huge effort, near-zero result, and the seller (or buyer) on the other side gets exhausted while the absorber accumulates inventory at one price. The practical edge: the strongest reversals don't start with bullish candles — they start with red candles that should have moved further than they did. Right now BTC is sitting at $78,126 because the $73K floor absorbed every wave of post-crash supply for two weeks straight.

§01
What It Is
Passive size eating aggressive flow · effort without result · the silent accumulator
Absorption is when one side keeps hitting the market with aggression and price won't move — the other side is quietly soaking up everything with passive orders.

Absorption is the orderflow signature of one large participant standing in front of a wave of aggression and refusing to move. Aggressive market sellers are firing into the bid. Volume is enormous. The footprint is a red wash. And yet the candle barely closes lower — sometimes it closes flat, sometimes it closes green. The asymmetry between volume printed and price moved is the entire signal. Whoever is on the passive side has more size than the entire incoming wave, and they are buying every single contract at one price.

This is the mirror image of continuation. In a continuation, aggression hits a level and price moves through it — sellers want out, the bids are thin, distance is travelled. In absorption, identical (or larger) aggression produces no distance. The hidden variable is what is sitting passively at the level: in continuation, almost nothing; in absorption, an institution-sized wall, often refilling itself iceberg-style as it gets eaten. Same aggressive flow, opposite outcome — and that opposite outcome is the trade.

This is also the cleanest live expression of Wyckoff's Law of Effort vs Result: when effort (volume) is large and result (range) is small, the dominant force is shifting under the surface. Wyckoff wrote about this watching tape spool out of a ticker in 1910. The mechanic hasn't changed in 116 years; only the venue has — from the NYSE floor to the Binance matching engine. What used to look like a thick block print at the same price now looks like a stacked footprint cell with 4,000 contracts at the bid and 50 ticks of nothing.

Absorption shows up in three places that matter: at support and resistance levels where institutions have pre-planted size; at Wyckoff springs and upthrusts where the absorber is the smart money triggering the reversal; and at liquidity pockets — the air gaps between value zones where retail piles in and gets fed to the wall. In all three cases, the absorber's intent is the same: load inventory at one price without moving the price against themselves.

§02
Mechanics
Four-stage process · effort:result formula · buy vs sell absorption
Absorption unfolds in four stages — aggression arrives, the wall holds, aggression exhausts, the reversal fires. Spotting it is a function of comparing volume printed to range travelled.

The mechanic is procedural, not magical. The same four phases play out whether you are watching BTC at the $73K floor or ES at a CME profile reference. Once you internalise the sequence, you stop chasing the entry candle and start waiting for the structure to complete.

The Four Stages of Absorption (sell-side example): ① AGGRESSION ARRIVES Aggressive market sellers fire into the bid at level L. CVD prints sharply negative. Footprint cells fill with red. ② THE WALL HOLDS Bid size at L stays constant (or refills) despite being hit. Price oscillates inside a 10–30 tick band — no follow-through. ③ AGGRESSION EXHAUSTS Sell market-order size shrinks. Tape pace slows. CVD goes flat. Last sellers have fired their ammo. ④ REVERSAL FIRES Absorber lifts the offer (or new aggressors do). Price snaps away from L. The trade is the snap. Effort:Result Anomaly Detection: E:R = Volume traded at level / |Range moved at level| Normal: E:R ≈ 1.0 — volume produces proportional range Absorption: E:R >> 5.0 — volume produces almost no range Threshold: Flag any 5m bar where E:R is >3× the prior 20-bar median Iceberg Detection (refilling order signature): IF best_bid_size remains ≥ X for N consecutive seconds WHILE aggressive_sells > X are firing into it THEN passive bid is iceberg → absorption confirmed
In crypto, "X" depends on the symbol — for BTC perp on Binance, a refilling 8–15 BTC bid eating 50–100 BTC of aggressive market sells over 30–90 seconds is textbook iceberg absorption.

The directional flavour matters. Buy absorption (passive bidders eating aggressive sellers) typically produces a base and reversal upward — the absorber is loading long inventory cheaply. Sell absorption (passive sellers eating aggressive buyers) produces a cap and reversal downward — the absorber is loading short inventory or distributing existing inventory at a peak. In both cases the giveaway is the same: aggression in one direction, no movement in that direction, then movement in the other direction.

Absorption pairs naturally with the previous two concepts in this series. OBI (Issue #3) shows you the structural lean — where the absorber's wall is sitting before price arrives. CVD (Issue #2) shows you the running aggression score — divergence between CVD direction and price is often the absorption signature in chart form. The absorption itself is the moment those two cross paths: large negative CVD + flat price = buy absorption in progress.

§04
In Practice
Four rules · worked entry · timeframe fit
The one rule: never enter while aggression is still firing. Wait for exhaustion — Stage 3 — then enter on the first lift.
1

Wait for Stage 3 — Exhaustion — Before Entry

The most common absorption mistake is entering during Stage 2 while sellers are still aggressive. The wall might hold, or it might break — you don't know yet. Wait for CVD to flatten and tape pace to slow. The signal is that the market-sell ammo has been spent. Now you have asymmetry: aggressive flow has stopped, passive size has accumulated, and the next aggressor will be on the other side.

2

Use the Absorbed Level as Your Stop — But Tight

If you fade an absorption setup, your stop sits just beyond the level the wall defended. If $73,120 absorbed and you go long, your stop is $72,950 — close enough that if the wall fails, you're out cheap; far enough that normal noise doesn't tag you. The R is paid for by waiting until Stage 3: you skip the slop and enter at the highest-conviction moment, so the stop can be tight without being noise-prone.

3

Differentiate Absorption From Continuation Live

Identical aggressive volume can produce reversal (absorption) or breakout (continuation). The live distinction is whether the resting size at the level holds or evaporates. Persistent bid size at one price as red prints fly is absorption. Bid size that thins out tick-by-tick as price approaches is the wall being pulled — that's continuation, do not fade it. Stand down rather than guess.

4

Layer With Wyckoff Structure for Conviction

Absorption at a random price is suspicious; absorption at a Wyckoff Spring, Selling Climax, or Phase B trading-range edge is institutional. Tony — when you spot tape-level absorption, mark whether the level is also the SC, ST, Spring, or LPS. The double signal — orderflow absorption + Wyckoff event — is what separates a scalp from a structural entry. A spring without absorption is hopium; absorption without structure is luck.

§07
Common Traps
Premature entries · iceberg illusions · absorption vs continuation · the rare-event problem
The biggest mistake: entering during Stage 2 while sellers are still firing. The signal is exhaustion, not the wall holding once.
1
Entering Before Exhaustion (Stage 2 Trap)

You see the bid wall hold for one minute. CVD is still crashing. You go long anyway because "absorption is happening." Then the wall gets pulled — or simply overwhelmed — and you're stopped out at the breakdown. The wall holding for one minute is not the trade. The trade is when sellers stop firing. Wait for the tape pace to die and CVD to flatten before entry. Patience pays the R.

2
The Iceberg Illusion — Visible Size Is Not Total Size

What looks like 5 BTC at the bid that keeps getting refilled may actually be a 200 BTC iceberg with only the top showing. Conversely, what looks like 50 BTC at the bid may be spoofed and pulled the moment price approaches. The visible book is one signal; the question is whether what you see persists under fire. Use Bookmap, Cignals, or any heatmap tool that shows historical book state — refilling icebergs leave a clear trail; spoofs vanish.

3
Confusing Absorption With Slow-Motion Continuation

Heavy red volume + small range can be absorption, or it can be the early stage of a breakdown where the offer side is holding briefly before the floor caves. The differentiator: in absorption, the bid keeps refilling at the same price; in slow continuation, the bid drips lower, level by level, even if each level holds for a bit. Track the actual best-bid price, not just the volume. If best bid migrates downward = continuation, stand aside. If best bid stays parked = absorption candidate.

4
Ignoring the Hidden Side — OTC and ETF Authorized Participants

In crypto, much of the "absorption" you see at major levels is the on-exchange visible footprint of off-exchange institutional positioning — ETF authorized participants buying spot BTC to deliver against creations, OTC desks unwinding hedges, miners using algorithmic sales programs. The visible bid wall is real, but it's a slice of a larger flow you can't see directly. Treat persistent absorption at major macro levels as a tell that something institutional is happening, even when you can't identify the participant directly.

5
Honest Callout: Textbook Absorption Is Rare — Most "Absorption" Is Noise

Real absorption setups — the kind that produce the 8R reversals — print maybe a handful of times per month per liquid pair. The vast majority of "I see absorption" trader posts on X are misreads of normal-range tape, premature pattern recognition, or post-hoc rationalisation of a low that happened to hold. Don't force the pattern. If you can't articulate Stages 1, 2, 3, and 4 in real time with specific numbers, you're not looking at absorption — you're looking at chop. The discipline is in passing on the unclear setups, not in trading every wall that holds for two candles.
