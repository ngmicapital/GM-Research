TL;DR — Driving Thesis

Amazon agreed to acquire the optimization technology behind Decart and hire its founders in a deal reported at about $7.5 billion, a bet that chip software can narrow Nvidia's lead. Anthropic's Claude pull-request tooling, Sierra's $30 billion raise, and the Poppy 1.0 agent protocol are the other items to act on, while the AI-trade sell-off in Asia is the risk to monitor.

Act on two things: the Amazon–Decart deal, because it signals where AI cost pressure is going (inference efficiency on custom silicon rather than only more GPUs), and Poppy 1.0, because any site that serves customers should publish a poppy.json file now even though the spec still allows breaking changes. Monitor three things: whether Decart clears regulatory review, whether Venice's buyback cadence holds after its late-November bounce, and whether the Asian AI-stock sell-off, which pushed Kospi down about 5% on Wednesday's open, deepens or stabilises.

Model Landscape

Alibaba's Qwen3.8 line is the open-weight story of the week, with the 27B model absorbing most community uptake while the 2.4-trillion-parameter Max model sits in a half-open state.

Qwen3.8-27B becomes the week's open-weight centre of gravity

On Hugging Face's trending list for 23 November, the Qwen3.8-27B base release had about 20,900 likes and 6.3 million downloads, and a quantised GGUF build from unsloth alone accounted for roughly 7.7 million downloads. The vendor model card reports 73.0 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and 61.7 on SWE-bench Pro, against 78.2 and 53.4 for the Opus 4.6 Max column it compares with. Those are vendor-reported numbers and the comparison model is no longer current, so treat them as directional.

Qwen3.8-Max: weights promised, rankings middling

Alibaba previewed the 2.4-trillion-parameter Qwen3.8-Max in July, said it would release the weights, and opened the cloud version on 3 August. An independent leaderboard places the Max model at 65.4 out of 100, ranked 36th of 216, so it is competitive but not leading. The open-weights release of the Max model is the item to verify before the next issue; I did not confirm a dated weights drop in today's sources.

Cohere Command A+ trails on quality but leads on latency

Cohere's Command A+, launched in May, scores 13.13 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v4.3.2 against 38.36 for GPT-5.5 and 41.79 for Claude Opus 4.8, so the quality gap is wide. Its reported median output speed of 156 tokens per second, against about 51 for Qwen3.8 27B, is the reason to consider it for latency-bound workloads. This is a background comparison rather than a new release.

Claude Ecosystem

Anthropic's news this cycle is about product plumbing, not a new model, and the single-source Claude Ship report needs confirmation before anyone builds on it.

Claude moves toward an end-to-end code pull-request pipeline

Two daily digests say Anthropic has turned Claude into a full pull-request pipeline, and one of them names a product called Claude Ship that lets agents review, fix and approve code changes [paraphrased]. Neither report links to an Anthropic primary announcement in the material I could access, so treat the feature set as provisional. If it ships as described, it moves Claude from suggesting diffs to approving them, which raises the bar for review policy in any team that adopts it.

Follow-up on the listing: the IPO narrative has been overtaken

This is a follow-up to the listing-day and first-session coverage in recent issues. The story has shifted from the IPO pop to the broader sell-off in AI shares, which has hit Asian markets and Nasdaq through this week. I did not confirm a current ANT share price for today, so no level is quoted here; check the market feed before relying on any valuation figure.

What to watch in Claude's product cadence

The useful test is whether the pull-request features arrive with audit logs, approval scopes and a clear rollback path. Those controls, rather than the headline capability, will decide whether enterprises turn the feature on for production repositories in the next quarter.

Venice ties its token to buybacks and emissions

Venice repurchased about $628,000 of VVV between 9 and 15 November, and its weekly buybacks rose roughly 303% from the prior week. The project also cut emissions, which is why the token gained about 28.6% over seven days. The test is cadence: buybacks have to keep pace with new supply, and the sources do not show a new product milestone behind the move.

Render and Akash show real but small on-chain revenue

On a 30-day annualised basis read on 20 November, Render showed about $6.35M and Akash about $2.94M, together roughly $9.3M. io.net's own estimate is larger at about $16.3M, which shows how much depends on the methodology. Render's AI work runs through its Dispersed subnet, and the revenue remains mostly rendering.

Bittensor keeps shipping subnet infrastructure

Bittensor's subnet model drew attention in late July, when TargonOS went live for confidential AI compute with privacy enforced by Intel hardware, and Engy began serving frontier open models with verified inference. The most recent protocol-level change in the sources is the August Root Reborn upgrade, and some trackers still disagree on whether TAO belongs in a DePIN category at all.

Open-Source Pulse

Alibaba's Qwen3.8-27B leads Hugging Face's trending list, with video and speech releases filling out the rest of the week.

Qwen3.8-27B and its derivatives dominate downloads

The Qwen3.8-27B family is the clear centre of the week's downloads, with the base release, unsloth's GGUF build and two community derivatives together accounting for tens of millions of downloads. A GitHub-hosted trending digest for 22 November also lists facebook/sam3 at about 7,100 likes and 1.85 million downloads, which confirms that SAM3 still holds the segmentation category.

Lightricks video model leads generative video

Lightricks' LTX-2.5, an image-to-video diffusion model, had about 3 million downloads in the 23 November digest, making it the most-downloaded generative video model of the week. Apple's FastFrames-L text-to-video model, built on Wan-AI's base architecture, is early-stage and shows zero downloads so far.

Compact speech models gain traction

Haru-4B, a full-duplex speech-to-speech model from higurashi-ai, has about 8,600 downloads and 894 likes, and it is the compact open option in that category. Inworld's voice-isolator model is newer, with high likes and no downloads yet, so its adoption is still unproven.

Deals & Regulation

Amazon's reported $7.5 billion purchase of Decart's optimization technology is the week's clearest infrastructure deal, and Sierra's $30 billion round sets the application-layer marker.

Amazon buys Decart's optimization stack and its team

Amazon announced on 23 November that it will acquire the technology behind the Decart Optimization Stack and bring on the founders and research team, and it did not disclose financial terms. Bloomberg reported a price of about $7.5 billion, nearly double the valuation from Decart's May round. The deal excludes Decart's own models, runs through Amazon's chip unit, and is subject to regulatory clearance. Decart claims its software can cut the cost of some neural networks by a hundredfold, which is a vendor claim.

Sierra raises at a $30 billion valuation

Sierra, the customer-service agent company co-founded by Bret Taylor, is raising $1.75 billion at a $30 billion valuation, led by Greenoaks and Lightspeed. The valuation nearly doubles its $15.8 billion mark from May, and the company says annual revenue has passed $400 million. Its pricing model charges only when agents complete the work.

Markets are pricing the capex question

Asian markets reversed early gains on Wednesday as investors questioned whether heavy AI spending would pay off, with Kospi down about 5% and Nikkei 225 down 2.9%. The Financial Times reports that US tech stocks entered bear-market territory after their worst week since the pandemic. This is market context rather than a regulatory story.

Workflow & Tools

The agent-access layer moved from draft to product this week: Poppy reached 1.0, and Walmart opened its storefront to a shopping agent before Black Friday.

Poppy 1.0 gives businesses a front door for agents

Sierra released version 1.0 of the Personal Agent Protocol on 13 November, following a draft announced with Meta on 6 October. The protocol uses OAuth, starts sessions signed out under a stable per-company user ID, and lets the person choose read or write access. Shopify co-authored the commerce extension, which handles what happens when an agent arrives, while the shopping transaction itself is handled by the separate commerce protocol. The spec is still labelled draft 1.0 and permits breaking changes, so publishing a poppy.json file now is low cost while the details settle.

Walmart's Agent Gateway goes live

Walmart launched its Agent Gateway on 23 November, with Agent Profiles that let customers set spending limits, approve purchases and review a full transaction history. Meta's Muse is the first agent through the gateway, and it can find and buy Black Friday deals through Walmart's Sparky assistant. Walmart says more agents and apps are coming, so this is the first retailer-scale test of agent ingress.

Research Radar

One small study is worth tracking this week, because it documents how people describe ongoing relationships with AI assistants.

Researchers at Spain's INGENIO centre, run by CSIC and the Polytechnic University of Valencia, interviewed 17 people who were in romantic relationships with AI assistants such as ChatGPT, Character.AI and Replika. The sample is small and qualitative, and this summary comes from a press write-up rather than the paper itself, so treat the findings as hypotheses to check against the full text. The value is in the design: it asks about the relationship from the user's side rather than the model's benchmarks.

Second, an industry estimate from Deloitte says inference already accounts for about two-thirds of AI compute in 2026. This is a report figure rather than a peer-reviewed result, but it matters for the decentralized-inference thesis in the section above, because it is the workload those networks are trying to serve.
