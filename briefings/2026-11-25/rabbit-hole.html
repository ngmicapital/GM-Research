The Traffic Jam That Needs No Cause, and Travels Backwards

How a jam forms on a road with no bottleneck, why it drifts upstream while every car moves forward, and what it tells us about feedback.

A traffic jam can form on a busy road with no crash, no roadworks and no bend, and the cause is ordinary human reaction delay amplified along the line of cars. In 2008 a Nagoya team put 22 cars on a 230 m loop with nothing to slow them and watched a jam grow out of nothing.

TL;DR

Most of us have sat in a jam with no visible cause: a wall of brake lights appears, crawls for a few hundred metres, and then dissolves for no reason anyone can see. The textbook answer is a bottleneck. A 2008 experiment by physicist Yuki Sugiyama and colleagues removed every bottleneck and still produced a jam that travels backwards. The mechanism is a feedback loop: each driver responds late to the car ahead, and those delayed responses stack up until a small wobble becomes a standing wave of compressed traffic.

Section 01

The Story

Almost everyone has sat in a jam with no visible cause: no crash ahead, no lane closure, no sign of a problem, just a band of brake lights that appears, crawls for a few hundred metres and then melts away. The standard explanation blames a bottleneck, a point where the road carries fewer cars than are entering it. In 2008 physicist Yuki Sugiyama and colleagues at Nagoya University set out to remove that explanation entirely and ask whether a jam would still form.

They built the cleanest possible test. The track was a 230 m circular road, flat and uniform, with no on-ramps, no merges and no lane changes allowed. Twenty-two drivers were told to hold a steady 30 km/h and keep a comfortable gap to the car in front. The loop had room for everyone, so by any ordinary reasoning nothing should have gone wrong. The only thing the drivers had to do was what drivers always do: react to the car ahead.

The experiment

22 vehicles, a 230 m circuit, a 30 km/h target speed and no bottleneck. The result was published in New Journal of Physics in 2008 as “Traffic jams without bottlenecks”.

Something did go wrong. Small differences in spacing, the ordinary wobble of human drivers who are never perfectly steady, grew lap after lap. A car slowed a little, the car behind slowed a little more, and the effect rolled down the ring. Eventually a dense cluster of slow cars formed and then travelled backwards around the loop, against the direction every car was moving. The jam was not located anywhere on the track. It was a pattern moving through the cars.

a jam cluster appears and propagates backward like a solitary wave
— Yuki Sugiyama et al., New Journal of Physics, 2008

What makes the ring so useful is that it strips out every excuse. The authors could not blame a truck, a merge or a crash, so whatever produced the jam had to be inside the way the drivers followed one another. That is the sense in which the jam has no cause: no single event started it, and no single driver did anything wrong. The jam is the collective output of many reasonable responses, each a fraction of a second late.

Section 02

Why a Brake Tap Becomes a Standing Wave

The modelling starts with the car-following rule. Each driver adjusts acceleration according to the gap to the car ahead and their own speed. In the optimal velocity model that Bando and colleagues published in 1995, each driver tries to reach a speed that depends on the gap, and does so with a finite sensitivity, meaning a finite willingness to respond to how far behind the car ahead they are. The model's key feature is that the response is not instant. A driver perceives the gap, decides, and then acts, and that delay is the ingredient that turns a harmless disturbance into a jam.

The stability test is a product of two numbers. Linear analysis of the model shows that traffic is stable when driver sensitivity multiplied by reaction time stays below one half. Sensitivity measures how strongly a driver reacts to a change in gap; reaction time measures how long the reaction takes to arrive. Below the threshold, a small speed change dies out as it passes back down the line. Above it, the same change grows. The optimal velocity function also has a turning point, so the stability depends on density: free-flowing traffic at very low density and very jammed traffic at very high density are stable, while the intermediate, dense-but-moving regime is where real roads spend their most fragile hours.

The stability test

In the optimal velocity framework, traffic is unstable when the product of driver sensitivity (λ) and reaction time (τ) exceeds one half. Below that line a disturbance fades; above it, it amplifies down the queue.

Now trace one brake tap. Driver A brakes slightly harder than necessary. Driver B, a second and a half later, sees the gap closing and brakes harder again to restore the gap. Driver C does the same, and each response overshoots a little because each driver is reacting to the car ahead as it was, not as it is. The slowdown moves backwards through the queue and gets larger at each step. The compression point, where cars bunch up, therefore drifts upstream, even though each individual car keeps moving forward. The jam is a wave in density, not an object sitting on the road. That is why the sources describe it as travelling backwards like a solitary wave rather than as a fixed blockage.

The threshold also explains why the jam appears only on busy roads. At low density each driver has room to absorb a disturbance, so the wave dies out. Only when the cars are close enough that the delayed responses can reinforce one another does a small fluctuation grow into a self-sustaining wave. Earlier simulation work by Nagel and Schreckenberg in 1992 had already shown that random slowdowns in a cellular automaton could produce jams with no bottleneck, and later work by Kerner and by Flynn and colleagues framed these as self-sustained nonlinear waves. The Nagoya experiment mattered because it put that prediction on a physical track with human drivers, rather than leaving it inside a computer.

Section 03

The Connections

The rival explanation, and where it falls short. Traffic engineering has long treated capacity as the key quantity: when inflow exceeds what a road can carry, a queue forms, and the queue is the jam. The ring shows that a bottleneck is sufficient to produce a jam but not necessary. It also has a limit worth stating plainly. A closed loop with no lane changes isolates car-following and nothing else. Real motorways add on-ramps, trucks, lane-changing and rubbernecking, and the Nagoya authors themselves describe lane-changing and car-following as the drivers' contributions. The ring tells us the mechanism can work without a bottleneck; how much of real congestion it explains is a question for field data, not for the loop alone.

The ironic coda. Every individual behaviour in the experiment is sensible. Keeping a comfortable gap is safe; braking gently is good practice; reacting to the car ahead is what the rules ask for. The harm comes from the sum of those sensible acts, each carrying a small delay. Nobody in the loop is the villain, and nobody can stop the wave by driving perfectly, because the wave is generated by the fact that perfect steadiness is impossible for a human. The problem is structural, which is why the cure has to be structural too.

Engineering a fix. If the instability lives in the feedback loop, one remedy is to change the loop itself. Field experiments on autonomous vehicles, published as a study on dissipating stop-and-go waves, examined whether a few vehicles driving smoothly could damp waves that human drivers generate. More recent work trains deep reinforcement learning agents in a setup modelled on Sugiyama's ring, and it takes as a premise that human driving is inherently unstable. The engineering question is therefore not whether drivers should try harder, but whether a small fraction of controlled cars can absorb the reaction delay for everyone behind them.

The markets parallel, offered as an analogy. Trading systems share this anatomy. Participants respond to price gaps with delays; algorithms respond to other algorithms; and a small disturbance in order flow can grow into a cascade in which no single order and no single piece of news is the cause. The analogy is mine rather than a claim from the traffic literature, but the structure is the same one the ring exposed: stability depends on how sensitively participants respond multiplied by how long their responses take. A market that looks calm when liquidity is abundant can become unstable when many participants are reacting to the same moving gap at once. Flash crashes, where prices fall and recover within minutes with no decisive headline, are the closest market equivalent of a phantom jam.

Further Down the Hole

📖
The original experiment

Sugiyama and colleagues’ 2008 paper in New Journal of Physics is short, readable and lays out the ring design, the observations and the mechanism. It is the place to start before any simulation.

🧮
The optimal velocity model

Bando and colleagues’ 1995 paper, “Dynamical model of traffic congestion and numerical simulation”, is where the instability threshold comes from. Reading it explains why reaction time, not speed, sets the fate of a queue.

🚗
Robot cars as damping

The arXiv study on dissipating stop-and-go waves with autonomous vehicles asks whether a few smooth-driving cars can heal waves that humans create. It is the most direct bridge from the ring to real motorways.

🌊
Waves that sustain themselves

Flynn and colleagues’ 2009 Physical Review E paper treats traffic jams as self-sustained nonlinear waves. It is a good next step for anyone who wants the mathematics behind the backward-moving jam.
