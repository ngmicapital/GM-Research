Alpha — Bid/Ask Imbalance

Wednesday, 25 November 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue TBC 10 min read
TL;DR

Bid/ask imbalance is the normalised difference between resting bid size and resting ask size inside the same distance from price, and it matters because it shows where friction sits in the book before anything trades. Practical takeaway: treat it as a context reading on posted intent, never a directional call. On 24 November the near book leaned bid on Binance BTCUSDT while BTC slid under $60,000, which is the whole lesson: a lopsided book only means something once executed flow confirms it.

§01
What It Is
The posted book, measured as a ratio
Bid/ask imbalance is the normalised difference between resting bid size and resting ask size inside the same distance from price.

Bid/ask imbalance is a measurement of posted size, not a prediction. Take the resting bids and the resting asks inside the same distance from the mid, subtract one side from the other, and divide by the total. The output runs from −1 (all size on the ask) to +1 (all size on the bid), and zero means the near book is balanced. That is the whole number. Everything you do with it afterwards is interpretation, and most of the mistakes happen in that step.

The number is worth having because a lopsided book changes how easily price can move. When the ask side is thin, each incoming market buy eats through more levels and prints a bigger jump. A bid-heavy book does the reverse for sells. The imbalance does not say which way the move will go; it says where the friction sits, and that is a narrower claim than most traders assume.

Keep it separate from order flow. Imbalance reads intent that has not traded yet. Footprint cells and CVD read trades that already happened. The two can disagree for long stretches, and the disagreement is often the most useful part of the picture.

Builds on Footprint Charts (2026-11-22): that issue covered diagonal imbalances inside each candle. This is the book-side version of the same idea, measured before the trade rather than after.

§02
Mechanics
Band, sum, normalise, persist
It shows which side of the near book holds more posted size, and how easily a market order can push through the thin side.

The mechanic is a ratio built in four steps from the visible book. Each step can be done wrong, and the errors compound.

  1. Choose the band. Pick a distance from the top of book, for example 0 to 3% either side. Both sides must use the same distance, or the ratio compares unlike things.
  2. Sum the resting bids inside the band, then sum the resting asks inside the same band.
  3. Normalise the difference by the total so the result sits between −1 and +1 and can be compared across time.
  4. Track the value over time. A reading that flips sign every few seconds is noise. A reading that holds through several windows while price tests a level is information.
I = (Bidband − Askband) / (Bidband + Askband)
I runs from −1 to +1. Both sums use the same distance from mid. Bid and ask are resting, cancellable size, so I is a snapshot of posted intent, not of executed trades.

The band choice changes the answer more than most traders expect. The table below shows the usual variants and where each one misleads.

VariantWhat it countsWhere it misleads
Top-of-bookBest bid size against best ask sizeOne queue can be pulled in milliseconds, so a small ratio flips easily
Near band (0 to 3%)All resting size within a fixed distanceIgnores size just outside the band, which can matter on a sweep
Multi-level cumulativeSeveral price rungs summed on each sideDeep size far from price can dominate and tell you little about the next few prints
Event-based (order flow imbalance)Adds, cancels and fills tracked across timeNeeds a live feed with clean timestamps and is hard to eyeball on a chart

§04
In Practice
Use it as a filter, not a trigger
Use both sides over the same band, require persistence across windows, and never read a single snapshot as intent.
1

Use it as a filter, not a trigger

Read the band at a level you already have a reason to care about, such as a prior low or a Wyckoff spring zone. A bid-heavy reading into support is context that makes the level more interesting, but it does not give you the entry.

2

Require persistence

A single reading is worth nothing. Hold the band across several windows and check whether the bid side absorbs sell prints without shrinking. Size that disappears on the first test has told you what it was for.

3

Watch for the flip

If the imbalance swings to the opposite side the moment price approaches the heavy side, the size was probably never meant to trade. That pattern belongs with the spoofing work in the 2026-11-23 issue.

Worked example (illustrative, not a live trade)

Reclaim of $60,000 with a retest. Entry at $60,050 only after executed buy flow confirms and the 0 to 3% band stays at or above +0.30 for three consecutive windows. Stop at $59,800, below the level, so risk is $250. Target at $60,550, a reward of $500, which is 2R. If the band goes neutral before the target, the trade is closed on the band, not on the stop.

Timeframe fit: read the band on 1 to 5 minute windows for execution, and use the 1h to 4h structure to decide whether a bid-heavy reading matters at all.

§07
Common Traps
Where the number lies
Treating a bid-heavy snapshot as a buy signal, when the bids may be pulled the moment price arrives.
1
Reading the posted book as intent

Resting size can be cancelled before anything trades against it. Ask how long the size has been there and whether it survived the first test. The spoofing issue from 23 November covers the pull pattern in detail.

2
Comparing unequal bands

Bid size within 3% against ask size within 30% is a units mismatch, not an imbalance. Fix the band, apply it to both sides, and write it down next to every reading.

3
Confusing book imbalance with order flow

A bid-heavy book can sit alongside taker sells that are doing the damage. Pair the static reading with executed aggressor flow before you believe either one.

4
Treating a fixed ratio as a rule

Some vendors quote a 3:1 bid-to-ask ratio as a warning. BloFin describes that kind of ratio as unreliable as a prediction tool, because large orders can be pulled instantly. Use the ratio as a prompt to look at the tape, not as a threshold to trade.

!
Overhyped: imbalance that predicts price

Academic work on order flow imbalance, including Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov (2010) [standard reference, not live-verified this run], found a near-linear link to very short-horizon price changes. Most of that evidence comes from traditional markets, not crypto. Any claim of a dependable crypto edge from a static book reading is marketing until you have tested it on your own data.
