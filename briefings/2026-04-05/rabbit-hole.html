Rabbit Hole

The Dollar
Nobody Controls

Economics 5 April 2026 · Sydney, Australia
TL;DR

There is a parallel dollar system operating beyond the reach of any government or central bank, and it is larger than the entire US economy. It is called the Eurodollar market — not because it involves euros, but because it began in Europe. It was born in the 1950s when Soviet banks, fearing US asset seizures, moved their dollar deposits to London. A US regulation capping bank interest rates then caused it to explode. By the end of the 1960s it was $70 billion. By 2023 it had surpassed $13 trillion. No physical dollars move through it; it runs on ledger entries between international banks. The Federal Reserve cannot directly control it. When it seized up in 2008, the Fed had to pump nearly $600 billion through emergency swap lines to foreign central banks just to prevent global dollar starvation — and almost nobody noticed.

Section 01

The Story

On 28 February 1957, a telex was sent from the Moscow Narodny Bank — a Soviet-controlled institution registered not in Moscow but in London — transferring $800,000 to a counterparty account in Europe. The dollars in question never moved. Physically, they remained in correspondent accounts at US banks in New York, as they always had. What changed was the legal ownership: the claim on those dollars now resided in a British institution beyond the jurisdictional reach of Washington. The first Eurodollars had been created.

The backstory begins earlier. In 1948, the US government seized Yugoslav gold held in American banks after Yugoslavia broke with Stalin. The message was unambiguous: dollar assets held in New York were assets the US could freeze or confiscate with the stroke of a pen. Soviet bloc banks took note. They needed dollars — the world's primary trading currency — but they needed them somewhere out of reach. The Moscow Narodny Bank in London, and its counterpart Banque Commerciale pour l'Europe du Nord in Paris (whose telex address, Eurobank, would eventually lend the system its name), were the solution.

The Soviet gambit would have remained a Cold War curiosity had American regulators not then accidentally supplied rocket fuel. The culprit was Regulation Q, a Depression-era rule capping the interest rates US banks could pay on deposits. Through the 1950s and into the 1960s, market interest rates crept higher while Regulation Q's ceiling stayed fixed. By 1969, the ceiling on savings accounts sat at 4%, while three-month Treasury bills were approaching 7%. American depositors were being paid below-market rates by law. Foreign banks faced no such restriction.

The arithmetic was simple: deposit dollars in London instead of New York and earn the market rate. American corporations with overseas operations began doing exactly that, parking their dollar surpluses in European banks rather than repatriating them. The market that Soviet paranoia had created, Regulation Q now filled with money. By 1970, the Eurodollar market stood at $70 billion. By 1980, it had crossed $500 billion. By the late 1990s, no authoritative estimate was possible — the market had grown too large, too distributed, and too intricately interlinked for any single institution to measure it.

The entities doing this business were not rogue actors. They were the London offices of Deutsche Bank, Barclays, Citibank, and eventually every major global financial institution. The Eurodollar market had evolved from a geopolitical workaround into the plumbing of global trade. Commodities from oil to copper to soybeans are priced and settled in dollars. International loans are denominated in dollars. Companies from São Paulo to Seoul borrow in dollars, invoice in dollars, hold their reserves in dollars. Most of that dollar activity flows not through the US banking system but through the Eurodollar network — the offshore ledger system that Washington cannot directly reach.

The Counterintuitive Core

The world's most important financial system was not designed. It was not authorised by any government or created by any treaty. It was assembled piecemeal — first by Soviet cold-war paranoia, then by a US domestic regulation that had nothing to do with international finance. The largest monetary market in history arrived by accident.

Section 02

The Mechanism

Understanding the Eurodollar system requires abandoning one intuition: that "dollars" means paper currency or coins. It does not. The system runs on ledger entries — accounting claims that represent the right to receive dollars, passed between banks through interlinked balance sheets. No physical dollar ever crosses a border. What travels is a liability: Bank A records that it owes Bank B one million dollars; Bank B records that it is owed one million dollars by Bank A. The dollar is the unit of account. The system is made of promises.

This matters because it means the Eurodollar market can create dollar-denominated liquidity without the Federal Reserve's involvement. When a London bank takes a dollar deposit and lends it to a Tokyo bank, which lends it to a Korean exporter, who uses it to pay a Brazilian supplier — that chain of transactions has created and distributed dollar credit without a single dollar ever leaving New York or passing through a Fed-supervised institution. The Fed sets the interest rate on base-money reserves. It cannot set the interest rate on this offshore credit chain. It cannot expand or contract it by turning a dial. It can only influence it indirectly, through its effects on the appetite of the global banks that are the nodes of the system.

The key actors are not retail banks. They are large wholesale banks — institutions that borrow and lend in enormous size through instruments like Eurodollar futures (the most actively traded futures contract in the world for much of the late 20th century), foreign exchange swaps, and short-term interbank lending. The system is not a unified market with a central exchange; it is a web of bilateral relationships, with hubs in London, Tokyo, the Cayman Islands, and Singapore. The dollars that flow through it are priced at LIBOR — the London Interbank Offered Rate — which was set by a daily survey of the largest participating banks, not by any government.

In Plain English

Imagine the US dollar as a language. The Federal Reserve controls the official dictionary and printing press. But billions of people speak the language in countries the Fed has no authority over, coining new words and doing deals in it without permission. The Eurodollar market is the global dollar conversation happening entirely outside America's jurisdiction — and it is far larger than the domestic one.

The 2008 financial crisis exposed what this architecture means in a crisis. As US mortgage-backed securities lost value, global banks that had loaded their balance sheets with them found themselves holding assets nobody wanted. The interbank trust that lubricates the Eurodollar system — the willingness of Bank A to lend overnight to Bank B — evaporated. Dollar credit seized. Banks around the world needed dollars to honour their obligations and could not get them through the normal private-market channels. The shortage was not a shortage of base money inside the US; it was a shortage of offshore dollar credit. The Federal Reserve had to act as lender of last resort to the entire global Eurodollar system — a role nowhere mentioned in the Federal Reserve Act. Between 2008 and 2009, the Fed extended nearly $600 billion in emergency loans to foreign central banks through currency swap lines. The European Central Bank, the Bank of England, the Bank of Japan, and the Swiss National Bank all queued up. Almost no mainstream media coverage connected the emergency to the Eurodollar architecture it was actually rescuing.

Section 03

The Connections

Dollar dominance is stranger than advertised. The standard story of the dollar's global role points to the 1944 Bretton Woods conference, when allied nations agreed to peg their currencies to the dollar, which was pegged to gold. That system collapsed in 1971 when Nixon ended dollar-gold convertibility. But the Eurodollar system had already moved most of the action offshore by then. What sustains dollar dominance today is not Bretton Woods, not the petrodollar (though that plays a role), and not US military power alone — it is the network effects of a $13-trillion offshore credit infrastructure that every major global bank is embedded in, and which denominates most global trade. Replacing it would require replacing not a policy but a web.

China's dollar problem. One reason China finds it difficult to displace the dollar with the renminbi is that the Eurodollar network is not owned or controlled by the US. It is a market. Any country that wants to trade globally at scale finds it more efficient to join the existing dollar network than to build a parallel one from scratch. The renminbi's internationalisation has made real but limited progress precisely because no offshore renminbi interbank credit market of comparable depth exists. China cannot decree its currency into global reserve status; the Eurodollar's incumbency advantage is structural, not political.

The Fed's real power (and its limits). When the Fed raises rates, it raises the cost of base money inside the US banking system. That tightening transmits to the Eurodollar market — but with lags and leakages. A Korean company borrowing offshore dollars from a Japanese bank is subject to market conditions shaped by Fed policy, but not directly controlled by it. Economist Jeff Snider has argued for years that the Fed is not the monetary sovereign it claims to be — that the Eurodollar system's chronic contractions since 2008 explain global deflationary pressure more accurately than any Fed model does. His is a minority view, but the 2008 swap-line scramble is a data point difficult to argue away.

Eurodollar futures as economic oracle. For most of the period from 1981 to 2021, the Eurodollar futures market at the Chicago Mercantile Exchange was the most liquid futures market on earth, trading contracts worth trillions of dollars annually. Because participants were pricing the future path of offshore dollar interest rates, the market functioned as a continuous real-time forecast of global credit conditions — more accurate and faster-moving than any economic survey. In 2021 the contracts began transitioning to SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate), as LIBOR was phased out after the rate-rigging scandal. The market still exists; only the reference rate changed.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

History The Yugoslav Gold Seizure (1948)

When Yugoslavia broke with Stalin, the US froze Yugoslav gold held in New York. The episode sent a signal to every Soviet-bloc nation: dollar assets in American banks were hostage to American politics. Trace this single act forward and it connects, 60 years later, to the 2008 global dollar crisis.

Regulation Regulation Q and the Architecture of Avoidance

The 1933 Glass-Steagall Act included Regulation Q to prevent bank competition from driving up deposit rates — a measure aimed at domestic stability that inadvertently made offshore dollar deposits the superior product. It was finally repealed in 2011, decades after the damage was done.

Crisis The Fed's Secret Bailout of the World

The 2008 currency swap lines were the largest emergency financial operation in Federal Reserve history. The full scale — $580 billion at peak — was not publicly disclosed until an audit mandated by the Dodd-Frank Act years later. Most Americans still have no idea it happened.

Geopolitics De-Dollarisation and Its Discontents

Russia's dollar reserves were frozen after the 2022 Ukraine invasion, reviving the same fear that created the Eurodollar in 1948. China, Saudi Arabia, and others have accelerated discussions about alternatives. The structural question is whether any alternative can replicate the depth and network of a 70-year-old offshore credit system built by the private sector.

Ideas Jeff Snider's Eurodollar University

The most systematic lay treatment of how the Eurodollar system works — and why the Fed cannot rescue it when it breaks — is Snider's long-running podcast and essay series. His central argument: persistent global deflation since 2008 is a Eurodollar problem, not a Fed failure. Polarising but rigorously argued.
