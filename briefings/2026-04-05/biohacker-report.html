⚡ TL;DR — Driving Thesis

Two papers just dropped in Nature that should change how you think about immune health and longevity: your thymus gland — long assumed to be a useless relic after puberty — is still actively predicting how long you live and how well you fight cancer. Simultaneously, the ACSM released their first resistance training guidelines update in 17 years, distilling 137 systematic reviews into one uncomfortable truth: 10 sets per muscle group per week is your hypertrophy target, and almost everything beyond that (advanced techniques, complex periodization, training to failure) doesn't move the needle for most people. This issue is heavy on landmark guidance: audit your volume, protect your thymus through consistent training and adequate sleep, and hold off on BPC-157 until someone actually runs a human trial.

01 Longevity & Biomarkers
Your Thymus Is Not Dead — And Its Size Predicts How Long You Live
Observational Observational via Nature (April 2026)

Two studies published simultaneously in Nature this week dismantle a 60-year-old assumption in medicine: that the thymus gland stops doing anything useful after puberty. Analysis of CT scans from over 50,000 adults using AI imaging showed that people with larger, healthier thymus glands had significantly lower all-cause mortality. The second study, in cancer patients undergoing immunotherapy, found that those with higher thymic health scores responded better to treatment and survived longer — driven by greater T-cell receptor diversity.

🔬 In Plain English — Thymus Gland

The thymus is a small gland behind your breastbone that trains immune cells called T-cells — the soldiers your body uses to fight infection and kill cancer cells. Medicine assumed it shrivelled up and switched off in your 20s, but these new studies show it stays active and influences your health well into middle and old age.

🔬 In Plain English — T-Cell Receptor Diversity

T-cell receptor diversity is like having a larger vocabulary of threats your immune system can recognise. Higher diversity means your immune system can respond to more types of cancer and infection. When the thymus is healthier, it generates more varied T-cells — which is why it predicted better cancer immunotherapy outcomes.

The AI-generated "thymic health score" derived from routine CT imaging is the key development here. This isn't a bespoke research scan — it was applied to existing clinical scans, which means this could be rolled into standard imaging protocols relatively quickly. Right now there's no commercial tool to get your thymic health score, but this is the kind of biomarker that moves from research to clinical practice fast when the underlying data is this strong.

What this means for you: You can't directly measure your thymic health score yet. But the well-established drivers of thymic preservation — regular exercise, adequate sleep, and avoiding chronic stress — are the same levers this research points toward. This isn't new advice dressed up as new science; it's old advice getting a mechanistic upgrade.

The Science of Aging Is Shifting Away From Individual Fixes
Expert Opinion via Targeting Longevity World Congress 2026

The 2nd World Congress on Targeting Longevity (Berlin, April 8–9) represents a meaningful consensus shift: the field is moving from the "fix one thing at a time" model (senolytic drugs, NAD+ precursors, single pathway targets) toward systems-level biology. The framing is now "preserving biological coordination" — meaning how well your body's systems communicate with each other may matter more than the health of any one system in isolation.

🔬 In Plain English — Senolytic Drugs

Senolytics are drugs designed to kill "zombie cells" (senescent cells) — old, damaged cells that stop dividing but refuse to die and instead release inflammatory signals. Removing them has shown life-extension effects in animal studies, but human evidence is still early.

🔬 In Plain English — NAD+

NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) is a molecule your cells use to convert food into energy and repair DNA. Levels decline with age. Precursors like NMN and NR are popular supplements that aim to boost NAD+, though whether oral supplementation meaningfully raises cellular NAD+ in humans remains contested.

This is a research-direction signal, not a protocol change. The practical implication is that multi-system interventions — training plus sleep plus nutrition plus stress management — are likely to outperform any single-compound approach, which is good news for those already doing the work.

⚡ Implication

Track your sleep and training consistency as thymic health proxies — a commercial thymic health score tool is plausibly 12–24 months away; stay positioned to act when it arrives.

02 Recovery & Sleep
Daily Exercise Beats Weekly Volume for Sleep Quality — The Frequency Signal
Meta Observational ScienceDirect / UT Austin

A 2026 meta-analysis confirms that aerobic exercise at low-to-moderate intensity, run for at least 10 weeks with ≥3 sessions/week and each session ≥30 minutes (≥90 minutes total weekly), is the most evidence-supported non-pharmacological intervention for sleep quality. More interesting is the UT Austin finding: daily exercise — even light activity — improves the quality of deep, restorative sleep more than equivalent volume compressed into fewer sessions. Frequency matters more than duration per session.

🔬 In Plain English — Deep Sleep (Slow-Wave Sleep)

Deep sleep is the most physically restorative phase of your sleep cycle — this is when your body releases growth hormone, repairs muscle tissue, and consolidates memories. More deep sleep = better physical recovery, stronger immune function, and better next-day performance. Exercise is one of the most reliable ways to increase deep sleep duration.

The practical read: if you're training PPL, you're hitting the gym 6 days out of 7, which positions you perfectly to capture this sleep benefit — provided your training ends early enough in the evening. High-intensity sessions within 3 hours of bed elevate core temperature and delay sleep onset, negating the benefit.

Fundamentals Card: The Sleep Optimisation Stack That Actually Has Evidence
RCT Meta

Since there's no single breakthrough finding this cycle, here's the evidence-ranked sleep stack — ordered by quality of evidence, not hype level:

Strong Evidence (Use These)
  • Magnesium glycinate — 300–400mg 30 min before bed. RCT evidence for reducing sleep onset time and improving subjective sleep quality. Glycinate form has best GI tolerance.
  • Temperature — Core body temp needs to drop 1–2°C to initiate sleep. Room at 18–20°C is optimal. Cold shower 1–2 hours before bed works; ice bath too close disrupts it.
  • Light timing — Morning sunlight (10–30 min) sets circadian phase. Zero bright light after 10pm. Blue-light blocking glasses show effect in RCTs when worn 2+ hours pre-sleep.
Weaker Evidence (Optional)
  • Apigenin — 50mg (found in chamomile). Mechanistic plausibility via GABA-A receptor binding; no robust RCTs in healthy adults. [Mechanistic]
  • L-Theanine — 100–200mg. Reduces sleep latency in some trials; effects modest. Works better for stress-driven insomnia than structural sleep issues. [RCT, mixed]
  • Melatonin — Effective for circadian phase-shifting (jet lag, shift work) but NOT for sleep quality in non-disrupted sleepers. Most people use far too much — 0.5mg is sufficient; 5–10mg is pharmacological.
🔬 In Plain English — GABA-A Receptor

GABA-A receptors are the brain's "calm down" switches — when activated, they reduce neural excitability and promote relaxation and sleep. Benzodiazepines work by flooding these receptors. Apigenin and alcohol also activate them, which is why both can make you drowsy — though alcohol is notorious for degrading sleep quality in the second half of the night.

The 2026 trend worth noting: A growing body of practitioners is pushing back against obsessive wearable tracking (Oura, Whoop sleep scores). University of Maryland research shows daytime function — how alert, focused, and emotionally regulated you feel — is a better treatment outcome metric than overnight PSG-derived scores. Stop chasing the perfect HRV number and ask how you feel at 11am.

🔬 In Plain English — PSG (Polysomnography)

PSG is the gold-standard overnight sleep study done in a clinical lab — sensors measure brain waves, eye movement, muscle activity, and oxygen levels to precisely score sleep stages. Consumer wearables estimate these stages using heart rate and movement; they're useful for trends but not as accurate as clinical PSG, especially for deep sleep quantification.

⚡ Implication

If you're training 6 days/week (PPL), your sleep benefit is already baked in — the lever to pull is finishing evening sessions before 8pm, not adding supplements.

03 Nutrition & Fuel
Stanford Breaks the Protein Myth Machine — New RDA, Same Old Evidence
Expert Opinion Meta Stanford Medicine, March 2026 · Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2025–2030

The new Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2025–2030 raised the protein RDA from 0.8g/kg to 1.2–1.6g/kg of body weight. For a 80kg male this means roughly 96–128g of protein daily. The headline feels like a win for the high-protein crowd. Then Stanford's Christopher Gardner, PhD dropped some cold water: "Were we really off by that much? What new evidence became available... There really isn't any." The updated number reflects a methodological recalculation more than a body of new evidence.

🔬 In Plain English — RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance)

The RDA is the daily intake level estimated to meet the nutritional needs of 97–98% of healthy people — it's a population minimum, not an optimal target. For protein, the old 0.8g/kg was the minimum to prevent deficiency, not the amount to optimise muscle growth or longevity. The new 1.2–1.6g/kg range better reflects the needs of active adults.

What the actual research supports for a male doing PPL resistance training: 1.6–2.2g/kg/day (this range is consistent across multiple meta-analyses and is what most serious practitioners already use). The new guidelines are behind where the sports nutrition literature has been for a decade.

The other key Stanford finding: The anabolic window — the window after training when you need to consume protein — lasts approximately 24 hours, not the 30–90 minute window that gym culture still insists on. Post-workout timing matters some, but total daily protein is overwhelmingly more important than timing precision.

📋 Quick Reference — Protein Targets for 80kg Male (PPL Training)
  • Daily target: 130–175g total protein (1.6–2.2g/kg)
  • Per meal: Aim for 40–50g per sitting — research suggests ~40g is near the ceiling for acute muscle protein synthesis in trained individuals
  • Best sources: Chicken breast (31g/100g cooked), Greek yogurt (10g/100g), eggs (6g each), whey protein (25g/scoop), canned tuna (25g/100g), cottage cheese (11g/100g)
  • Meal idea: 200g chicken + 250g Greek yogurt + 2 eggs across 3 meals = ~140g protein, ~1,600 calories
⚡ Creatine Does NOT Reduce Inflammation — New Meta-Analysis Closes the Debate
Meta Frontiers in Immunology, 2026

A new systematic review and meta-analysis in Frontiers in Immunology (2026) examined 8 RCTs and found that creatine supplementation does not significantly reduce CRP or IL-6 — the two primary inflammatory biomarkers. The effect sizes were essentially zero (SMD = -0.11 for CRP; SMD = -0.06 for IL-6). Evidence certainty was rated moderate by GRADE standards.

🔬 In Plain English — CRP and IL-6

CRP (C-Reactive Protein) and IL-6 (Interleukin-6) are proteins your liver and immune cells release when inflammation is present. They're used as blood markers of systemic inflammation — elevated levels are linked to heart disease, metabolic syndrome, and accelerated aging. Lowering them is a common target in longevity interventions.

🔬 In Plain English — SMD (Standardised Mean Difference)

SMD is a way of expressing how large an effect is across studies with different measurement scales. An SMD near 0 means no meaningful difference between groups. The values here (-0.11 and -0.06) are negligible — essentially noise. For reference, an SMD of 0.5 is considered a moderate effect; 0.8 is large.

🔬 In Plain English — GRADE Framework

GRADE is the standard system researchers use to rate how confident they are in a body of evidence. "Moderate" certainty means further research could change the estimate, but the current direction is reasonably reliable. It's not as strong as "high" certainty (which requires multiple large RCTs showing consistent results), but it's much stronger than "low" or "very low."

The practical read: take creatine for strength, power output, and muscle mass — the evidence for those outcomes is unambiguous. Don't take it expecting an anti-inflammatory benefit. The cognitive benefit claims (separate from this study) still have a reasonable RCT base and are worth monitoring, especially the unpublished data suggesting 10g/day in older adults improves both cognition and body composition.

⚡ Implication

Hit 1.6–2.2g/kg protein daily, take creatine for strength and cognition — not inflammation — and stop worrying about the post-workout timing window unless your total daily intake is already dialled.

04 Protocols
BPC-157: The Peptide With 30 Years of Animal Data and Zero Human Trials
Mechanistic Expert Opinion STAT News, Feb/April 2026 · USADA · FDA

BPC-157 (Body Protective Compound-157) has become the most-hyped peptide in gym culture and biohacking podcasts — promising accelerated tendon and ligament healing, gut repair, and nervous system protection. STAT News published a comprehensive review this year that deserves to be required reading for anyone considering it. The verdict: the entire evidence base comes from a single Croatian research group led by Predrag Sikiric, spanning 100+ animal and in-vitro papers over 30 years. Zero completed human clinical trials. Not one.

🔬 In Plain English — BPC-157

BPC-157 is a synthetic peptide (a short chain of amino acids) derived from a protein found in gastric juice. In rodent studies, it promotes blood vessel formation and reduces inflammation, with apparent benefits for healing tendons, ligaments, muscles, and gut tissue. "Synthetic" here means it doesn't occur naturally in this form — it was engineered in a lab based on a naturally occurring sequence.

⚠ Hype Watch

Dr. Flynn McGuire (University of Utah Health) states BPC-157 "should not be used by humans" until well-designed human studies are completed: "The amount of hype to evidence is just so skewed, it's crazy." The FDA banned compounding pharmacies from supplying BPC-157 in 2023 and classifies it as Category 2 — meaning significant safety concerns in compounding. RFK Jr. has signalled interest in loosening restrictions on experimental peptides, which could shift the regulatory landscape — but loosening regulations is not the same as generating safety evidence.

Australian legal status: BPC-157 is not approved by the TGA for any indication. It exists in a grey market — technically illegal to sell as a therapeutic product without TGA approval, but sold as "research chemical." If you're a competitive athlete, WADA prohibits it under S0 Unapproved Substances (banned from sport regardless of TGA status).

Stated view: The mechanism is plausible and the animal data is interesting — but "interesting animal data" describes thousands of compounds that fail in humans. The risk here isn't dramatic toxicity (no clear harm signal in animals), it's that you're essentially running an n=1 human trial with no pharmacokinetic data, no established human dosing, and no understanding of long-term effects. Wait for human data. Preferably a phase II trial, not a single case report from a wellness clinic.

Alternatives with actual human evidence for injury recovery: Collagen + vitamin C peri-workout (RCT evidence for tendon health), creatine (RCT evidence for connective tissue force production), and progressive mechanical loading (the gold standard for tendon and ligament adaptation).

⚡ Implication

Do not add BPC-157 to your stack this cycle — no human trial data means no informed consent, and the regulatory risk in Australia is real; revisit if a human phase II trial publishes in the next 12 months.

05 Movement & Load
ACSM 2026 Resistance Training Guidelines: First Update in 17 Years — Here's What Changed
Meta American College of Sports Medicine, April 2026 — 137 systematic reviews, 30,000+ participants

The American College of Sports Medicine just published their landmark updated Position Stand on resistance training — the first major revision since 2009, synthesising 137 systematic reviews across over 30,000 participants. This isn't a fringe paper or a single researcher's opinion. This is the closest thing to scientific consensus that resistance training has. And it's both reassuring and humbling: most of the noise in programming discussion simply doesn't matter.

🔬 In Plain English — 1RM (One-Rep Maximum)

Your 1RM is the maximum weight you can lift for exactly one repetition of a given exercise. It's used as a reference point for programming — so "80% 1RM" means using a weight equal to 80% of your max. If you can bench press 100kg for 1 rep, 80% would be 80kg for multiple reps. You don't need to actually test your max — calculators estimate it from submaximal sets.

✅ What to Change (or Confirm)
  • Volume check: ~10 sets/muscle/week is the evidence-based hypertrophy target. If you're running PPL with 3–4 working sets per exercise × 2–3 exercises per muscle group per session, you're likely in range — audit it.
  • Frequency: All major muscle groups ≥2× per week. PPL at 6 days already delivers this.
  • Load for hypertrophy: Any load from ~30% to 85% 1RM works if sets are taken close to failure. Stop obsessing over exact weight if effort is high.
  • Progressive overload: The only mandatory variable — weight, reps, or sets must increase over time. Everything else is secondary.
🚫 What NOT to Change (Stop Overthinking)
  • Advanced techniques: Drop sets, blood flow restriction, supersets — the meta shows small, non-significant hypertrophy benefit vs standard multiple sets. Don't rebuild your program around them.
  • Training to failure: Not consistently superior to stopping 1–2 reps short, and the fatigue cost is higher. Periodise failure training rather than doing it every set.
  • Exercise complexity: Elastic bands, bodyweight, cables, machines — all produce equivalent hypertrophy to free weights. The limiting factor is progressive overload, not equipment.
  • Periodization schemes: Linear, undulating, block — no clear winner for typical healthy adults. Pick one and be consistent.
🔬 In Plain English — Blood Flow Restriction (BFR) Training

BFR training involves wrapping a cuff around a limb to partially restrict blood flow, then training with light weights. It tricks the muscle into a high-metabolic-stress state similar to heavy training while using much lighter loads (20–30% 1RM). It's useful for injury rehab or when you can't load heavy — but the new guidelines confirm it's not superior to standard training for healthy adults who can train normally.

The dose-response meta-regression published alongside the ACSM guidelines confirms something Dr. Mike Israetel and Brad Schoenfeld have said for years: there are strongly diminishing returns beyond ~10–20 sets per muscle group per week, particularly for strength (even more pronounced than for hypertrophy). If you're pushing 20+ sets per muscle group and wondering why you're not growing faster, the answer is almost certainly recovery, not volume.

Zone 2 Calibration: Your Target Zone Is Probably Wrong
Observational PMC, 2026 · Cooper Center Longitudinal Study

A 2026 paper analysed Zone 2 intensity markers across individuals and found coefficients of variation from 6% to 29% depending on which marker you use to set the zone. Fixed-percentage HRmax targets (e.g., "65–75% of max heart rate") showed the widest individual differences and the poorest alignment with actual physiological Zone 2. Ventilatory Threshold 1 and maximal fat oxidation were the most reliable anchors.

🔬 In Plain English — Zone 2 / Ventilatory Threshold 1 (VT1)

Zone 2 is a low-intensity cardio intensity where your body primarily burns fat for fuel and your mitochondria (the energy generators in your cells) are maximally stimulated to adapt and grow. It's the intensity at which you can hold a conversation but it feels slightly effortful. VT1 (Ventilatory Threshold 1) is the precise physiological point where your breathing starts to increase — it's the most accurate way to define where Zone 2 ends. A heart rate monitor percentage is a rough approximation; a proper threshold test is much more precise.

The Cooper Center Longitudinal Study (122,000+ participants) provides the why: moving from the bottom 25% of cardiorespiratory fitness to the median reduces all-cause mortality by roughly 50%. VO2max is one of the strongest longevity predictors we have. If you're doing Zone 2 without a calibrated threshold, you may be training too easy (below Zone 2, minimal mitochondrial stimulus) or too hard (drifting into Zone 3, accumulating fatigue without the aerobic adaptation).

🔬 In Plain English — VO2max

VO2max is the maximum rate at which your body can consume oxygen during intense exercise — it's the ceiling of your aerobic engine. Higher VO2max means more efficient oxygen delivery, better endurance, and (critically) lower all-cause mortality. It's trainable at any age, and Zone 2 training is the primary way to build the aerobic base that underpins it.

Action: If you don't have a lactate or ventilatory threshold test, the "talk test" is your best proxy — Zone 2 is where you can speak full sentences comfortably but couldn't sing. Your Whoop or Garmin HR zones are reasonable approximations until you get a proper test, but treat them as estimates, not truth.

⚡ Implication

Audit your PPL volume against the 10 sets/muscle/week ACSM target this week, and add 2× weekly Zone 2 sessions (30–45 min) using talk-test calibration if you're not already doing cardio.
