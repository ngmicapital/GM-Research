The Lost World Beneath the North Sea: How Ancient DNA Is Finally Mapping a Drowned Continent

Between roughly 18,000 and 7,000 years ago, a landmass the size of the United Kingdom connected Britain to mainland Europe. It had forests, rivers, megafauna, and people. Then the sea swallowed it. In 2026, scientists pulled its genetic archive from sediment cores drilled from the ocean floor.

Doggerland was a now-submerged landmass beneath the North Sea that connected Britain to continental Europe during the last ice age. A 2026 study published in PNAS used sedimentary ancient DNA extracted from 252 samples across 41 marine cores to show that Doggerland hosted temperate oak, elm, and hazel forests more than 16,000 years ago — thousands of years earlier than previously believed — along with boars, deer, bears, beavers, and aurochs. The findings suggest Doggerland was a rich ecological heartland for Mesolithic hunter-gatherers, which may explain why their traces are so sparse in Britain: they were living somewhere else, in a homeland that no longer exists.

TL;DR

Doggerland was a landmass roughly the size of the United Kingdom that connected Britain to mainland Europe during and after the last ice age. As global temperatures rose and ice sheets melted, sea levels climbed and Doggerland drowned — not all at once, but over roughly 11,000 years of gradual flooding punctuated by catastrophic events, including a massive tsunami around 8,150 years ago. A study published in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in early 2026, led by researchers at the University of Warwick, used sedimentary ancient DNA recovered from North Sea marine cores to reconstruct what Doggerland’s ecology actually looked like. The results were unexpected: temperate forests were present more than 16,000 years ago, far earlier than previous models suggested. The landscape hosted oak, elm, hazel, and at least one tree genus thought to have vanished from the region 400,000 years ago. Brown bears, wild boars, red deer, beavers, and aurochs left their genetic signatures in the same sediments. The researchers believe this rich, forested land was a central habitat for Mesolithic hunter-gatherers — which may explain why the archaeological record of early humans in Britain is so thin. The people were elsewhere, in a homeland that now lies under 40 metres of seawater.

Section 01

The Story

The first hint that something substantial once existed beneath the North Sea came not from science but from fishing. In the late nineteenth century, trawlermen working the shallow banks between Britain and the Netherlands began hauling up strange catches: mammoth teeth, ancient antlers, and fragments of peat that smelled of land. They called it “moorlog” — a dark, fibrous material that clearly had nothing to do with the sea floor and everything to do with a vanished landscape. For decades these finds accumulated in museums and private collections with no coherent explanation.

The geologist Clement Reid proposed in 1913 that the southern North Sea had once been dry land, a territory he called “Northsealand.” The idea was not widely accepted. The term Doggerland was coined in the 1990s by the archaeologist Bryony Coles, who named the region after Dogger Bank — the submerged sandbar that is Doggerland’s highest remaining point, just 15 to 36 metres below the modern sea surface. Dogger Bank itself was still an island as recently as 7,000 years ago. The North Sea herring industry would later give the bank its name: doggers were a type of medieval Dutch fishing vessel that worked these waters.

The systematic scientific investigation of Doggerland began in earnest in the 1990s and 2000s, initially using seismic survey data collected by the oil and gas industry. The petroleum companies had no interest in lost continents, but their data — acoustic profiles of the North Sea bed accumulated over decades of hydrocarbon exploration — turned out to reveal the buried topography of Doggerland in extraordinary detail. Ancient river channels, lake basins, and coastal terraces emerged from the noise. Researchers at Birmingham and Bradford began interpreting this data to produce the first palaeographic maps: charts of what the North Sea floor would have looked like when it was dry.

The trawlermen had been right all along. Beneath forty metres of cold grey seawater lay the drowned remains of a landscape that, at its peak, was home to more people than Britain or Ireland.

The 2026 PNAS study, led by geographer and ancient-DNA specialist Laura Parducci and her colleagues at the University of Warwick, took the investigation into a new phase. Rather than trying to infer Doggerland’s ecology from pollen records, physical artefacts, or topographic reconstruction alone, they used sedimentary ancient DNA — sedaDNA — a relatively new technique that extracts genetic material directly from the sediment layers preserved in marine cores. The cores were drilled along the course of a reconstructed prehistoric river system in the southern North Sea. From 252 samples across 41 cores, they recovered genetic evidence stretching from roughly 16,000 years ago through the landscape’s final submergence, producing the most detailed ecological portrait of Doggerland yet achieved.

Section 02

The Science

Sedimentary ancient DNA is a technique that emerged from the intersection of palaeogenomics and environmental science in the 2010s. The principle is straightforward in concept and technically demanding in practice. When organisms die, they shed genetic material — cells, hair, pollen, excrement, decaying tissue — that settles into the sediments beneath lakes, rivers, and eventually sea floors. Under the right conditions of cold temperature, low oxygen, and stable sediment chemistry, fragments of that DNA can survive for tens of thousands of years. By extracting and sequencing these fragments from dated sediment layers, researchers can reconstruct which species were present in a landscape at any given time, without needing to find a physical bone, seed, or tool.

What the Cores Revealed

The 2026 Doggerland study recovered DNA from temperate trees including oak (Quercus), elm (Ulmus), and hazel (Corylus) in sediment layers dated to more than 16,000 years ago — the height of the last glacial maximum’s retreat. More strikingly, they detected DNA from a tree genus (not publicly identified pending further verification) that mainstream palaeoecology believed had been absent from the region for approximately 400,000 years. Animal DNA recovered from the same cores included brown bear (Ursus arctos), wild boar (Sus scrofa), red deer (Cervus elaphus), Eurasian beaver (Castor fiber), and aurochs (Bos primigenius) — the now-extinct wild ancestor of all domestic cattle.

The ecological picture that emerges is not what earlier models predicted. Prior reconstructions, based primarily on pollen records from peat deposits recovered by North Sea trawlers, suggested that Doggerland was a relatively open, shrubby landscape — tundra-like conditions with scattered trees. The sedaDNA evidence indicates something considerably more productive: a mosaic of temperate woodland, river margins, and open glades supporting a diverse megafauna community. The temperate tree species detected require a warmer, wetter climate than previous reconstructions allowed for in the early post-glacial period. The implication is that parts of Doggerland were a refugium — a pocket of warmer microclimate sheltered from the broader cold by the insulating effect of the shallow North Sea basin — allowing species to persist and expand earlier than the surrounding region could support them.

The timeline of Doggerland’s inundation, as reconstructed from the cores, involved multiple stages. The initial retreat of the ice sheets beginning around 18,000 years ago raised sea levels gradually, shrinking Doggerland from its maximum extent — which included much of what is now the southern North Sea floor, connecting Britain to the Netherlands, Denmark, and Germany — to progressively smaller areas over millennia. Then, approximately 8,150 years ago, the Storegga slide occurred. An enormous section of the Norwegian continental shelf, an area approximately the size of Iceland, catastrophically collapsed into the deep ocean in a matter of minutes. The resulting tsunami swept across the North Sea at speeds of up to 700 kilometres per hour, inundating coastal Doggerland with waves estimated at 10 to 25 metres in height. The Storegga event did not destroy Doggerland entirely — Dogger Bank and some adjacent areas remained above water for another thousand years — but it devastated the coastal populations and accelerated the landscape’s final disappearance.

The Storegga Slide in Numbers

The Storegga submarine landslide is one of the largest known landslides in Earth’s history. It involved approximately 3,500 cubic kilometres of sediment — equivalent to the volume of Iceland — collapsing off the Norwegian shelf. The resulting tsunami reached heights of up to 25 metres on the Norwegian coast, around 10–20 metres on the Scottish coast, and an estimated 3–6 metres across much of Doggerland’s coastal lowlands. Evidence of the event is preserved in sand and gravel tsunami deposits found in coastal peat bogs across Scotland, Norway, and the Faroe Islands.

Section 03

The Connections

The missing Mesolithic problem. The early Mesolithic period in Britain — roughly 12,000 to 8,000 years ago — is archaeologically impoverished. The number of confirmed sites is small, the artefact record is thin, and human skeletal remains from this period are extraordinarily rare. This has puzzled archaeologists for decades. The conventional explanation is preservation bias: Mesolithic sites are often near rivers and coasts, environments where organic materials decay rapidly and later flooding or erosion destroys what was left. The Doggerland hypothesis adds a different dimension. If the richest, most productive environments of early post-glacial north-western Europe were in Doggerland — the temperate river-laced lowlands between Britain and the continent — then the primary settlement zone for those millennia is now underwater. The sparse British record is sparse precisely because Britain was not where most of the people were. They were on the lost continent to the east.

A genetic bottleneck? The Storegga tsunami and the final submergence of Doggerland around 7,000–6,000 years ago may have caused a significant demographic collapse among the hunter-gatherer populations of north-western Europe. Some researchers have proposed that this catastrophe — the destruction of a major heartland of Mesolithic settlement — weakened the native hunter-gatherer population at roughly the same time that farming communities were beginning to expand from central Europe and Anatolia into northern and western Europe. The arrival of the Neolithic — agriculture, pottery, domesticated animals, permanent settlement — in Britain around 6,000 years ago was not a gradual transition but appears, from ancient DNA evidence of later British populations, to have involved a dramatic population replacement. Whether Doggerland’s destruction contributed to that vulnerability is debated but impossible to dismiss.

Britain was not isolated from Europe by the Channel and the North Sea. It was connected to Europe by Doggerland. The separation is recent history. The connection is deep time.

The 400,000-year-old tree. One of the most provocative findings in the 2026 PNAS study is the detection of DNA from a tree genus believed absent from the region since the penultimate glacial period, some 400,000 years ago. The researchers are being cautious about the identification — ancient DNA from environmental samples can be contaminated, misidentified, or represent long-distance pollen dispersal rather than local presence — but if confirmed, it would suggest that Doggerland functioned as a glacial refugium for plant species over multiple glacial-interglacial cycles, maintaining populations through repeated ice ages in its relatively sheltered basin. This would make Doggerland not just a post-glacial hunting ground but a long-term evolutionary reservoir, a place where species survived extinction elsewhere.

The land that shaped the language. Some historical linguists have proposed that proto-languages ancestral to later Indo-European branches were spread through or around Doggerland during the Mesolithic, carried by the same mobile hunter-gatherer populations whose ecology the 2026 study illuminates. The proposal is speculative and contested, but it gestures at a larger point: the cultural, genetic, and linguistic foundations of north-western European civilisation were partly laid in a landscape that no longer exists and cannot be excavated by conventional means. The genetic and ecological data pulled from North Sea sediment cores may be the only record we will ever have of what happened there.

Further Down the Hole

📖
Bryony Coles — Doggerland: A Speculative Survey (1998)
The paper that named and defined the concept of Doggerland as an archaeological research object. Coles coined the term, synthesised the available physical evidence, and laid out the research programme that subsequent investigators have been executing ever since. Technically a journal article in the Proceedings of the Prehistoric Society rather than a book, but foundational reading.
🧬
Parducci et al. — PNAS (2026)
The primary source for this rabbit hole: “Early colonization before inundation consistent with northern glacial refugia in Southern Doggerland revealed by sedimentary ancient DNA.” The paper is open access at PNAS. The supplementary materials include detailed species lists and core location data that reward careful reading.
🌊
Brian Fagan — The Long Summer (2004)
Fagan’s account of how the end of the last ice age and the long warm period that followed transformed human societies across the Northern Hemisphere. Doggerland appears as one of several case studies in how rising seas remade human geographies. The broader climate narrative provides essential context for understanding why Doggerland disappeared when and how it did.
🌍
Vincent Gaffney et al. — Europe’s Lost World (2009)
The first book-length treatment of Doggerland, produced by the team at the University of Birmingham that pioneered the use of oil-industry seismic data to map the submerged landscape. Lavishly illustrated with reconstructed maps and site visualisations. Somewhat dated by subsequent discoveries but remains the best single introduction to the palaeotopography of the region.
