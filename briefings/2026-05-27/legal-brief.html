Headline U.S. Court of Appeals (9th Cir.) [Primary] Prediction Markets · Federal Preemption
🟢 High — Orders issued, mandates active

Ninth Circuit Denies Kalshi And Polymarket Stays Pending Appeal In Nevada And Washington Cases — Orders Federal-Question Jurisdiction Cannot Rest On CEA Preemption Defense, State Gambling Enforcement Now Proceeds Against Both Platforms In State Court And Crystallises 3rd-Circuit / 9th-Circuit Split That Tees Up Supreme Court Review

"Principles of federalism and comity tip the balance of hardships and public interest in favor of allowing [the states to enforce their laws in state court]… The CEA preemption defense is an affirmative defense, which cannot by itself give rise to federal question jurisdiction." — Ninth Circuit panel (Nelson, Bade, Lee JJ.), orders in State of Nevada v. KalshiEX (No. 26-1304), Nevada v. Blockratize (No. 26-1343), Washington v. KalshiEX (No. 26-3106), 21 May 2026

A three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit on 21 May denied Kalshi's and Polymarket's motions for stays pending appeal of district-court remand orders in Nevada and Washington enforcement suits. The orders unwind the platforms' federalisation strategy: state attorneys general can now litigate the legality of event contracts on sports, elections and other gambling-adjacent outcomes in state court, where their unlicensed-gambling theory is procedurally far stronger than in federal forums.

The panel — Judges Ryan Nelson, Bridget Bade and Kenneth Lee, all Trump first-term appointees — rejected the platforms' argument that CFTC's exclusive jurisdiction over swaps and futures under §2(a)(1) of the Commodity Exchange Act creates federal-question jurisdiction sufficient to remove state actions. The court adopted the orthodox Caterpillar v. Williams rule: a federal defense, even preemption, does not appear on the face of the well-pleaded complaint.

The decisions create a hard circuit split with the Third Circuit, which in Kalshi v. Flaherty (3d Cir. Jan. 2026) affirmed a preliminary injunction against New Jersey gaming regulators on CEA-preemption grounds. With the same operators now barred from removal in two western states but federally protected in the Mid-Atlantic, Supreme Court review is the only path to a unified answer — likely cert briefing in the fall 2026 term.

📎 Historical Precedent: Similar Caterpillar-rule jurisdictional rejection in Beneficial Nat'l Bank v. Anderson (2003) — preemption defense alone cannot remove state-court claims absent complete-preemption doctrine, which courts have rarely extended outside ERISA, LMRA §301, and National Bank Act usury.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Davis Polk's 22 May client alert on the orders frames the split as "structurally unstable" — operators will simultaneously be enjoined from offering products in NJ (under preemption) and prosecuted for the identical products in NV/WA (no preemption removal). The Brief's read: The Ninth Circuit panel correctly applied black-letter Caterpillar doctrine; the Third Circuit's federalisation theory in Flaherty was always the outlier. SCOTUS will likely grant cert and resolve in favour of the Ninth — at which point the entire premise of off-shore-style listing on a CFTC-registered DCM as a state-gambling-law immunity gets vaporised. Kalshi and Polymarket are now litigating a 50-state patchwork from a position of weakness, with state AGs holding both the procedural and the merits hand.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Stanford Law's Joseph Grundfest (writing in Law360, 23 May) argues the split is overstated — the Third Circuit case was a preliminary-injunction stage merits ruling, while the Ninth is a jurisdictional remand. SCOTUS rarely takes a circuit split where the two cases sit on different procedural rails. The Brief's read: Plausible but underweights the fact that the merits question (does the CEA preempt state gambling law as applied to event contracts) is identical in both — and a unified jurisdictional rule is the necessary predicate to consistent merits adjudication. SCOTUS will treat the split as ripe.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Kalshi's $11bn TAM thesis (Brookings, Jan. 2026) rested on national federal regulation as the moat — that moat is breached. Expect Polymarket's US subsidiary QCX LLC to slow its planned multi-leg parlay launches (per its 20 May CFTC self-certification) and accelerate state-AFCA-style licensing strategy in friendly states. The Brief's read: The real losers are venture-staged copycats — Drift's prediction product, Manifold, and the half-dozen pre-launch firms that priced regulatory clarity into their seed rounds. Listed comparables (FanDuel, DraftKings) catch a bid: every $1bn that doesn't flow to event contracts is $1bn fighting for licensed-sportsbook attention.

"This Minnesota law turns lawful operators and participants in prediction markets into felons overnight. Minnesota farmers have relied on critical hedging products on weather and crop-related events for decades to mitigate their risks." — Michael S. Selig, CFTC Chairman, press release 9233-26, 19 May 2026

The CFTC filed suit in federal district court on 19 May, one day after Governor Tim Walz signed a public-safety omnibus making it a felony to operate, host or advertise prediction markets on sports, elections, assassinations, weather and "mention markets" starting 1 August 2026. The complaint seeks a preliminary injunction grounded in the Supremacy Clause and the CEA's §2(a)(1) exclusive-jurisdiction grant.

Minnesota AG Keith Ellison (D) confirmed his office will defend the law, citing prediction-market harm to consumers. Notably, Walz's signing of a flat-out ban — versus the licensing-and-prohibition models the other five sued states deployed — gives Selig's complaint its cleanest Supremacy Clause vehicle yet.

📎 Historical Precedent: Closest analog is Murphy v. NCAA (2018) — but that case cut against federal preemption of state sports-gambling choices (PASPA), not in favour. Selig's bet is that CEA exclusive jurisdiction is structurally distinct because it explicitly displaces state futures/swaps regulation.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Sidley's 22 May commodities alert calls the suit "the cleanest CEA-preemption test case since Dunn v. CFTC" because Minnesota's flat-felony approach maps directly onto state regulation of CEA-defined "contracts of sale of a commodity for future delivery." Davis Polk's Pillar & Sword note observes that the same Ninth Circuit doctrine that just sank Kalshi's removal play (Story #1) actually helps the CFTC here — because the federal government brings its preemption claim affirmatively, no Caterpillar-rule obstacle attaches. The Brief's read: The CFTC will likely win the PI; the harder fight is the merits when full briefing arrives. If Minnesota's "criminal-code carve-out" theory survives, expect a wave of copycat state criminal-code bans designed to evade civil-licensing-statute preemption arguments.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Coin Center's Peter Van Valkenburgh, in a 21 May Substack note, argues the suit is overreach — "the CFTC has no exclusive jurisdiction over event contracts on assassinations, and Minnesota's law happens to also touch lawful weather hedges." The Brief's read: Probably right on the scope critique, but a court is unlikely to bisect the statute mid-PI — Minnesota will either get a clean injunction or none at all. Severability fights wait for the merits.

The Brief's read: Watch Pennsylvania, Michigan, Virginia state legislatures — three swing-state legislatures with pending bills modelled on Minnesota's approach. The PI ruling (expected mid-July) will determine whether they go forward this session or wait.

"Our businesses and citizens want two things from AI rules. They want to be able to innovate and feel safe. Today's agreement does both. With simpler and innovation-friendly rules, we make it easier to innovate without lowering the bar on safety." — Henna Virkkunen, EU Executive Vice-President for Tech Sovereignty, Security and Democracy, Consilium press release, 7 May 2026

The European Parliament and Council struck a provisional political agreement on the Digital Omnibus on AI, materially amending the 2024 AI Act ahead of its 2 August 2026 high-risk implementation cliff. The deal moves stand-alone Annex-III high-risk systems to 2 December 2027 and product-embedded Annex-I high-risk systems to 2 August 2028; extends SME-style technical-documentation relief to small mid-caps up to 500 employees; and clarifies the AI Act's interplay with existing EU product-safety regimes (machinery, medical devices) to prevent double-regulation.

A second headline change is a new fully prohibited use-case — AI systems whose primary purpose is generating non-consensual sexual or intimate imagery, including CSAM — applicable from 2 December 2026. Watermarking obligations also slip from August to 2 December 2026.

📎 Historical Precedent: The closest analog is the GDPR's 2019 enforcement-deferral negotiation — but unlike GDPR, the AI Act's deferral is now codified at the legislative level rather than left to national DPA discretion, which gives industry clearer planning horizons.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Hogan Lovells' 12 May client alert summarises the deal as "Commission climbdown on timing, EU spine intact on scope." Lewis Silkin's UK-EU comparative note observes the small-mid-cap relief is structurally significant because it captures most Series-B-and-up AI scale-ups headquartered or operating in the EU. The Brief's read: The political agreement is meaningfully better for industry than the August deadline scenario, but worse than the deep deregulatory rollback some lobbyists wanted. The new intimate-imagery prohibition is the load-bearing political concession — it lets Council Members go home with a "tougher on harm, lighter on compliance" message. Compliance leads should treat the Dec 2027 / Aug 2028 dates as firm and use the window to actually re-architect Annex-III pipelines, not just defer the work.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The Brief's read: Legitimate critique, but the alternative was operationally worse — having two parallel regimes with overlapping but non-identical requirements. The trade-off favours certainty over coverage, which is normally the right call.

🏗️ Industry Implications

OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Mistral and the European GPAI cohort gain a 16-month implementation runway for high-risk applications; embedded-AI players (Siemens, Bosch, ABB, John Deere Europe) get 24 months. The Brief's read: Listed beneficiaries include SAP, ASML's customers, and Stellantis (vehicle-AI compliance window opens).
