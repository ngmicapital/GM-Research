Alpha — Funding + OI + Price Decoder

Tuesday · 5 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #13 11-min read
TL;DR

The Funding + OI + Price decoder is the three-variable framework for reading perpetual futures positioning — funding tells you who is paying to hold, open interest tells you whether positions are being added or unwound, price tells you the direction of pressure. Read in combination they collapse the noise of perp markets into four legible regimes — new longs, new shorts, long flush, short squeeze — and the regime, not the price level, is what tells you whether a move has fuel or is running on empty.

§01
What It Is
Three variables · one positioning signal
A tape decoder that reads three derivatives variables together — funding rate, open interest, and price — to classify what is actually happening in perp positioning.

Funding rate, open interest, and price are the three native data fields of any crypto perpetual futures market. Read in isolation each is ambiguous: a high funding rate could be a healthy uptrend or a crowded long that is about to fold. A rising OI could mean conviction or capitulation. Price could be marking up because of real demand or because thin liquidity is being shoved by leverage. The decoder works by reading them in combination — three variables, eight possible sign-combinations, and a small set of regimes that account for the four that actually matter.

The mechanic this measures is positioning, not opinion. Funding is the periodic payment that anchors the perpetual price to spot — when it is positive, longs are paying shorts to keep the perp tracking spot; when negative, shorts are paying longs. Open interest is the total notional of contracts open and unsettled — it goes up only when a new long meets a new short, and down only when a long and short close against each other. Price is the result. The decoder asks: when price moves, who is paying for the privilege, and is the position pool growing or shrinking? That single question separates new positioning from forced unwinds.

Why this matters at the chart level: a rally on rising OI with rising funding is very different from a rally on falling OI with funding flipping from negative to positive. The first is new longs piling in; the second is shorts being squeezed out. Both look identical on a candlestick. But the first has continuation fuel only if real demand is behind it, while the second is mechanically self-limiting — once the trapped shorts are flushed, there is no one left to buy. The decoder lets you tell the two apart in real time, before the move resolves.

§02
Mechanics
Funding formula · OI accounting · the four regimes
Funding = premium index + interest component, paid every 4–8 hours; OI changes only when a long-short pair opens or closes; the decoder reads the joint state.

Funding rate is set per exchange but the basic formula is universal: a premium component (how much the perp is trading above or below spot) plus a small interest-rate component (a fixed bias, usually 0.01% / 8h on Binance for BTC and ETH). When perp trades above spot, premium is positive, funding is positive, longs pay shorts. The payment is multiplied across every open notional dollar at the funding timestamp — which is why crowded markets bleed real money each cycle.

Funding rate (simplified): F = clamp(premium_index + clamp(interest − premium, ±0.05%), −0.75%, +0.75%) Per-cycle payment to your account: payment = F × position_notional × side side = +1 if long (you pay if F>0, you receive if F<0) side = −1 if short Open Interest accounting: ΔOI = +1 contract when a new long opens against a new short ΔOI = −1 contract when an existing long and existing short close against each other ΔOI = 0 when an existing position transfers between two new participants Daily funding cost (Binance, 8h cycle): daily_cost ≈ F × 3 × notional example: F = +0.05% per 8h, 1 BTC long at $76k → ~$114/day in funding
Funding is calibrated per exchange. Binance and Bybit use 8-hour cycles for BTC/ETH. dYdX uses 1-hour cycles. Hyperliquid uses 1-hour cycles with separate interest assumptions. Cross-exchange funding spreads (e.g. Binance perp vs OKX perp) are themselves a tradeable signal — when one venue's funding diverges sharply from the rest, positioning is concentrating there.

The OI accounting rule is the part most retail traders miss. OI does not measure who is winning; it measures how much position is open. Rising OI means new contracts are being created — for every new long, a new short stepped in to take the other side. Falling OI means contracts are being closed — paired long and short positions are unwinding into each other. This is what makes "OI ↓ on a price ↑" the mechanical signature of short covering: shorts are buying back to close, longs are selling to close, net OI drops, but the marginal flow into the book is buying.

The two extreme rows are where the decoder pays for itself. Distribution is the regime where funding is screaming positive but price has stopped advancing — longs are still paying, but no new highs are being printed. The market is stalling on top of crowded leverage. The next move down does not need a catalyst; it just needs the marginal long to give up. Capitulation is the mirror: funding deeply negative, OI bleeding, but price has stopped falling. The shorts are still paying to hold, and the bottom is being built underneath them.

§04
In Practice
Reading the regime · trading the transitions
The trade is at the regime transition — when the market shifts from new shorts to short squeeze, or from new longs to distribution, the edge is the flip itself, not the regime in steady state.

The decoder is not a pattern that fires entries. It is a state classifier. Most of the time the market is simply in one of the four regimes, and your job is to know which one. The trades come at the transitions — the bar where OI starts to fall while price keeps rising (new longs → short squeeze, fade), or the bar where funding flips negative while price holds (new longs → distribution risk, lean short).

1.

Read OI direction first

OI direction is the cleanest signal of the three. Rising OI = new positioning, falling OI = unwinding. Look at OI before looking at price. A move on falling OI is mechanically self-limiting; a move on rising OI has continuation potential — both look identical on a candle.

2.

Use funding to identify who is trapped

Funding tells you who is paying. Persistent positive funding into a flat or falling price means crowded longs are bleeding — they will eventually fold. Persistent negative funding into a flat or rising price means crowded shorts are bleeding — squeeze fuel is loaded. The trapped side is the one paying.

3.

Cross-check perp regime against spot CVD

A "new longs" regime that is leveraged-only (rising perp OI, flat spot CVD) is fragile — it is paper bull. A "new longs" regime confirmed by spot CVD is real. Same logic for the short side. Cross-checking with spot is what separates a sustainable trend from a wick waiting to happen.

4.

Trade the transition, not the steady state

Steady-state regimes give you bias. Transitions give you entries. The bar where new longs flips to short squeeze (OI rolls over) — that is where the fade lives. The bar where capitulation flips to new longs (funding crosses zero, OI base) — that is where the long lives.

Worked example — short-squeeze setup, BTC perp
Pre-conditionBTC −12% over 5 sessions. Funding has driven from +0.005% to −0.022% per 8h on Binance. Long/short ratio: 37% long, 63% short.
TriggerPrice reclaims a swing-high level on the 1H chart; OI flat on the bar (existing shorts not adding); funding still −0.018% (shorts still paying).
ReadCrowded short, no new short pressing — squeeze fuel intact. Reclaim invalidates the short thesis structurally.
EntryLong on retest of reclaimed level. Stop below the swing low that defined the short trap.
RiskStop ~1.8% below entry. First target = next major liquidity pool above (where the cluster of shorts get force-closed). R:R ≈ 1:3.
ExitTrail as OI drops on the way up — when OI bleeds 8–12% from the local high, the squeeze is mostly out of fuel. Take the rest off into the next obvious resistance.
Timeframe fit: 1H–4H execution, 1D bias. Funding cycles are 8h on Binance/Bybit — anything below 1H gets noisy. Above 1D the decoder still works but the entries become positional.

§07
Common Traps
Where the decoder breaks down · what to ignore
Reading any one of the three variables in isolation is the most common, most expensive mistake in perp trading.
1.
Treating high funding as a contrarian signal on its own

Funding can sit elevated for weeks during a healthy trend. The signal is funding extreme plus stalling price — if price is still making new highs while funding is high, longs are getting paid back through price appreciation faster than they pay funding. Don't fade trend just because funding is positive.

2.
Reading exchange-aggregated OI when the divergence is the point

Aggregated OI smooths out the most useful information: where positioning is concentrated. A spike on a single venue (Binance, Bybit, Hyperliquid) often precedes a venue-specific squeeze that aggregated charts will mute. Look at per-exchange OI for the leading edge.

3.
Confusing OI ↓ for closing-the-book vs forced flush

OI can drop because traders rationally take profits at a target — not because they got liquidated. Cross-check OI drops against the liquidation feed: a clean OI bleed with no liquidation prints is healthy unwinding; an OI cliff with a liquidation cluster is the flush. Different setups, different trades.

4.
Ignoring the spot side

Perpetual decoders read positioning, not demand. A flawless "new longs" regime that is unconfirmed by spot is paper bull — it sells off the moment funding gets uncomfortable. Always cross-check perp positioning against spot CVD or spot volume; the perp tape lies more often than the spot tape.

5.
Overhyped: precise funding-rate "thresholds"

You will see content claiming 0.05% / 8h is "the squeeze threshold" or 0.1% means "imminent reversal." There is no universal threshold. Funding is relative to the asset, the regime, and the cycle — what is extreme for BTC in a low-vol chop is normal for a meme perp in a mania. Use percentile context (where does this print sit versus the trailing 90 days?) not absolute cutoffs.
