TL;DR

In 1889, Otto von Bismarck invented the world's first state pension and set the retirement age at 70 — at a time when average male life expectancy at birth was barely 45 years. The arithmetic was deliberate: almost no worker would live long enough to collect a single pfennig. The pension was not welfare. It was a political weapon designed to neutralize the Social Democrats by giving German workers just enough stake in the state to stop them joining the revolution. It worked brilliantly. Then the world copied the model — without copying the cynical actuarial logic that made it sustainable. Today, every major economy runs a version of Bismarck's system. And now that people actually live into their eighties, it is failing everywhere simultaneously.

Section 01

The Story: A Chancellor, a Revolution He Feared, and a Retirement Age He Knew Nobody Would Reach

By 1880, Otto von Bismarck had already accomplished the impossible. In less than a decade he had united thirty-nine squabbling German states into a single empire, defeated Austria and France in two wars, and installed Kaiser Wilhelm I as the head of a new great power. He was 65 years old, the most feared diplomat in Europe, and he was terrified.

What terrified him was not another war. It was the Social Democratic Party — the SPD. German industrialization had produced exactly what Marx predicted: a vast proletariat working brutal hours in Ruhr Valley factories, living in slums, and growing increasingly attracted to the idea that the system should be abolished entirely. The SPD was gaining seats in the Reichstag. Bismarck had banned the party once in 1878, using emergency laws after two assassination attempts on the Kaiser. But the bans had not worked. Workers kept voting socialist. The party kept growing underground.

Bismarck needed a different approach. His reasoning, later described to the Reichstag in frank terms, was simple: if the state gave workers just enough — health insurance, accident protection, an old-age payment — they would have too much to lose by joining a revolution. "Whoever has a pension for old age," he told the parliament, "is far more contented and far easier to handle than one who has no such prospect."

"Give the working man the right to work as long as he is healthy, assure him care when he is sick, assure him maintenance when he is old."

— Otto von Bismarck, speech to the Reichstag, 1881

In June 1889, the Reichstag passed the Gesetz betreffend die Invaliditäts- und Altersversicherung — the Old Age and Disability Insurance Law. It was the first state pension program in history. Workers and employers both contributed through payroll deductions; the state topped it up. Anyone who had contributed for at least five years and reached the age of seventy would receive a modest monthly payment.

Seventy years old. Bismarck himself was seventy-four when he signed it — one of the few men in Germany old enough to collect. Male life expectancy at birth in the German Empire in 1889 was under 45 years. The retirement age had been set above the level that most workers would ever reach.

The Social Democrats, whose votes the system was designed to capture, actually opposed the bill. They saw it correctly as a cynical co-optation — the benefits were too small, the age threshold was a joke, and the contributions came from workers' own wages. Bismarck did not care. The system was not designed to be generous. It was designed to exist. The mere fact of a state pension changed the political calculus. Workers who had contributed for years had a tangible stake in the empire's continued existence. Joining a revolution that might overturn the state meant losing your pension.

It worked. The SPD kept growing in votes, but Germany never had its revolution. When the Weimar Republic lowered the retirement age to 65 in 1916 — in the middle of the First World War, to boost morale — it was an afterthought to a system that had already accomplished its real purpose.

Section 02

The Mechanism: How Pay-As-You-Go Works, and Why Demographics Are Its Achilles Heel

Understanding why Bismarck's system worked — and why the modern version is breaking — requires understanding what a pay-as-you-go pension actually is, because it is not what most people think.

When you pay into a national pension scheme, you are not putting money into a savings account that sits waiting for you. There is no pot with your name on it. You are paying the pensions of the people who are currently retired. When you retire, people currently working will pay yours. The whole system is a generational compact: each working generation funds the previous one, trusting that the next generation will do the same for them.

The Hidden Assumption Bismarck's System Was Built On

For a pay-as-you-go system to remain solvent, the ratio of workers to retirees must stay high enough. In 1889, for every one person old enough to collect, there were dozens of younger workers contributing. The math worked effortlessly. What nobody modeled was what happens when that ratio inverts.

In 2025, the OECD average is 33 people aged 65+ for every 100 people aged 20–64. By 2050, that figure is projected to reach 52. In Japan, South Korea, and several Southern European nations, the ratio is already approaching parity — more retirees than workers.

The system Bismarck designed was essentially a Ponzi structure with a very long horizon. It is only sustainable as long as each generation is at least as large and productive as the one before it. In 1889, in an era of high birth rates, high child mortality, low life expectancy, and rapid industrialization, those conditions held easily. The original program even had a built-in buffer: most contributors died before collecting anything, and those who did collect received payments for only a few years before dying.

The modern version faces the opposite problem. Life expectancy in OECD countries has risen from roughly 47 in 1900 to nearly 80 today. A worker who retires at 65 may collect a pension for 20 or 25 years — sometimes longer than their entire working career. Meanwhile, birth rates across the developed world have collapsed below replacement level. The OECD's working-age population is projected to fall by 13% by 2064.

1889 — Germany
World's first state pension introduced. Retirement age: 70. Life expectancy at birth: ~45. System is immediately cheap to run because almost nobody survives to collect.
1908 — United Kingdom
Old Age Pensions Act. Retirement age: 70. Benefits means-tested and tiny. Britain copies the structure but not the political motivation — this one is about genuine poverty relief.
1916 — Germany
Retirement age lowered to 65 during World War I to maintain civilian morale. Most other countries eventually follow. This becomes the global standard — though nobody recalculates the actuarial implications.
1935 — United States
Social Security Act passed under FDR. Retirement age: 65. At that point, average life expectancy at 65 was roughly 12–13 more years. The math seemed fine.
1950s–1960s
Baby Boom floods the labor market with contributors. Pay-as-you-go systems look spectacularly solvent. Benefits expand. Nobody plans for what happens when the Baby Boomers themselves retire.
2025 onward
Baby Boomers fully in retirement. OECD working-age population shrinking. By 2050, 52 retirees per 100 workers in OECD countries. The generational compact Bismarck invented is under the most severe pressure in its 136-year history.

The average net pension replacement rate across OECD countries — the pension as a share of final working wage — is currently 63%. For workers entering the labor market today, it is projected to fall to levels below 40% in Estonia, Ireland, South Korea, and Lithuania. Countries are essentially now offering Bismarck's original bargain in reverse: you contribute your whole career; we offer you something that probably will not be enough.

Section 03

The Connections: What Bismarck Actually Invented, and Why It Is Impossible to Undo

The most interesting thing Bismarck built was not a pension. It was a political lock-in mechanism so powerful that no democracy has ever successfully dismantled it.

The logic of pension lock-in works like this. The moment a government creates a contributory pension, it creates two politically potent groups: future beneficiaries (current workers who have paid in and expect to collect) and current beneficiaries (retirees who are already collecting). Both groups vote. Both groups will punish any politician who reduces benefits. The longer the system runs, the more people have contributed, and the more politically toxic any reform becomes.

France spent most of 2023 in street protests after the government raised the retirement age from 62 to 64 — an increase of two years, still well below what demographic reality requires. The protests were so severe that the government used emergency constitutional powers to bypass parliament. Greece's debt crisis was in large part a pension crisis: the country's pension spending had reached 17% of GDP by 2010, the highest in the European Union. The structural reforms demanded by the IMF — cutting pensions — produced political upheaval for a decade.

What the system assumed (1889)

  • Most contributors would die before collecting
  • Birth rates would remain high, keeping the contributor base large
  • Life expectancy at 65 would add only a few years of collection
  • Workers outnumbered retirees by many multiples
  • Immigration would supplement the labor force if needed

What is actually happening (2025)

  • Average retiree collects for 15–25 years
  • OECD birth rates are below replacement in almost every country
  • Life expectancy has nearly doubled since the system was designed
  • The old-age dependency ratio is rising rapidly toward 1:2
  • Immigration is politically contested; the politics of welfare complicate it further

The underlying arithmetic has only three solutions: raise retirement ages, cut benefits, or increase contributions. Every one of them is politically excruciating. Raise the retirement age and you punish people who did physical labor their whole life. Cut benefits and you break a promise people built their lives around. Raise contributions and you tax the young — who are already the first generation in modern history projected to be less wealthy than their parents — to pay for the old. No combination is painless, and any politician honest enough to propose one in clear terms tends to lose elections to one who is not.

Bismarck's real genius, seen from this distance, was not social welfare. It was the creation of an entitlement so psychologically durable — so woven into what people believe they are owed — that 136 years later, no government can touch it without existential political risk. He solved his problem. He created everyone else's.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

Four threads worth pulling if this one caught you.

The Ponzi Accusation

Economists debate whether pay-as-you-go pensions are structurally equivalent to Ponzi schemes. The answer is nuanced: they require perpetual growth in contributors, but unlike fraud schemes they are transparent and legal. Paul Samuelson's 1958 paper "An Exact Consumption-Loan Model" mathematically proved they can be sustainable — under specific demographic conditions that no longer hold.

Japan as the Preview

Japan is 30 years ahead of Europe in demographic decline. Its pension system has been in structural crisis since the 1990s. The government has responded with automatic adjustment mechanisms that quietly cut real benefits when the ratio worsens. By 2025, younger Japanese workers are projected to receive pensions worth roughly 50% of their contributions in real terms — effectively a tax with a small rebate attached.

Chile's Radical Experiment

In 1981, Pinochet's Chile abolished its pay-as-you-go pension entirely and replaced it with individual savings accounts managed by private funds. Forty years later, the results are mixed: the accounts accumulated wealth but benefits were lower than expected for many retirees, especially women with interrupted careers. Chile is now partially reversing course — re-introducing a collective element. Neither pure model is working.

The "Silver Tsunami" Political Economy

In most democracies, voters over 60 now outnumber voters under 35, and older voters turn out at far higher rates. This produces a structural electoral bias toward protecting pension benefits over other spending. Some political scientists argue that demographic aging is fundamentally reshaping what governments are able to do — creating gerontocracies by arithmetic rather than design.
