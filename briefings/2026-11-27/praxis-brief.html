Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

Two sources point at the same fixed point this week: build plans on what does not change, and keep judgment in-house. Morgan Housel's Same as Ever argues that forecasts keep failing while human responses to greed, fear and incentives repeat, and Ethan Mollick's account of working with agents says the human job that remains is summoning, assigning and evaluating work. This issue follows on from the 19 November issue on designing for surprise, but comes at it from the other side: not the risk you cannot see, but the constants you can rely on, and an incentive check that tests whether your own views are still yours.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Book · Morgan Housel · “Same as Ever” · 2023

Forecasts keep failing, so anchor your plans to the human behaviour that repeats

Housel's book reverses the usual planning question. Where most people ask what is about to change, he asks what will not, an approach he traces to a Jeff Bezos remark that the ten-year constants are rarely the subject of questions, a point Bezos made by calling that the more important question. The book's core claim is that the setting moves while the people inside it stay the same: circumstances shift across eras while the underlying nature of people holds, which means you may not know the specifics of the next shift yet still have a reasonable sense of how people will respond to it.

The practical consequence is a change in what you spend attention on. Housel's argument is that we cannot predict change, so attention is better spent on behaviours that have shown up throughout history. Fear, greed, envy and impatience are the stable inputs. A plan built on them survives a wrong forecast; a plan built on a forecast does not.

“Which of my current views would change if my incentives were different?”

Morgan Housel, Same as Ever, as quoted by Leadership Now

Forecasts are a weak foundation. Incentives and fear are a durable one, and that is where the plan should start.

Book · Morgan Housel · the incentive test · 2023

Your current views are probably shaped by incentives you have never checked

The most uncomfortable idea in the book is that a view you hold can be a product of your position rather than your reasoning. Housel's warning is that someone who answers that none of their views would shift under different incentives is likely blinded by those incentives. The test is simple, and it makes the abstract point concrete: take one belief that drives a decision you are making this month and ask what you would conclude if your pay, status or exposure were different.

This sits on top of a psychology he has written about for years. Housel's speaker profile describes his work as using insights from psychology, history, neurology and sociology to show how cognitive biases make investors their own worst enemies. The incentive question is the practical version of that claim: the bias is easiest to see when you ask what the view would be if the incentive had pointed elsewhere.

His anecdote about Warren Buffett and the candy bar makes the same point from a different angle. Buffett asked which bar sold best in 1962 and which sells best today, and the lesson was that some things never change, so attention should go there.

The incentive question is cheap to ask and expensive to skip, because it tests the view before the view tests you.

Forbes · John Werner on Ethan Mollick · “New Blog, New Book” · Oct 28, 2026

When agents do the work, the scarce skill becomes judging what came back

Mollick's recent writing gives the other half of the picture. Forbes reports that he suggests humans may become conjurers who summon agents, assign work and evaluate results. The shift is from producing output to directing it and checking it, which is a judgment job rather than a labour job.

The mechanics are already visible. Agents can call other agents for sub-tasks, which makes delegation to them smoother for the human. Delegation scales; checking does not scale in the same way. In one example Mollick describes, the AI pointed out the human's own mistake while building charts, which is evaluation running in the loop rather than being left to the end.

Set beside Housel, the two accounts land in the same place. Housel says the stable thing is human incentive and behaviour; Mollick says the human role that survives delegation is judgment about whether the output is right. Neither source calls it a constant, but both point to the same fixed point from different directions.

Housel says the human constants are what to plan around; Mollick says the human job left after delegation is judgment. Same fixed point, different entrance.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · drawn from Housel and Mollick

Write your constants down before you write the forecast

The convergence gives a simple sequence for the week: name what holds, test the views that depend on it, and then decide what you will check when someone else, or a tool, does the work for you. Keep it to one page so you actually use it.

  • Name three constants. Pick the decision you face this month and list three human behaviours that will matter in it regardless of the forecast, such as patience, fear of loss or the pull of comparison.
  • Run the incentive test on one live view. Write the belief in a single sentence, then write what you would conclude if your pay, status or exposure were reversed. If the two answers match, the view is probably sound.
  • Keep a delegation log. For each task you hand to an AI tool this week, record what you asked for, and what you checked before you relied on the output.
  • Book one evaluation slot. Pick a fixed Friday slot to review one delegated output against a standard you wrote down in advance, not one you invented after seeing the result.

A plan built on constants and a checking routine will hold up better than one built on a forecast you cannot test.

Practice · drawn from Housel's wealth and incentive writing

Build one rule around the behaviour that keeps repeating

A constant only helps if it becomes a rule you can follow on a bad day. The aim here is to turn one repeating behaviour into a standing instruction, so the decision does not depend on how you feel at the time.

  • Pick the behaviour. Choose one pattern you have seen repeat in your own decisions, such as overreacting to a short losing run or buying to keep up with peers.
  • Write the rule before the event. Phrase it as a condition and an action, for example: if the position falls by a set amount, then review the thesis and do nothing else that day.
  • Test it against your last three decisions. Check whether the rule would have helped, and whether it would have been ignored, then adjust the wording rather than the discipline.

04 On the Horizon
Finance

Expect the same behaviours to return in every new market cycle

If Housel is right that greed, fear and comparison repeat, then the useful planning question is not which asset leads next but which reactions will recur. That points toward buffers, position sizes and pre-set review triggers, which hold up better than a forecast does when a cycle turns.

AI

Delegation will push the bottleneck from producing work to checking it

Mollick's conjurer framing suggests the value of a worker will increasingly sit in choosing what to ask for and judging what comes back. That makes evaluation a skill to train deliberately, rather than something that will simply happen as tools improve.

Work

Teams that write down their constants may review better

A team that lists what will not change about its customers and standards has a fixed yardstick for reviewing both human and machine output. This is our reading of the convergence rather than a finding from either source, but it follows directly from the thesis above.
