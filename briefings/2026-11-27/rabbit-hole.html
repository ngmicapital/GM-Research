The Molasses Wave That Killed 21 in Boston — and the Factor of Safety Nobody Checked

January 1919, Boston's North End: a steel tank of syrup burst, and a wave of molasses moved through the streets at about 35 mph.

On a mild January afternoon in 1919, a 50-foot steel tank of molasses in Boston's North End burst open and sent a brown wave through the streets at about 35 mph, killing 21 people. The tank was poorly built and had never been properly tested, and a court eventually placed the blame on the company that owned it, not on anarchists or fermentation alone.

TL;DR

Most people hear 'molasses flood' and picture a joke. The reality was a steel tank holding about 2.3 million gallons, a wave up to 25 feet high moving at roughly 35 mph, and 21 dead. The physics is simple once you see it: a column of syrup that dense, released in a single breach, behaves less like a spill and more like a pressurised charge. The deeper story is about who decided the tank was safe, and why a state auditor needed more than five years and thousands of witnesses to say so.

Section 01

The Story

On 15 January 1919, at a little after noon, a steel storage tank on Commercial Street in Boston's North End gave way. It held roughly 2.3 million gallons of molasses, stacked about 50 feet high in a structure around 90 feet across. What came out was not a puddle. Witnesses described a wall of syrup, estimated at up to 25 feet, moving at about 35 mph and carrying steel, horses, carts and people. The rivets popped out of the plates with a noise survivors compared to gunfire, and the flow flattened buildings and bent the steel of the elevated railway above the street.

The first explanation was not engineering. The tank's owner, the United States Industrial Alcohol company (USIA), argued that anarchists had dynamited it. That story suited the Red Scare mood of the time and a company that had made large profits converting molasses into alcohol for wartime munitions. The company spent more than $50,000 on expert witnesses to argue sabotage, and the claim was never supported by evidence.

An occurrence of this kind must not and cannot pass without a rigid investigation to determine the cause of the explosion
— Andrew Peters, Mayor of Boston, January 1919

The tank had been a problem from the start. It was built in 1915 under a rushed programme, and neighbours had reported it leaking molasses from its seams for years. Once the case was heard, the picture was blunt. The victims filed 119 separate claims, which were merged into a single proceeding that ran for years. The auditor, Hugh W. Ogden, was a former military officer appointed to hear the evidence, and by April 1925 he had ruled that USIA was liable for the collapse, citing poor planning, weak oversight and a history of negligence. The company was ordered to pay $628,000, a sum the sources describe as roughly $8 million to $11 million in today's money depending on the inflation measure used.

The collapse also happened on an unusual day. Boston had warmed to about 4 °C after several frigid days, and molasses had been pumped in from a tanker only days earlier. The timing matters for the mechanism that follows, and it is also the reason the case is still argued over.

Section 02

The Mechanism

Start with the fluid. Molasses is extremely viscous, several thousand times thicker than water, and it is denser too, at roughly 1.4 tonnes per cubic metre, about 40 percent heavier than water. Thick liquids are slow to drain through a small hole, which is why a leaking seam can weep for years without a catastrophic release. But a tank does not fail through a small hole. Once the wall gave way along a seam, the whole column above the breach was free to fall, and the speed of the leading edge depended on the height of the column and not on how sticky the syrup was.

A rough check makes the 35 mph figure believable. The simplest model of an opening at the base is that the exit velocity equals the square root of twice gravity times the head of liquid. With a head of about 15 metres, that gives roughly 17 metres per second, or about 38 mph, before any viscous losses. The viscosity and friction of the street would slow the real flow, so 35 mph is consistent with an order-of-magnitude estimate rather than a freak. At the base of that column the hydrostatic pressure was about two atmospheres above air. The mass was the weapon, and the tank had been built to hold it only as long as the steel stayed intact.

The fermentation question

Hot molasses was pumped on top of cold molasses that had already been sitting in the tank. Several accounts, including the Massachusetts state house history, say the mixing could trigger fermentation and carbon dioxide build-up. Yeasts in unrefined molasses can ferment, but the defence argued the material was too cold to ferment, and modern commentary still treats gas pressure as one theory among several. A 2015 structural study focused on the steel and the construction, while a Harvard study examined the effect of winter temperatures on the collapse. The honest summary is that structural weakness is the best-supported cause, with temperature and gas as possible contributing factors.

The structural story is about margin. Engineers call the ratio between a structure's real capacity and its expected load the factor of safety. The tank's margin was too thin for a fluid this heavy, and the rivets were too few and too small for the plates they joined. Standard practice included a test: fill the new tank with water, look for leaks and watch how the structure behaves under load. According to the account in the case history, the tank was filled only to a shallow depth before being declared ready for millions of gallons. A water test would not have reproduced the temperature and fermentation conditions, but it would have exposed a poorly joined structure that was never designed to hold its load.

The tank was poorly constructed, never properly tested, and repeatedly showed signs of structural weakness that were ignored.
— Paraphrased synthesis of the 1925 Ogden findings, as summarised by list25.com
Section 03

The Connections

The sabotage defence. The most revealing part of the story is not the wave but the instinct to blame an outside enemy. A company with a political and commercial interest in a clean narrative reached first for a villain that the court had no reason to take seriously. The same pattern appears in corporate accidents across the twentieth century: the first account is about an external shock, and the engineering record comes later. Ogden's verdict mattered because it refused that move and put the failure back inside the firm.

Why the rules changed. The Massachusetts response to the verdict was not only financial. The state and most others moved to certify engineers and regulate construction, a shift that turned design and inspection from private judgement into public obligation. It is a reminder that many safety regimes are scar tissue from a specific failure. The building codes, pressure-vessel standards and licensing rules that now govern industrial storage largely descend from cases where a tank, boiler or bridge gave way and somebody had to say who was responsible.

The competing explanations. Historians and engineers still disagree about the exact sequence. Structural failure, temperature change, fermentation gas and the rough way the tank was filled can all be argued as the trigger. The useful lesson is that complex failures usually have several causes stacked in sequence, and that the most dangerous one is often the one that was merely tolerated. The molasses was not dangerous on its own; a thin-walled tank holding a dense, warming, possibly gassing liquid was. The verdict assigned responsibility to the owner, but the physics is a story about many small margins eroding at once.

The modern parallel. For a reader who watches markets and AI systems, the molasses tank looks uncomfortably familiar. Leverage, automated trading and large language models all store energy in forms that behave benignly until a shock arrives, and the people who built them often rely on the same reassurance that the structure has never failed. Stress testing in banking and model evaluation in AI are the modern versions of the water test, and the question they ask is the one Ogden asked: where is the factor of safety, and who verified it? The honest caveat is that these analogies compare very different systems, and the molasses case is a clean example of a physical failure in which the cause was traceable to the steel, the rivets and the paperwork.

Live parallel

Industrial storage of hazardous liquids is still regulated through engineering certification and periodic inspection, the direct legacy of the 1925 verdict. The modern version of Ogden's question is whether a system has been tested under the conditions it will face, not just the ones that are convenient to simulate.
