NOAA Found a Golden Blob Two Miles Down and It Took Two and a Half Years to Identify It

In August 2023 a NOAA remotely operated vehicle came across a glowing golden sphere attached to rock at 3,250 metres depth in the Gulf of Alaska. No one on board could identify it. It was not a sponge, not a coral egg case, not a mineral deposit. Initial DNA testing was inconclusive. It took whole-genome sequencing, more than two years, and two institutions to determine that it was something that had simply never been observed in isolation before — and the answer raised questions nobody had thought to ask.

A glowing golden sphere found 2 miles deep in the Gulf of Alaska in 2023 baffled NOAA scientists for two and a half years before whole-genome sequencing revealed it was the calcified base of a giant deep-sea anemone, Relicanthus daphneae — an anatomical region so hidden beneath the animal's body that it had never been described in isolation, packed with the largest spirocysts known among all cnidarians. The finding is a reminder that more than 80% of the ocean remains unexplored and that even well-known animal groups contain body parts no human has ever properly observed.

TL;DR

On a routine survey of the Gulf of Alaska seafloor in the summer of 2023, the remotely operated vehicle Deep Discoverer — launched from NOAA's research vessel Okeanos Explorer — descended past two miles of water and found something sitting on the rock that nobody on board recognised. It was roughly the size of a grapefruit, golden-coloured, firmly attached to the substrate, and glowing faintly in the ROV's lights. The footage went online. Scientists around the world watched it. Nobody could agree on what it was. Standard DNA testing from a tissue sample returned inconclusive results. It took two and a half years, the combined resources of NOAA Fisheries and the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History, and whole-genome sequencing before researchers could say with confidence what the golden orb actually was: the detached base of a giant deep-sea anemone that has been seen only about thirty times in its entire documented history — a part of the animal that is always hidden beneath its body and had never once been properly described in the scientific literature.

Section 01

The Story

The Okeanos Explorer is NOAA's dedicated deep-sea exploration vessel, and in 2023 it was running a systematic survey of poorly-mapped seafloor in the Gulf of Alaska. The ROV dives are broadcast live online, in real time, with a team of scientists watching from the ship and a wider audience watching via the internet. The footage from most of these dives is geologically interesting and biologically routine: soft corals, sea stars, rockfish, the occasional unusual sponge. On this particular dive at 3,250 metres, everything was routine until it was not.

The golden orb appeared on-screen as the ROV moved along the bottom. It was unlike anything in the immediate vicinity: smooth, golden, roughly 10 centimetres across, attached to bare rock. The surrounding seafloor was dark and colourless, so the object's warmth made it immediately conspicuous. One scientist on the ship channel said, live, "Everyone was like, What the heck? What is that?" Several guesses circulated in real time: a sponge of some kind, possibly a sea sponge reproductive structure; a coral egg case; a colonial organism; a hydrothermal mineral deposit. None of the guesses were confident. The ROV team collected the object with the vehicle's manipulator arm and brought it to the surface.

The ocean covers 71% of the planet, makes up more than 98% of Earth's habitable space, and we have explored less than 20% of it. The golden orb was not found in some extreme, inaccessible corner of the abyss. It was found in a routine survey of waters we nominally consider explored.

The tissue sample collected aboard ship was submitted for DNA analysis. This is now a routine first step in deep-sea taxonomy: extract genetic material, compare it to reference databases, identify the organism. For most deep-sea specimens the process is fast. For the golden orb it returned nothing useful. The sample's genetic signatures were too degraded or too unusual for standard short-read sequencing methods to produce a confident match. The object sat in a lab freezer. Months passed. It was eventually transferred to the Smithsonian, where it was subjected to additional physical analysis alongside the genetic work. Researchers examined its microscopic structure. They noted the presence of spirocysts — specialised coiled organelles used by cnidarians for adhesion — in extraordinary density. The spirocysts were the largest ever documented among all cnidarians, a phylum that includes jellyfish, corals, and anemones. That observation narrowed the search. But it did not end it.

The final answer required whole-genome sequencing: extracting, assembling, and comparing the orb's complete genetic blueprint rather than targeted marker genes. When researchers did this, the orb's genome was found to be "genetically almost identical" to that of Relicanthus daphneae, a species of giant deep-sea anemone first formally described in 2014 and documented fewer than thirty times across its entire known range — from the Antarctic Ocean to the Aleutian Islands to the Central Pacific.

The orb, researchers concluded, was the calcified basal disc of one of these anemones: the organ that anchors the animal to the rock. Because it is always located beneath the animal's body, resting on the substrate, it has never once been documented in isolation. Every prior sighting of Relicanthus daphneae had shown the animal from above: its dark, foot-wide trunk and its extraordinary tentacles, which can extend to two metres in length. The part that held it in place was invisible. No one had described it. No one had a photograph of it. It was the biological equivalent of a footprint of an animal that may have moved on or died — left behind as the only evidence that something extraordinary was once anchored to that rock.

Section 02

Why It Was So Hard to Identify

The difficulty in identifying the golden orb is instructive because it illustrates three separate problems that converge whenever science attempts to catalogue life in the deep ocean: morphological incompleteness, genetic database gaps, and the degradation of biological material under handling conditions.

Morphological incompleteness is precisely the problem the golden orb itself reveals. For Relicanthus daphneae, no scientific description of the basal disc existed in the literature because no scientist had ever collected one in isolation. The formal species description from 2014 was based on live video observations and tissue samples taken from the body column and tentacles. The base was simply never in view. When taxonomists later tried to match the orb to known species by visual morphology, they had nothing to compare it to. This is not unusual in deep-sea cnidarian taxonomy: many species are described from incomplete specimens collected under difficult conditions, and entire anatomical regions remain uncharacterised.

The Spirocyst Problem

Spirocysts are coiled, thread-like organelles within cnidarian cells that function like sticky lassoes, firing outward to adhere to surfaces. The spirocysts found in the golden orb were the largest ever documented for any cnidarian — a finding that should have narrowed identification considerably but in practice did not, because the spirocyst size data for most deep-sea cnidarian species simply does not exist in comparable published form. The absence of a reference is itself a data gap that compounds the identification problem.

Genetic database gaps explain why standard DNA barcoding failed. DNA barcoding typically targets a short, standardised region of mitochondrial DNA (the COI gene) and compares it to sequences already deposited in public databases. For common or commercially important marine species, database coverage is excellent. For rare deep-sea anemones documented only thirty times in history, coverage is thin to non-existent. The golden orb's DNA did not match any reference sequence in BOLD (the Barcode of Life Data System) or NCBI with confidence. Whole-genome sequencing bypassed this problem by generating a complete genetic dataset that could be compared to whatever reference genomes existed, however incomplete — and the genome of Relicanthus daphneae was close enough that the comparison worked.

Sample degradation compounded both problems. Deep-sea ROV samples are collected at 3°C and then brought to the surface over roughly two hours, during which the pressure changes from 325 atmospheres to 1. The thermal and pressure shock can degrade nucleic acids rapidly. Tissue samples collected in the field are typically preserved in ethanol immediately upon recovery, but the preservation window matters and is not always optimal. The golden orb's initial DNA was degraded enough to make standard barcoding inconclusive — a common problem for rare specimens where there is no opportunity to re-collect if the first sample fails.

The resolution — whole-genome sequencing at a major national museum two and a half years after collection — is now increasingly feasible for unusual specimens, but it is slow, expensive relative to barcoding, and requires institutional infrastructure. For the thousands of unknown deep-sea specimens collected annually by research vessels worldwide, most do not receive this level of analysis. They sit in freezers or are described from photographs alone. The golden orb is unusual not because the delay in identification was exceptional, but because it was eventually identified at all.

Section 03

The Connections

The 80% problem and what "unexplored" actually means. The statistic that more than 80% of the ocean remains unexplored is widely quoted, but it obscures what the word "unexplored" means in practice. It does not mean waters humans have never been near. It means seafloor that has never been directly observed, sampled, or mapped at high resolution. The Gulf of Alaska, where the golden orb was found, is not a remote or exotic ocean basin; it is in the North Pacific, adjacent to a rich fishing region with a century of commercial activity. The dive that found the orb was at a depth accessible to modern ROVs and not unusual by research standards. The golden orb was found not at the edge of the known, but in waters that, by the conventional accounting, we consider moderately well-surveyed. This is what 80% unexplored actually means: that even the surveyed 20% holds objects that baffled expert researchers for two years.

Anemones with two-metre tentacles and the underestimation of deep-sea body plans. Relicanthus daphneae has tentacles that can reach two metres in length. Its trunk is roughly thirty centimetres wide. It lives at depths between 2,000 and 4,000 metres, anchored to rock or dead coral. When it was first formally described in 2014, it was placed in its own family — Relicanthidae — because it is so morphologically divergent from all known anemones that it does not fit within any existing family groupings. There are probably many such divergent animals in the deep sea that we have not yet found, let alone described at the family level. The golden orb is a reminder that the known taxonomy of deep-sea cnidarians is a rough sketch, not a complete map.

The ROV broadcast model and citizen science as a detection system. The live-streaming of NOAA Okeanos Explorer dives is not primarily a public relations effort; it functions as a distributed expert network. Footage streams in real time to scientists around the world who watch from their own institutions. On any given dive, the live audience may include specialists in sponge taxonomy, crustacean biology, coral ecology, and geology who are not present on the ship. The identification of unusual organisms — or the flagging of "I have no idea what that is" moments — is crowd-sourced to whoever is watching. The golden orb's fame was partly a consequence of this model: it was identified as unusual by a large audience in real time, which created the institutional pressure to collect it and investigate properly. Without the broadcast, it might have been filmed and filed as "unknown object" in a database annotation.

Seabed 2030 and the race to map the ocean floor. The Nippon Foundation-GEBCO Seabed 2030 project has a single goal: to produce a complete, high-resolution map of the global ocean floor by 2030. As of 2025, roughly 26% of the seafloor had been mapped to modern resolution standards. The remaining 74% is known only from satellite-derived gravity data, which can identify large-scale features like mid-ocean ridges and seamounts but cannot resolve features smaller than a few kilometres. The golden orb was attached to a rock feature that would be invisible to satellite mapping. Completing the survey in the remaining five years requires every research vessel in the world to route its multibeam sonar across unmapped areas while in transit — a logistics problem as much as a technology one.

🦬
Relicanthus daphneae
The species was formally described in 2014 by Rodolfo Riccardi and colleagues in a paper in PLOS ONE based on observations from multiple ocean basins. Its placement in its own family, Relicanthidae, signals how morphologically isolated it is. With tentacles up to two metres and a documented range from the Antarctic to the Central Pacific, it is one of the least-known large invertebrates on Earth despite being technically observable at accessible ROV depths.
🧬
Whole-Genome Sequencing in Taxonomy
The golden orb case is an early example of what taxonomists call "phylogenomics" replacing traditional morphology and DNA barcoding for unusual specimens. As sequencing costs have fallen below $1,000 per genome, the technique is becoming routine for deep-sea museum specimens that could not be identified by other means. The Smithsonian's National Museum of Natural History is building a genomics pipeline specifically for such cases.
🌊
Seabed 2030
The Nippon Foundation-GEBCO Seabed 2030 project tracks progress toward mapping the entire ocean floor at high resolution. Its public dashboard shows which portions of the seafloor have been mapped to modern standards and which regions remain data-sparse. As of early 2026, progress has been concentrated in the North Atlantic and Pacific; large portions of the Southern Ocean and deep Indian Ocean remain unmapped at useful resolution.
🔬
Spirocysts and Cnidarian Weaponry
Cnidarians (anemones, jellyfish, corals) deploy two main types of stinging/adhesive organelles: nematocysts, which fire barbed threads to inject venom, and spirocysts, which fire sticky coiled threads for adhesion. The golden orb's spirocysts were the largest ever documented in the phylum. For comparison, a sea anemone's spirocysts typically measure 30–50 micrometres; those in the golden orb significantly exceeded this. Their functional role in Relicanthus daphneae's base remains uncharacterised.
