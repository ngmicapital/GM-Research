Driving Thesis

Three advances this cycle sharpen the Alzheimer’s defence and functional longevity picture. USC researchers have identified BRI-50460 — the first selective, brain-penetrating inhibitor of cPLA2 (calcium-dependent phospholipase A2) — a drug candidate published in npj Drug Discovery (Nature, 2026) that reduces APOE4-driven neuroinflammation in preclinical models without disrupting the enzyme’s healthy cellular functions; cPLA2 elevation is specifically associated with Alzheimer’s risk in APOE4 carriers, making this the first drug-discovery milestone targeting APOE4-linked brain inflammation directly rather than via amyloid clearance alone. In parallel, a 2026 randomized crossover trial in 39 participants provides direct human evidence that quality sleep drives glymphatic clearance of amyloid-beta and tau from brain to blood — higher morning plasma levels of both proteins after a full night of sleep versus sleep-restricted nights, consistent with active overnight brain-to-blood transport via the glymphatic system — validating in humans a mechanism previously established only in rodent models. Longevity & Performance Science

The USC cPLA2 / BRI-50460 discovery is mechanistically significant because it targets a pathway that is specifically dysregulated in APOE4 carriers — the roughly 25% of the population carrying at least one APOE4 allele who face 3–12× elevated Alzheimer’s risk. The problem with most Alzheimer’s drug targets so far — amyloid plaques, tau tangles, APOE cholesterol transport — is that they operate across all genotypes and the interventions have shown limited benefit in APOE4 carriers specifically. cPLA2 is different: it is an inflammatory enzyme whose elevated activity correlates specifically with APOE4 carrier status, and reducing it selectively targets the APOE4-driven neuroinflammatory cascade without suppressing the enzyme’s baseline healthy functions (which protect normal phospholipid membrane composition). The compound design challenge — achieving selectivity for cPLA2 without cross-inhibiting related PLA2 enzymes, and achieving blood-brain barrier penetration — was solved by computational screening of billions of molecules to identify BRI-50460, which achieved an IC50 of 0.88 nM in cellular assays and demonstrated favorable brain-to-plasma ratios in vivo. This is a preclinical result; human trials are not yet underway. But the target selectivity and brain penetration profile mean BRI-50460 is now positioned as a genuine Phase 1 candidate.

The glymphatic clearance human trial is the first randomized crossover study in humans to directly demonstrate that sleep — not just rest — drives the elimination of Alzheimer’s-associated proteins from brain tissue into peripheral blood. The mechanism, first described in rodent models by the Nedergaard lab (Science, 2013), involves cerebrospinal fluid flowing through perivascular spaces along arteries during slow-wave sleep, driven by reduced noradrenergic tone and astrocytic aquaporin-4 (AQP4) channel activity. In sleep-restricted conditions, this flow is disrupted and amyloid-beta and tau accumulate in brain tissue instead of being transported to peripheral clearance. The 2026 crossover trial finding — that morning plasma levels of both amyloid-beta and tau are measurably higher after a full night of sleep than after sleep deprivation — is directionally counterintuitive (higher plasma = more clearance, not more disease), but perfectly consistent with the glymphatic model: quality sleep pushes these proteins out of the brain and into the blood, where they can be cleared by hepatic and renal processes. The clinical implication is that sleep quality is not just a recovery variable — it is an active Alzheimer’s risk modifier operating through a specific, now human-validated mechanism.

USC Identifies BRI-50460: First Selective, Brain-Penetrating cPLA2 Inhibitor Targeting APOE4-Linked Alzheimer’s Neuroinflammation — npj Drug Discovery, Nature, 2026
[Mechanistic]

Researchers at the Keck School of Medicine of USC, led by senior author Dr. Hussein Yassine (Director, Center for Personalized Brain Health), published findings in npj Drug Discovery (Nature; Volume 3, Issue 1; 2026; PubMed ID: 41523267) identifying and validating a novel drug target for APOE4-associated Alzheimer’s disease. The target is calcium-dependent phospholipase A2 (cPLA2) — an enzyme whose elevated activity has been specifically linked to Alzheimer’s risk in APOE4 carriers, making it a genotype-stratified therapeutic opportunity distinct from amyloid or tau-focused approaches.

The challenge: cPLA2 also supports healthy brain function (phospholipid membrane regulation, normal signaling), so complete inhibition would cause cellular harm. The team needed a compound that could: (1) selectively inhibit the harmful inflammation-driving activity of cPLA2 without suppressing related PLA2 enzymes that maintain healthy function, and (2) cross the blood-brain barrier at sufficient concentrations to be pharmacologically active in neural tissue. To find this, co-author Vsevolod Katritch used computational screening of billions of candidate molecules, ultimately identifying a lead compound that was then prepared for testing by co-author Stan Louie.

Results: The lead compound, designated BRI-50460, demonstrated: an IC50 of 0.88 nM in cellular assays (indicating extremely potent inhibition at very low concentrations); high selectivity for cPLA2 with minimal cross-inhibition of related PLA2 enzymes essential for normal cell function; and favorable brain-to-plasma ratios in in vivo studies, confirming that the compound successfully crosses the blood-brain barrier and reaches the central nervous system at therapeutically relevant concentrations. In human brain cell models under Alzheimer’s-related stress conditions, BRI-50460 modulated neuroinflammatory pathways and restored lipid homeostasis — the two primary mechanisms through which elevated cPLA2 is thought to drive APOE4-associated pathology.

The clinical context: people with the APOE4 genotype who also show elevated cPLA2 activity are the highest-risk subgroup for Alzheimer’s development. Current approved Alzheimer’s treatments (lecanemab, donanemab) target amyloid plaques and show modest benefits in mixed APOE genotype populations; APOE4 homozygotes show higher side-effect rates (ARIA — amyloid-related imaging abnormalities) with amyloid-clearing antibodies. A genotype-specific neuroinflammation target like cPLA2 could offer a mechanistically complementary approach with a more favourable safety profile for the highest-risk carriers. BRI-50460 is currently a preclinical candidate; no human trials have been initiated. Dr. Yassine stated: “In this study, we identified compounds that act selectively on cPLA2, with minimal effects on related PLA2 enzymes that are important for normal cellular function.”

2026 Human Randomized Crossover Trial Confirms Quality Sleep Drives Glymphatic Clearance of Amyloid-Beta and Tau From Brain to Blood — PMC13079953 Review Synthesis
[Human RCT]

A 2026 randomized crossover trial involving 39 participants provided the first direct human experimental evidence that quality sleep actively drives glymphatic clearance of Alzheimer’s-associated proteins from brain tissue into peripheral blood. Participants underwent both a full-night sleep condition and a sleep-restricted condition in crossover design; blood samples were taken at matched time points the following morning. Morning plasma levels of amyloid-beta and phosphorylated tau were significantly higher after the full-sleep night than after sleep deprivation — a result that is directionally counterintuitive but mechanistically consistent with the glymphatic model: sleep pushes these proteins out of the brain into circulation, where peripheral clearance (hepatic, renal) can eliminate them. Sleep deprivation suppresses this overnight export, causing proteins to accumulate in brain tissue.

The mechanistic background: the glymphatic system — first described in rodent models by the Nedergaard lab at the University of Rochester (Science, 2013) — is a network of perivascular channels lined by astrocytic aquaporin-4 (AQP4) water channels that facilitates the flow of cerebrospinal fluid through brain tissue during sleep. During slow-wave (non-REM) sleep, reduced noradrenergic tone (the brain is no longer maintaining waking-state alertness) allows interstitial space between neurons to expand, increasing convective CSF flow through the perivascular spaces. This flow physically transports metabolic waste — including amyloid-beta and tau — from the interstitium into the CSF and ultimately into blood. Three conditions are required for optimal function: (1) intact AQP4 expression at astrocyte endfeet; (2) adequate arterial pulsatility to drive convective flow; (3) slow-wave sleep neurochemistry. All three degrade with age, explaining why glymphatic clearance declines approximately 40% between young adulthood and the seventh decade — and why older adults accumulate amyloid-beta faster than younger adults even at equivalent sleep duration.

A critical caveat highlighted in a 2026 review (PMC13079953, Sleep-Dependent Clearance of Brain Metabolites via the Glymphatic System): sedative-induced sleep does not replicate glymphatic function. Many sleep medications (benzodiazepines, Z-drugs) suppress deep slow-wave sleep while producing behavioral sleep — the patient appears to sleep but the neurochemical drivers of glymphatic flow are absent. This means pharmacological sleep is not biologically equivalent to natural sleep for brain waste clearance purposes, with direct implications for how sleep quality is assessed in Alzheimer’s prevention protocols. The review also notes that the bidirectional relationship between sleep disruption and neurodegeneration suggests that treating sleep disorders in midlife — before amyloid pathology is clinically apparent — may be one of the highest-leverage preventive interventions available.

Source: PMC13079953 · Sleep-Dependent Clearance of Brain Metabolites via the Glymphatic System: Implications for Alzheimer’s Pathophysiology · 2026 narrative review synthesising human RCT (39-participant crossover trial) and mechanistic literature · Nedergaard lab foundational work (Science 2013) · Nature Communications 2026 (s41467-026-68374-8)
⚡ Implication: BRI-50460 is the first drug candidate to specifically target the APOE4-linked neuroinflammation mechanism — a genotype-stratified approach that could complement amyloid-clearing therapies or replace them for the highest-risk APOE4 carriers where amyloid antibodies carry elevated ARIA risk. The compound is preclinical; watch for Phase 1 safety data. On sleep: the human glymphatic clearance evidence formally closes the loop from rodent model to human trial — slow-wave sleep is not passive recovery, it is the active brain waste removal operation. Optimising sleep quality (not just duration) and avoiding sedative-induced sleep that suppresses slow-wave phases are now mechanistically grounded Alzheimer’s prevention strategies.
Section 02
Training & Performance

The JAMA Network Open grip strength study is methodologically significant because it separates functional strength from general physical activity and from cardiovascular fitness as an independent mortality predictor. Studies that track exercise volume (step counts, MET-hours per week) or cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2max) are the dominant longevity biomarker literature — but the University at Buffalo study adjusted for both physical activity level and cardiovascular fitness, and the grip strength association remained independently predictive. This means functional strength carries longevity information that exercise volume and aerobic capacity do not fully capture — it likely reflects neuromuscular integrity, fast-twitch motor unit preservation, and musculoskeletal reserve that are not adequately proxied by steps walked or aerobic fitness alone. The chair stand test finding is particularly relevant for population-level screening: it requires no equipment, takes under 60 seconds, and now has precise mortality-predictive values (6 seconds of improvement in time = 4% mortality reduction) that can guide clinical conversations about functional independence.

The cold water immersion (CWI) hypertrophy trade-off is the most important confound for athletes and longevity-oriented practitioners who use cold plunges for recovery. The evidence is mechanistically coherent and consistently replicated: post-exercise inflammation is not a side effect of hard training — it is part of the anabolic signal. Blunting it with cold water suppresses downstream mTOR activation, satellite cell recruitment, and muscle protein synthesis. A systematic review and meta-analysis (PMC11235606, Frontiers in Sports and Active Living) of available controlled studies confirms that CWI following resistance training attenuates hypertrophic adaptations compared with passive recovery — while not significantly affecting maximal strength gains, which appear to be driven more by neural adaptations than protein accretion. The practical distinction matters: CWI is genuinely useful for between-session recovery in high-frequency competition contexts (multi-day tournaments, daily training blocks) where performance preservation matters more than long-term hypertrophy. For the standard athlete training for muscle growth, CWI within 4 hours post-lift is a physiological liability.

Grip Strength and Chair Stand Performance Predict Longevity Independently of Exercise Volume — 5,000+ Women, 8 Years, JAMA Network Open, University at Buffalo, 2026
[Longitudinal Cohort]

A longitudinal observational study published in JAMA Network Open (2026; 9(2): e2559367) by Michael LaMonte, PhD and colleagues at the University at Buffalo School of Public Health tracked 5,047 women aged 63–99 years for eight years, measuring two functional strength metrics at baseline and tracking all-cause mortality as the primary outcome. Collaborating institutions included the National Cancer Institute, UC San Diego, Texas A&M, Brown University, Stanford University, and Fred Hutch Cancer Center.

Measurement 1 — Grip strength: Measured via a standard handheld dynamometer. Every additional 7 kg of grip strength at baseline was associated with a 12% reduction in all-cause mortality risk over the eight-year follow-up. The relationship was linear across the range tested and persisted after adjusting for: age, chronic medical conditions, socioeconomic factors, physical activity level (self-reported and accelerometer-measured), cardiovascular fitness (estimated VO2max), and systemic inflammation (C-reactive protein). The persistence after adjusting for cardiovascular fitness and physical activity is the critical finding — grip strength carries longevity information independent of aerobic fitness and exercise volume.

Measurement 2 — Five chair stand test: Participants performed five sit-to-stand movements from a standard chair as quickly as possible without using their arms. Every 6-second improvement in completion time was associated with a 4% lower mortality rate — again independent of physical activity level, cardiovascular fitness, and inflammation markers. The chair stand test is particularly notable for clinical utility: it is non-equipment, takes under one minute, and measures coordinated lower extremity power, balance, and functional mobility simultaneously — a composite of the capacities that determine whether older adults can live independently.

Population note: The study enrolled only women, limiting direct extrapolation to men. However, the mechanistic basis for grip strength as a longevity biomarker — neuromuscular integrity, fast-twitch motor unit preservation, musculoskeletal reserve — is not sex-specific, and prior mixed-sex cohort studies (including a 2025 Nature Aging meta-analysis of 2.5 million participants across 60 studies) have confirmed grip strength as a mortality predictor across sexes. The additional clinical significance of this study is that the mortality association held even among women who did not meet the 150-minute-per-week aerobic physical activity guideline — suggesting that functional strength has independent value even in individuals who are not meeting cardiorespiratory activity targets.

Clinical translation underway: In 2026, multiple primary care networks are formally adding dynamometer grip testing to their standard vitals panel alongside blood pressure — a recognition that grip strength, like blood pressure, is a simple measurement that captures systemic biological age and disease risk in a single number. The chair stand test is already included in many geriatric assessment batteries (Timed Up and Go, SPPB) but is increasingly being proposed for routine use in earlier-age clinical populations.

⚠ Hype Watch

Cold water immersion post-resistance-training for muscle growth: Post-workout cold plunging has grown into a near-universal recovery ritual among strength athletes and biohackers, marketed as accelerating muscle repair and growth alongside its recovery benefits. The evidence on recovery (reduced DOMS, faster performance restoration for next-day training) is largely legitimate. The evidence on hypertrophy — long-term muscle growth — is consistently negative. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in Frontiers in Physiology (PMC11235606; “Throwing cold water on muscle growth”) synthesizing all controlled CWI-resistance training studies confirms that post-exercise cold water immersion attenuates hypertrophic adaptations compared with passive recovery. The mechanism is precise: the acute inflammatory response to resistance training — specifically IL-6 and other pro-inflammatory cytokines released by stressed muscle fibers — is a necessary upstream signal that activates satellite cells, triggers mTOR pathway phosphorylation, and initiates muscle protein synthesis. Reducing local muscle temperature via cold water suppresses this cascade, blunting both satellite cell recruitment and anabolic signaling. The cold plunge is not breaking anything; it is interrupting the conversation between the muscle damage signal and the repair-and-grow response.

The nuanced picture: cold water immersion does not fully eliminate hypertrophy — it attenuates it. And for specific use cases, this trade-off is rational: multi-day competitions, double training days, or any context where recovering performance for the next session matters more than maximising long-term muscle growth. The critical variable is timing. Studies show the hypertrophy-blunting effect is strongest when CWI is performed immediately post-lift (within 1 hour). Delaying CWI by 4+ hours allows the initial anabolic signaling cascade to complete before cold suppresses it, substantially reducing interference with hypertrophy. If recovery is the goal, delay CWI by 4–6 hours after resistance training. If the goal is maximising muscle growth, CWI is best reserved for non-training days or before upper-body training if lower-body training was completed the previous day. The norepinephrine spike and dopamine elevation from cold exposure (documented at ~530% and ~250% respectively above baseline) are real and valuable independently — but these neurochemical benefits are available without the hypertrophy trade-off when CWI is timed away from resistance training.

Functional Strength — Integrate Now
  • Add a simple grip strength test as a personal longevity baseline: if you do not own a dynamometer, a clinic or physio can measure it. Track twice yearly. Progressive improvement or maintenance is the target; regression warrants investigation
  • Chair stand performance — 5 unassisted sit-to-stands as fast as possible — is a useful functional mobility proxy. Track time, not just completion. Under 11 seconds for adults 60–69 is associated with normal functional status; over 14 seconds warrants functional assessment
  • For PPL practitioners: grip strength is a direct output of pulling exercises (deadlifts, rows, pull-ups). Farmer carries and dead hangs specifically develop grip and are worth incorporating as standalone accessory work — not just as a general training side-effect
Cold Plunge Timing Rules
  • Avoid cold water immersion within 4 hours of resistance training if hypertrophy is the primary goal. The inflammatory signal that drives muscle growth peaks and begins resolving in the 2–4 hour post-training window; immersing before this completes interrupts the anabolic cascade
  • CWI is rational for: between-session performance recovery in multi-day competitions, daily two-a-day training blocks, or as a standalone neurochemical/mood regulation tool on non-training days
  • Strength gains (neural adaptations) are not attenuated by CWI — only hypertrophy (muscle protein accretion). Athletes training primarily for strength rather than size have less to lose from post-workout cold exposure
⚡ Implication: Grip strength is now validated as a mortality biomarker independent of cardiovascular fitness and exercise volume — a result that should prompt strength-focused training at every age as a distinct longevity lever from aerobic conditioning. The 12% mortality reduction per 7 kg of grip strength is a large effect that persists across physical activity adjustment. On cold plunging: the recovery benefits are real but the hypertrophy trade-off is mechanistically well-documented. Timing is the resolution — 4+ hours between the lift and the plunge preserves both benefits.
Section 03
Supplements & Nutrition

The Frontiers in Nutrition 2026 narrative review on protein quality and sarcopenia prevention consolidates a shift that has been building in the clinical nutrition literature for several years: the standard dietary protein recommendation of 0.8 g/kg/day, which is based on nitrogen balance studies and designed to prevent deficiency rather than optimise muscle maintenance, is inadequate for older adults. The review finds that older adults require approximately 0.4 g/kg of body weight per meal to stimulate muscle protein synthesis — compared with 0.24 g/kg in younger adults — because of anabolic resistance: age-related reduction in the sensitivity of muscle protein synthesis pathways to amino acid and insulin signaling. This is not a disease state; it is a normal physiological shift that occurs from the fifth decade onward and requires a compensatory increase in per-meal protein dose to generate the same anabolic stimulus that a younger person achieves with a smaller dose.

The protein quality dimension is equally important and more practically actionable: not all grams of protein are equivalent for muscle protein synthesis. Leucine, an essential branched-chain amino acid, functions as the primary trigger for mTORC1 pathway activation — the signaling node that initiates muscle protein synthesis. A protein source can be adequate in total grams but insufficient in leucine content to reliably trigger this cascade. Animal-sourced proteins (whey, eggs, lean meat, fish) provide leucine at approximately 8–11% of total amino acid content and are highly digestible, delivering leucine rapidly and at concentrations sufficient to reach the threshold for mTORC1 activation. Plant proteins are typically lower in leucine (soy: ~8%, rice: ~8%, pea: ~8%), may contain anti-nutritional factors that reduce digestibility, and are frequently incomplete across other essential amino acids. This means vegetarian and vegan older adults need to both consume more total protein and more strategically combine protein sources to ensure consistent leucine delivery per meal — a population-level gap the review identifies as underaddressed in clinical practice.

Protein Quality Over Quantity: Leucine Threshold, Meal Distribution, and Digestibility Are the Critical Sarcopenia Prevention Variables — Frontiers in Nutrition, 2026
[Narrative Review]

A narrative review published in Frontiers in Nutrition (DOI: 10.3389/fnut.2026.1817891; 2026) titled “The role of protein quality and amino acid composition in preventing sarcopenia and functional decline in older adults” synthesized the full evidence base for how protein characteristics — not just protein grams — determine whether older adults can maintain muscle mass and prevent age-related functional decline.

Why 0.8 g/kg/day is insufficient: The current international protein RDA of 0.8 g/kg/day was derived from nitrogen balance studies and is calibrated to prevent deficiency, not to support muscle maintenance in aging. The review cites evidence showing that older adults experience anabolic resistance — a reduction in muscle protein synthesis responsiveness to amino acid stimulation — beginning in the fifth decade and worsening progressively. To overcome this blunted response, older adults require larger per-meal doses: the review endorses at least 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older adults and 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day for those with chronic disease, frailty, or active sarcopenia. More important than daily totals, the review emphasises meal distribution: protein should be spread across meals at a minimum of 0.4 g/kg per meal (e.g., ~30–40g per meal for a 75 kg adult) rather than concentrated at dinner — the typical Western dietary pattern that provides inadequate protein at breakfast and lunch while overshooting at the evening meal.

The leucine mechanism: Leucine acts as both a substrate and a signaling molecule for mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) activation — the master anabolic signaling node that triggers muscle protein synthesis. There is a leucine threshold effect: below approximately 2–3 g of leucine per meal, mTORC1 is not reliably activated even if total protein intake is adequate. Whey protein is approximately 10–11% leucine by amino acid content (roughly 2.5 g leucine per 25 g serve). Eggs are approximately 8–9% leucine. Lean beef and chicken are 7–8% leucine. This means a standard 25 g serve of whey or a 3-egg breakfast reliably crosses the leucine threshold; a 15 g serve of pea protein (8% leucine = 1.2 g leucine) likely does not. For plant-based older adults, the review recommends either larger serving sizes of plant protein or leucine-enriched formulations to ensure threshold activation is achieved at each meal.

Digestibility matters independently of amino acid content: The PDCAAS (Protein Digestibility-Corrected Amino Acid Score) and DIAAS (Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score) systems rank protein sources by both amino acid completeness and real-world digestibility. Whey protein scores 1.09 (DIAAS > 1.0 = excellent); whole eggs score 1.05; beef scores ~0.99; pea protein scores ~0.82; rice protein scores ~0.59. The digestibility difference means that even with equivalent amino acid profiles on paper, lower-DIAAS protein sources deliver less bioavailable amino acids per gram consumed. For older adults with reduced gastric acid production and slower gut motility (both common with aging), digestibility differences between protein sources are amplified — further disadvantaging plant-based sources unless consumed in larger quantities.

Supplement stack supported by evidence: The review endorses combining adequate protein intake with: creatine monohydrate 3–5 g/day (supports phosphocreatine energy substrate for muscle and brain simultaneously — see BIO-054 for full evidence base); vitamin D 1,000–4,000 IU/day (widespread deficiency in aging adults, impairs muscle protein synthesis at the receptor level when deficient); omega-3 fatty acids 2–4 g/day EPA+DHA (anti-inflammatory, shown in some RCTs to partially reverse anabolic resistance when combined with resistance training); resistance training as the non-negotiable co-intervention — no supplement stack meaningfully substitutes for the mechanical loading stimulus that drives muscle protein synthesis pathways.

Source: Frontiers in Nutrition · DOI: 10.3389/fnut.2026.1817891 · 2026 · Narrative review of protein quality and amino acid composition for sarcopenia prevention in older adults · PDCAAS / DIAAS scoring system · mTORC1 leucine threshold mechanistic review
Protocol Upgrade
  • Target 0.4 g/kg per meal minimum (not just daily total). For a 75 kg adult: ~30 g protein per meal, spread across breakfast, lunch, and dinner. The Western pattern of protein-light breakfast and protein-heavy dinner is suboptimal for muscle protein synthesis
  • Prioritise leucine content per meal: a 25 g serve of whey (~2.5 g leucine), 3 whole eggs (~2.0 g leucine), or 150 g of lean beef (~3.0 g leucine) reliably crosses the mTORC1 activation threshold. Check that plant-based meals reach the same leucine level, not just total protein
  • For plant-based eaters: combine complementary proteins per meal (rice + pea, for example) or use leucine-fortified plant protein products. Larger serving sizes are necessary to compensate for lower DIAAS scores and leucine density
Caveats
  • The 1.0–1.5 g/kg/day range is a research consensus target, not yet reflected in most official national dietary guidelines (which still use 0.8 g/kg/day). Clinicians may not proactively recommend higher protein for older adults without a sarcopenia diagnosis
  • Very high protein intake (above ~2.0 g/kg/day) has not demonstrated additional benefit for muscle protein synthesis in older adults and may place additional burden on renal clearance in individuals with compromised kidney function. Escalating to the upper range requires kidney function baseline
  • Supplement co-interventions (vitamin D, omega-3, creatine) without resistance training show substantially smaller benefits than the same supplements combined with mechanical loading. The training stimulus remains the primary driver
⚡ Implication: The shift from “how much protein” to “what kind and when” is the actionable update from the sarcopenia nutrition literature. Total daily protein above 1.0 g/kg/day is necessary but not sufficient — leucine content per meal, digestibility (DIAAS score), and meal distribution are independent variables that determine whether the anabolic signal actually fires. For older adults, vegetarians, and anyone eating breakfast with less than ~25–30g of protein, the gap between theoretical protein intake and biological muscle protein synthesis stimulus may be substantially larger than believed.

Protein RDA Update for Older Adults — Clinical Guidelines vs. Evidence Gap
The current international protein RDA of 0.8 g/kg/day is inadequate for older adults by a growing consensus in the nutritional gerontology literature — yet no major clinical guideline body (NIH, WHO, EFSA) has formally revised the recommendation for healthy older adults. Individual specialty societies (ESPEN for clinical nutrition, ASPEN for parenteral/enteral nutrition) have already moved to 1.0–1.5 g/kg/day for geriatric populations in clinical settings.
