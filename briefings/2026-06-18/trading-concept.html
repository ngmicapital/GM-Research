Alpha — Maker vs Taker

Wed · 18 Jun 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #53 11 MIN
TL;DR

Maker vs taker is the split that defines every fill: a maker posts a resting limit order and supplies liquidity; a taker crosses the spread with a market order and removes it. It matters because the maker earns the spread (often a fee rebate) while the taker pays for immediacy — and because makers widen or pull their quotes the instant adverse-selection risk rises, which is exactly when books thin and slippage punishes takers. Practical takeaway: in calm tape, take the fill if you need it; in fear-driven, deleveraging tape like today's BTC, the disappearing bids are makers stepping back — size down and lean on limits.

§01
What It Is
Two roles, one trade
Every print on the tape is a handshake between two roles: a maker who posted a resting order, and a taker who crossed the spread to hit it.

Every fill has two sides, not one. A maker is the trader who got there first — they posted a limit order that rested in the book, advertised a price, and waited. A taker is the trader who showed up impatient — they sent a market order (or a marketable limit) that crossed the spread and executed against the maker's resting size immediately. The maker supplied the liquidity; the taker consumed it. That single distinction is the bedrock under CVD, order-flow imbalance, absorption and every sweep we've covered this month — all of them are just patterns in who is making and who is taking.

As Larry Harris frames it, dealers "make themselves available so that other traders can trade when they want to trade. They supply liquidity." The maker is selling immediacy; the taker is buying it. The price of that immediacy is the bid–ask spread plus, on most venues, an explicit taker fee — while the maker is often paid a rebate for the service of quoting both sides of the book.

This builds directly on Trade Classification (Issue earlier this month), which tagged each print as buyer- or seller-initiated — that tag is literally "which side took." Today is the role beneath the tag: the economics and incentives that decide who posts and who crosses, and why the makers are the first to vanish when conditions sour.

§02
The Mechanics
Spread · rebate · adverse selection
The spread is the maker's wage for bearing two risks — inventory and adverse selection; raise either risk and the spread widens with it.

A maker quotes both sides — a bid below the mid and an ask above it. If the maker buys at the bid and later sells at the ask with no price drift, they pocket the spread. Do that thousands of times a day across roughly balanced flow and the spread becomes a wage for a service: continuous, two-sided quoting that lets everyone else trade on demand.

Maker gross edge ≈ (Ask − Bid) per round-trip  +  Maker rebate  −  infra / exchange cost
Taker cost ≈ (½ × Spread) crossing cost  +  Taker fee  +  Slippage if the order walks the book
Simplified, per fill. The maker's edge is gross of the two risks below; the taker's cost is the price of instant execution.

The maker is not printing free money. Two well-modelled risks eat the spread:

1. Inventory risk (the Ho–Stoll problem): if flow turns one-sided, the maker accumulates a position they never wanted and must hedge or unwind it — often at a worse price. 2. Adverse selection (the Glosten–Milgrom problem): some takers know something. When an informed taker lifts the offer right before a move, the maker is run over. To recover what they systematically lose to informed flow, the maker widens the spread on everyone — economists call that widening the adverse-selection spread. Uninformed takers paying the wider spread subsidise the maker's losses to the informed ones.

Exchanges codify all of this with the maker–taker fee model, borrowed from equities. Takers pay more; makers pay less or earn a rebate — some venues even run negative maker fees for high-volume desks. Per Binance Academy, makers "receive lower fees or rebates because they provide the liquidity that attracts traders," while takers "pay higher fees since they benefit from liquidity makers have already supplied." Exact numbers are venue- and volume-tier specific (taker fees commonly sit in the low single-digit basis points up to ~0.15%; maker fees lower or negative) — but the structure is the point: the venue pays you to build the book and charges you to drain it.

§03
Anatomy of a Fill
Who rests, who crosses
The order book is a ladder built entirely of makers; a taker is the order that climbs it.
A market BUY (taker) crosses the spread and consumes the resting best ask (a maker). The maker collects the spread/rebate for having quoted; the taker pays the fee and eats any slippage from walking up the ladder.
Same book, two regimes. When adverse-selection risk jumps, makers widen their quotes and pull resting size — the book thins and the same taker order travels far further. This is the §05 mechanism in one picture.
§04
In Practice
When to make, when to take
Default to maker when you have a level and time; pay the taker fee only when being filled now is worth more than the spread.
1

Post, don't chase

When you have a pre-planned level and patience, rest a limit at or just inside the book. You collect the spread/rebate and avoid paying for immediacy. The cost is fill uncertainty — your order may never trade, or fill only when you turn out to be wrong.

2

Take when immediacy > spread

If the move is happening and missing it costs more than the spread plus fee, cross it. Stops, breakouts and risk-off exits are taker events by nature — don't try to make liquidity into a cascade and watch your limit get left behind.

3

Read who's initiating

Aggressive prints lifting offers = takers paying up for immediacy. Passive size soaking them up = makers holding. Persistent taker buying into a flat tape that won't lift price is makers absorbing — the tell we covered in the Absorption issue.

4

Watch the makers leave

Thinning books, widening spreads and vanishing resting size mean makers are pricing adverse-selection risk higher. That's your cue to size down, widen stops, and stop assuming your limit will be there when you need to get out.

Worked example — making vs taking the same level

You want long BTC at the $64,400 support shelf. Two ways in:

Maker routeRest a bid at 64,400. Filled at 64,400, fee ≈ 0% (small rebate). Stop 63,900 (risk $500), target 65,400 (reward $1,000) → 2.0R, and you were paid to enter.
Taker routePrice already bouncing — market buy at 64,460 (paid the spread + ~0.05% fee ≈ $60 on 1 BTC). Same stop 63,900 (risk now $560), same target 65,400 (reward $940) → ≈1.7R.
The readThe maker route is ~0.3R better here — but only if you get filled. If price never trades 64,400 and runs, the maker earns 0R while the taker is already long. That trade-off — better price vs certain fill — is the whole decision.
Timeframe fit: maker logic shines on 15m–4h swing entries at pre-planned levels; taker logic dominates on 1m–5m momentum, stop-outs and exits where the fill matters more than the basis points.

§07
Common Traps
Where it goes wrong
The biggest mistake is treating the maker rebate as free money — it isn't; non-fills and adverse fills cost far more than a basis point of rebate.
1
Chasing the rebate

Posting passive purely to harvest the maker rebate ignores adverse selection: your resting orders often fill because informed flow chose to trade against you. A 0.01% rebate doesn't begin to cover being on the wrong side of the next move.

2
Assuming your limit will be there

In stress, makers pull. The bid you're relying on to exit can evaporate before your stop even triggers. Liquidity is a fair-weather friend — plan your exits for the thin book, not the deep one you see when nothing is happening.

3
Confusing taker volume with conviction

Aggressive taker buying that doesn't lift price isn't strength — it's makers absorbing. Read effort versus result, not raw aggression; heavy lifting that goes nowhere is supply quietly being distributed into the buyers.

4
Spread blindness on illiquid alts

On thin books the spread plus slippage dwarfs the headline fee. A 0.05% taker fee is noise next to a 0.8% spread you cross on a low-cap. Always price the spread you'll actually pay, not just the fee schedule.

5
Overhyped: "maker = smart, taker = dumb"

There is nothing inherently superior about making. Makers earn small, frequent edges and carry inventory and adverse-selection risk; takers pay for certainty and directional conviction. Professionals do both, deliberately. Maker vs taker is a cost-of-immediacy decision, not a skill hierarchy — anyone selling "always be the maker" is selling a fee hack, not an edge.
