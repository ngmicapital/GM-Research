Space destroys your red blood cells — and it never stops

A 2022 Nature Medicine study overturned 60 years of assumption about what space does to blood — and the answer is stranger and more alarming than anyone expected.

Astronauts' bodies destroy 54% more red blood cells in space than on Earth, and this accelerated destruction lasts the entire mission — not just the first few days as doctors assumed for six decades. Even a year after landing, cells are still being destroyed 30% faster than before flight.

TL;DR

Since the first crewed missions, astronauts have come back anemic. For six decades, medicine assumed it was a brief side effect: fluids shift headward in microgravity, the body jettisons a few surplus red blood cells to rebalance, and everything normalises within days. A 2022 Nature Medicine study of 14 ISS astronauts proved that story completely wrong. Hemolysis — the destruction of red blood cells — runs at 3 million cells per second throughout a 6-month mission (versus 2 million on Earth), five of thirteen astronauts land clinically anemic, and destruction rates remain 30% above preflight baselines a full year after splashdown. The mechanism is still not fully understood. The implications for Mars are serious.

Section 01

The Wrong Answer That Lasted Sixty Years

Every astronaut who has returned from a long-duration mission since the 1960s has come back slightly anemic. The medical establishment had an explanation that felt complete: when you enter microgravity, body fluids no longer pool in the legs — they redistribute upward toward the chest and head. The cardiovascular system senses what looks like too much blood volume, triggers a rapid purge of roughly 10 percent of red blood cells to compensate, and within 10 days everything normalises. The condition was considered a brief, self-correcting adaptation. Nobody was particularly worried about it.

The dominant name for the supposed mechanism was neocytolysis — the selective, short-lived destruction of the newest red blood cells (neocytes) in response to the fluid shift. It was plausible. It fit the available data. And it was not even close to what is actually happening.

Space anemia has consistently been reported when astronauts returned to Earth since the first space missions, but we didn't know why.
— Guy Trudel, Principal Investigator, MARROW Study (Ottawa Hospital, 2022)

Between 2015 and 2020, rehabilitation physician and researcher Guy Trudel at the Ottawa Hospital enrolled 14 astronauts — 11 men and 3 women — in the MARROW study aboard the International Space Station. The measurement method was elegant: when red blood cells are destroyed, the heme molecule (the iron-containing pigment that carries oxygen) breaks down and releases exactly one molecule of carbon monoxide as a byproduct. By analyzing astronauts' exhaled breath with a gas chromatograph before, during, and after their six-month missions, the team could calculate the rate of red blood cell destruction in real time, non-invasively, while the astronauts floated 400 kilometres above the Earth.

What they found demolished six decades of assumption. The rate of red blood cell destruction was not elevated for a few days then corrected. It was elevated from the moment of arrival in space — and stayed elevated for the entire six-month mission. The body was destroying 3 million red blood cells per second instead of the normal 2 million. Five of the thirteen astronauts whose blood was measured on landing were clinically anemic. One year after returning to Earth, destruction rates remained 30 percent above preflight baselines. The study, published in Nature Medicine in January 2022, did not tinker at the margins of the neocytolysis hypothesis — it invalidated it entirely.

Section 02

The Mechanism — What We Know, and What We Don't

Trudel's team measured two independent biomarkers of hemolysis throughout the missions: carbon monoxide in exhaled breath (baseline: ~1,662 ppb; in-flight: ~2,627 ppb — a 56% increase from day five onward) and serum iron markers (ferritin, transferrin saturation), which remained elevated throughout. Both tell the same story: the destruction of red blood cells is a primary, continuous effect of microgravity — not a transient response to fluid dynamics. The authors explicitly ruled out the neocytolysis mechanism: “Persistently increased CO elimination in space showed that hemolysis was not an acute adaptation to hemodynamic alterations upon entering microgravity.”

The breath test

One CO molecule is released every time a heme molecule is degraded. Trudel's team used a portable gas chromatograph to measure CO in astronaut breath before launch, monthly during the ISS mission, and at intervals for 12 months post-landing. The CO readings are the most direct real-time proxy for red blood cell destruction rate available without drawing blood. Preflight average: 1,662 ppb. In-flight average: 2,627 ppb (+56%). One year post-landing: still +30% above preflight.

What the study does not establish is precisely why microgravity triggers sustained hemolysis. The authors were candid: “The anatomic site and mechanism of space hemolysis remain unknown.” Several candidate mechanisms have been identified in the literature, none yet definitive. The leading proposals involve changes to the red blood cell membrane itself: in microgravity, RBC membranes accumulate more phosphatidylcholine and cholesterol, making them stiffer and less deformable. Normal red blood cells must squeeze through capillaries far narrower than their resting diameter — rigidity makes this increasingly difficult, leading the spleen to flag and destroy cells that can no longer deform on demand.

A second candidate pathway is oxidative stress. Microgravity appears to deplete glutathione (a key antioxidant) in red blood cells and increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) — free radicals that attack the cell membrane. Sphingomyelin, a membrane lipid, increases dramatically in space conditions (studies cite a 42:1 ratio under ROS exposure), triggering inflammatory signalling that further damages cell integrity. A third pathway involves the bone marrow itself: as marrow fat tissue (marrow adipose tissue) accumulates in microgravity, it may interfere with the production and release of healthy erythrocytes, leading to immature cells being destroyed before they fully mature. The true answer may be all three acting simultaneously.

The downstream consequence of sustained hemolysis creates a paradox that is dangerous in its own right. Breaking down extra red blood cells releases extra iron. Astronauts become anemic and iron-overloaded simultaneously. Serum ferritin rose by 70% in male cosmonauts and 220% in female cosmonauts within 15 days of launch in one study of 23 Russian/Soviet cosmonauts (Stein et al., 2012). Excess free iron catalyzes the formation of reactive oxygen species through Fenton chemistry — accelerating oxidative damage to other tissues. Research has directly linked this iron surplus to bone loss: elevated ferritin correlates with increased bone resorption and inhibited osteoblast activity, compounding the skeletal deterioration microgravity already causes through mechanical unloading.

Section 03

The Connections

The Mars problem. A six-month ISS mission already produces clinical anemia in roughly 40% of astronauts. A Mars transit is 7–9 months each way. The Trudel study found a dose-response relationship: severity and duration of anemia scaled with time in space. Epidemiological modelling predicts lower lifelong hemoglobin concentrations for every additional day in microgravity. An astronaut landing on Mars after a 9-month transit would step out onto another planet already weakened, with reduced oxygen-carrying capacity in their blood, at the exact moment they need physical exertion — suiting up, repairing equipment, managing the psychological shock of a new environment. There is no quick fix: recovery on Earth took three to four months, and that was with gravity working in the astronauts' favour.

The Earth medicine parallel. Trudel was direct about the clinical spillover: if we can understand what microgravity does to red blood cells, we may understand mechanisms relevant to hemolytic conditions on Earth. Sickle cell disease, hereditary spherocytosis, autoimmune hemolytic anemia — all involve accelerated red blood cell destruction through partly overlapping pathways. The iron overload paradox (anemia + elevated ferritin) also mirrors what clinicians see in chronic disease anemia and in patients on long-term dialysis. The space environment is effectively a fast-forward model of certain blood disorders, creating a population of otherwise healthy adults whose red blood cell biology is being altered in ways that precisely resemble pathology. Space medicine and hematology are converging research fields whether they intend to or not.

The space tourism question. The 14 astronauts in Trudel's study were career professionals in peak physical condition, carefully screened, medically monitored throughout, and resting in a weightless environment where anemia caused no symptoms (gravity is what makes low red blood cell counts feel like fatigue). Civilian space tourists have no such screening. Anyone with pre-existing anemia, cardiac conditions, or clotting disorders who takes a multi-day orbital flight would experience the same hemolytic effect with no mitigation. Trudel's team noted the implications explicitly: those with any kind of red blood cell problem should be warned and perhaps take specific measures. As ticket prices fall and passenger volumes rise, space anemia will move from an astronaut health concern to a public health screening question.

The measurement insight. The most underappreciated part of the MARROW study is methodological: a breath test measuring parts-per-billion CO resolved a question that blood draws from 60 years of missions had failed to answer. The prior literature was largely based on blood samples taken before and after flight — snapshots that masked the sustained in-flight signal entirely. This is a recurring pattern in medicine: phenomena that are continuous and gradual become invisible when you only measure at the endpoints. The equivalent exists in market data — measuring a portfolio's value at open and close can miss intraday volatility that determines whether positions survive. The tool determines what you see, and neocytolysis was a theory built on the wrong tool.

Further Down the Hole

🩸
The original MARROW study (Nature Medicine, 2022)

Trudel et al., 'Hemolysis contributes to anemia during long-duration space flight' — the primary source. Fourteen astronauts, six-month ISS missions, breath CO measurement throughout. Open access via PMC. Worth reading the supplementary data for the individual astronaut variance.

🦴
Iron, bones, and space: the downstream cascade

The PMC review 'Effects of Iron Overload and Oxidative Damage on the Musculoskeletal System in the Space Environment' (2018) maps the chain from hemolysis → free iron → ROS → bone loss. A compact piece on how anemia and osteoporosis in space share a common upstream cause.

🧪
The SPARK follow-up: why does it happen?

The Canadian Space Agency's SPARK experiment is a direct follow-on to MARROW, designed to identify the anatomical site and mechanism of space hemolysis — specifically whether it's intravascular (in blood vessels) or extravascular (in the spleen/liver). Results pending as of 2026.

🌌
Frontiers review: the full complexity

'Understanding the complexities of space anaemia in extended space missions' (Frontiers in Physiology, 2024) synthesises the oxidative stress, membrane rigidity, and bone marrow pathways into a single framework. Good for anyone wanting the full mechanistic picture beyond what a six-page Nature Medicine letter could contain.

💉
Earth disease parallel: hemolytic anemia

The Cleveland Clinic's overview of hemolytic anemia is a useful grounding in the Earth-side pathology that space is inadvertently modelling. The mechanisms — RBC membrane stiffness, oxidative attack, spleen-mediated destruction — map closely to what Trudel's team observes in astronauts.
