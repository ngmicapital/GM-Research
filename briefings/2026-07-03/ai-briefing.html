Model Landscape

The model everyone was already using anonymously turns out to be China's biggest bet yet on chip independence — and Grok 4.5 wants a rematch with Opus, this time behind closed doors.

Meituan unmasks "Owl Alpha" as LongCat-2.0 — the stealth model that was already winning

For roughly two months, an anonymous model called "Owl Alpha" quietly climbed usage leaderboards: first place on Hermes Agent by monthly call volume, second on Claude Code, third across OpenClaw deployments [paraphrased]. Meituan has now confirmed Owl Alpha is LongCat-2.0, a 1.6-trillion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts model it open-sourced June 30 under a permissive MIT license, with dynamic per-token activation ranging 33B–56B parameters. The detail that matters more than the parameter count: LongCat-2.0 is the first trillion-parameter-class model trained and served end-to-end on domestic Chinese AI ASICs, on a 50,000-chip cluster, with no Nvidia hardware anywhere in the pipeline. Benchmarks: 59.5% SWE-bench Pro (ahead of GPT-5.5's 58.6%), 70.8% Terminal-Bench 2.1, 77.3% SWE-bench Multilingual, and a 1M-token context window [paraphrased]. Meituan claims overall performance comparable to Gemini 3.1 Pro — self-reported, not yet independently verified on third-party leaderboards, so treat that specific claim as unconfirmed. Pricing is the real disruption: $0.75/$2.95 per Mtok input/output, with a 1-billion-token pack around $60 — meaningfully cheaper than GPT-5.5's $5/$30 or even Claude Sonnet 5's introductory $2/$10. The fact that real usage had already voted for this model before anyone knew its name or its chip provenance is the story worth sitting with.

Grok 4.5 enters private beta at SpaceX and Tesla — internal-only, unverified against Opus

Elon Musk announced on June 28 that Grok 4.5, built on a new 1.5-trillion-parameter V9 foundation model that completed pre-training May 26, is now running in private beta inside SpaceX and Tesla only — no public or API access yet [paraphrased]. Musk has claimed internally that Grok 4.5 performs close to, and on some evaluations ahead of, Claude Opus 4.8; these are xAI's own internal assessments, not independently verified, and the model has not been submitted to any public leaderboard. xAI says it plans a new foundation model trained from scratch every month through the end of 2026 — an aggressive cadence claim worth watching for whether it actually holds, since "trained from scratch" model-a-month is not a pace any other frontier lab has sustained.

Gemini 3.5 Pro still hasn't shipped — GA slips further into July with no firm date

Google's June GA target for Gemini 3.5 Pro (announced at I/O, targeting a 2M-token context window and a "Deep Think" reasoning mode) has now fully lapsed with the model still confined to limited Vertex AI enterprise preview [paraphrased]. No day-and-time release date has been set. This is now the second consecutive month of slippage on a model Google originally wanted live by June — worth flagging as a pattern rather than a one-off delay, especially set against Gemini 3.5 Flash's uncontested cost-leader position covered in prior issues.

Claude Ecosystem

Nothing new shipped from Anthropic today, but two details from the Claude Science launch and the enterprise push deserve a second look now that the initial coverage has settled.

Claude Science's actual deadlines: apply by July 15, awards by July 31, projects run September–December

Yesterday's issue covered Claude Science's launch and the up-to-$30,000-per-project credit structure; the operational detail worth adding is the actual calendar. Applications for the 50 AI-for-Science grant slots close July 15, 2026, award notifications go out by July 31, and funded projects run September 1 through December 1, 2026 [paraphrased]. Anthropic has been explicit that Claude Science "is not a new AI model and not a more capable model for biology" — it runs the same Claude models (including Opus 4.8) already generally available, with no special gating; the product is the workbench (one environment spanning databases, pipelines, and tooling, with every generated figure carrying its exact code, environment, and message history), not a new model. If you're evaluating whether to apply, the two-week application window is the actual constraint, not model access.

California becomes the largest US state-government Claude deployment

Governor Newsom announced California has partnered with Anthropic to make Claude available across all state agencies, cities, and counties through a centralized procurement framework, at 50% discounted pricing for state agencies, alongside a $15 million cyber-defense program for state, local, and tribal governments [paraphrased]. California and Texas are the first two states in this program. Named applications include DMV wait-time reduction and Medicaid workflow optimization. This is a meaningfully larger and more concrete public-sector commitment than the general "AI in government" framing that's circulated all year — a Fortune-scale procurement counterpart to the Azure Foundry GA covered in Tuesday's issue, and worth tracking as a template other states may follow given the discount structure.

Anthropic's enterprise services JV makes its first acquisition

Anthropic's private-equity-backed enterprise services venture (founding partners Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs, with Apollo, General Atlantic, GIC, Leonard Green, and Sequoia also invested, valued around $1.5B) acquired Fractional AI — an applied-AI deployment engineering firm that had previously partnered with OpenAI — pulling its integration talent into Anthropic's camp [paraphrased]. Terms weren't disclosed. Read this alongside OpenAI's parallel joint-venture push: both labs are now competing not just on models but on who does the hands-on work of actually wiring a model into a portfolio company's workflows — the deployment-engineering layer is becoming its own competitive front.

Bittensor proposes BIT-0011 Conviction Mechanism to fix subnet governance post-Covenant AI

Following April's Covenant AI exit (which accused Bittensor of "decentralization theatre"), co-founder Const has proposed a Conviction Mechanism requiring subnet owners to lock TAO for extended periods — months to years — in exchange for a decaying conviction score that determines voting rights and subnet-ownership privileges [paraphrased]. The notable wrinkle: this proposal was reportedly drafted by departing Covenant founder Sam Dare before his exit, which reads as unusually collaborative for a split that was framed publicly as adversarial. Initial rollout targets three mature subnets — 3, 39, and 81 — as the first test of whether locked-stake governance actually addresses the concentration concerns Covenant raised. TAO trades at $210.48, up 3.18% on the day, back above the $200 line flagged as at-risk in prior issues.

Teutonic subnet (formerly Templar) targets a 1T-parameter training run

Separately from the governance proposal, Const has outlined plans for the renamed Teutonic subnet to run a new large-scale training algorithm: a 30-billion-parameter training run within one month, building toward a 1-trillion-parameter model [paraphrased]. No confirmed completion date has been independently verified for the 1T target — treat this as a stated roadmap goal, not a delivered result, and worth checking back on given how aggressive the timeline is relative to what centralized labs need in dedicated compute.

Intel and Targon land first major enterprise Bittensor deployment via PwC

Open-Source Pulse

LongCat-2.0's reveal is this cycle's open-source headline — see full detail in §01 — and it's a reminder that the trending-repo leaderboard increasingly undercounts what's actually winning by usage.

LongCat-2.0's stealth run is a data point about how open-weight adoption actually happens now

The mechanics of how "Owl Alpha" climbed to top rankings on Hermes Agent, Claude Code, and OpenClaw usage charts before anyone knew its provenance (full story in §01) says something about 2026's open-weight market: routing layers and cost-sensitive agent pipelines will adopt a cheap, capable model anonymously and at scale well before it shows up in headline coverage or GitHub trending lists. If your team runs a multi-model router with automatic fallback-to-cheapest-capable, it's worth checking your own logs for how much traffic has already silently shifted to entrants like this one.

Deals & Regulation

An $800M raise for a company that rents Nvidia GPUs is the clearest sign yet that centralized AI-infrastructure capital isn't slowing down just because decentralized alternatives are maturing.

Together AI raises $800M Series C at $8.3B valuation, led by Aramco Ventures

Together AI — the neocloud that rents Nvidia GPU clusters and AI-specific infrastructure, specializing in hosting open-source models as a cheaper alternative to closed proprietary APIs — closed an $800 million Series C on July 1 at an $8.3 billion valuation, more than 2.5x its early-2025 Series B valuation of $3.3 billion [paraphrased]. Aramco Ventures led, with Vista Equity Partners, General Catalyst, Emergence Capital, Nvidia, March Capital, Pegatron, and SentinelOne's S Ventures participating. The company reports annual bookings exceeding $1.15 billion and counts Cursor, Cognition, and Decagon among its paying customers. Founded in 2022 by Vipul Ved Prakash, Percy Liang (Stanford), and Ce Zhang (ETH Zürich/University of Chicago). Read this against LongCat-2.0's chips-independent training run in §01: Together AI's entire business model depends on Nvidia scarcity and pricing power remaining real, which is exactly the assumption Chinese domestic-chip training runs are testing.

Baseten's $1.5B Series F confirms the inference-infrastructure land grab is broad, not just Together AI

Baseten — AI-application infrastructure spanning GPUs, autoscaling, observability, billing, and developer tools — closed a $1.5 billion Series F on June 22 across two tranches ($13B and $11B valuation respectively), landing at a $13 billion valuation. Led by Altimeter Capital, Conviction, and Spark Capital, with Sands Capital and Wellington Management co-leading. Revenue reportedly grew ~20x year-over-year, and the platform now processes over 1 billion inference calls daily across 87 clusters and 18 clouds [paraphrased]. Baseten plans to triple headcount this year. Between Together AI and Baseten, roughly $2.3B has flowed into AI-inference infrastructure in the past two weeks alone — capital intensity at the infrastructure layer shows no sign of cooling even as model-layer competition gets cheaper (see LongCat-2.0's pricing in §01).

Workflow & Tools

MCP's biggest-ever spec rewrite is now three weeks from locking — if your production MCP servers use Roots, Sampling, or Logging, this is the warning, not the surprise.

MCP 2026-07-28 release candidate enters final validation window

The Model Context Protocol's lead maintainers published the release candidate for spec version 2026-07-28 back in May, calling it "the largest revision of the protocol since launch"; the final specification locks July 28, giving SDK maintainers a roughly 10-week validation window that's now well past its midpoint [paraphrased]. Key changes: the protocol becomes stateless at the transport layer (self-contained requests replace session-ID routing, simplifying deployment behind load balancers and gateways), explicit application state moves into visible handles instead of hidden protocol sessions, a structured extensions framework with reverse-DNS identifiers replaces ad-hoc capability negotiation, authorization is rewritten around OAuth 2.1 and OpenID Connect, and tool definitions get full JSON Schema 2020-12 support. The part that actually breaks things: Roots, Sampling, and Logging are deprecated outright. Any MCP server or client relying on those three features needs a migration plan before July 28 — this is a harder deadline than the Devin Local cutover covered earlier this week, because it's a protocol-level change affecting every MCP integration, not one product's local agent.

Dynamic Workflows context, one month on from its May 28 research-preview launch

No new feature news on Dynamic Workflows in this window, but worth a status check now that it's had over a month in the wild: the feature (Claude Code v2.1.154+, all paid plans, plus Bedrock/Vertex/Foundry access) lets Claude write a JavaScript orchestration script on the fly rather than deciding turn-by-turn what to spawn, coordinating up to 1,000 total agents per execution with a max of 16 running concurrently [paraphrased]. It remains a research preview rather than GA — worth tracking for when Anthropic actually promotes it to default availability, since that's the signal it's considered production-stable rather than experimental.

Research Radar

Two new papers this cycle both attack the same underlying problem — agent memory that can't be audited after the fact — from opposite ends: one makes reasoning traces version-controllable, the other characterizes the systems cost of remembering at all.

GitOfThoughts: Version-Controlled Reasoning and Agent Memory You Can Replay, Diff, and Merge (arXiv 2606.14470)

Published June 12, this paper starts from a concrete complaint: LLM reasoning is ephemeral — chains of thought vanish with the context window, and memory buffers can't be diffed, merged, or audited after the agent has moved on [paraphrased]. Its proposed fix is almost provocatively literal: store an agent's reasoning tree as an actual git repository, where every scored thought becomes a commit, scores attach as git notes, outcomes become tags, and retrieval is just git log over the agent's own history. The appeal is that it reuses infrastructure every engineering team already trusts for exactly the audit problem (who decided what, when, and why) that's been hardest to solve natively in agent memory systems. Worth testing against a real long-horizon agent deployment before assuming the git metaphor holds at scale — commit-graph tooling wasn't built for the query patterns agentic retrieval needs.

Agent Memory: Characterization and System Implications of Stateful Long-Horizon Workloads (arXiv 2606.06448)

This June 4 paper takes the systems-engineering angle rather than the algorithmic one: as agents run longer between checkpoints, a real ecosystem of memory approaches has emerged — flat retrieval, LLM-mediated extraction, consolidating fact stores, and agentic control flows — and the paper characterizes what each actually costs in terms of latency, storage, and consistency guarantees under sustained multi-session use [paraphrased]. Useful as the practical companion to the March survey (arXiv 2603.07670, carried in prior issues) that mapped the mechanism taxonomy — this paper is closer to a systems benchmark, telling you what each memory architecture costs to actually run rather than just what it's capable of in principle. Read alongside GitOfThoughts above: one paper proposes a new mechanism, this one gives you the cost model to judge whether adopting it (or any alternative) is worth it at production scale.
