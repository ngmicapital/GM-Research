Alpha — Bid-Ask Bounce

Friday, 3 July 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue 67 11 min read
TL;DR

Bid-ask bounce is the mechanical zig-zag in trade prices that happens purely because buyers pay the ask and sellers hit the bid — the mid hasn't moved, but the last-price tape looks like it's oscillating, and that fake oscillation shows up as negative serial correlation in returns even in a perfectly efficient market. Richard Roll's 1984 model exploits exactly that artifact: since the bounce alone should produce negative covariance between consecutive price changes, you can invert the math and back out an implied effective spread from nothing but a time series of trade prices — no order book snapshot required. The practical edge isn't running Roll's formula by hand; it's recognizing bounce-driven noise on your own execution and refusing to mistake a spread-crossing reversal for a real momentum signal, especially on thin books where every fill briefly looks like a trend change.

§01
What It Is
The spread disguised as price action
Bid-ask bounce is trade prices mechanically alternating between bid and ask as buyers and sellers take turns crossing the spread — with zero change in underlying value.

Bid-ask bounce is the price tape lying to you about movement. Every trade prints at either the bid or the ask — a market buy lifts the ask, a market sell hits the bid — and when order flow alternates sides even slightly, the last-traded price alternates with it. Nothing about the asset's actual value changed between those two prints; the mid-price could be dead flat. But if you're only looking at last-trade price (which is what most retail charts, and most naive momentum signals, are built on), you see a saw-tooth pattern that looks exactly like short-term volatility. It isn't. It's the spread, worn as a costume.

This matters because the bounce is not a curiosity — it's a measurable statistical artifact with a name and a formula. Richard Roll noticed in 1984 that in an efficient market, consecutive price changes should be uncorrelated (today's move tells you nothing about tomorrow's). But real trade-price series consistently show negative serial correlation instead — a price move up is disproportionately followed by a move down, and vice versa. Roll showed this negative correlation isn't a market inefficiency to trade on; it's the bounce, mechanically produced by the spread itself, and its magnitude is a direct, invertible function of the spread's size.

The trader-relevant consequence splits two ways. First, defensively: any strategy that reads last-trade-price reversals as a directional signal is at risk of just re-discovering the bid-ask bounce and calling it edge — this is a well-documented false-momentum trap, especially on illiquid pairs or during low-volume hours. Second, constructively: because the bounce encodes the spread, you can measure execution cost from a plain trade-price series alone, without ever seeing the order book — useful for backtesting historical fills where you only have trade prints, not L2 depth.

§02
Mechanics
Turning a zig-zag into a spread estimate
Because the bounce alone creates negative correlation between consecutive price changes, Roll's 1984 model inverts that covariance to estimate the spread from trade prices alone, no book data needed.

Roll's insight was elegant precisely because it needs so little data. You don't need the order book, you don't need trade direction tags, you don't even need to know the quoted spread — just a time series of transaction prices. Here's the mechanical chain:

1. A trade happens at the ask (a buyer crosses the spread). 2. The next trade, absent any real news, is more likely to happen at the bid (a seller crosses back) than to happen at the ask again — because the market maker who was just paid the spread on the buy side is now quoting to capture it again on a sell. 3. Repeat this over thousands of prints and consecutive price changes show up negatively correlated — a rise tends to be followed by a fall of similar magnitude, purely mechanically. 4. Since the size of that negative correlation is driven by the size of the spread (a wider spread means a bigger mechanical bounce), you can solve for the spread if you know the correlation.

Effective Spread = 2 × √(−Cov(ΔPt, ΔPt−1))
where ΔPt is the price change from trade t−1 to trade t, and Cov is the serial covariance of consecutive price changes — the formula only produces a real number when that covariance is negative

That last clause is the model's most-cited limitation and the honest caveat every practitioner note repeats: Roll's estimator is undefined whenever the measured covariance comes out positive — you'd be taking the square root of a negative number. In real data (Roll's own 1984 sample and every replication since) this happens a meaningful fraction of the time, especially over short windows or trending regimes, because momentum, order clustering, and adverse selection dynamics all push covariance the other direction and can overwhelm the pure-bounce effect. The estimator is a clean idea with a well-known blind spot, not a black box you can trust blindly.

RegimeWhat serial covariance showsWhat it means

Crypto-specific research (see Further Reading) has since shown Roll's 1970s NYSE-equity assumption — that buy and sell probability at any given moment is roughly 50/50 — often doesn't hold on perpetual futures order books, where one side can dominate for extended stretches. The mechanism still exists; the clean 0.5/0.5 math underneath it doesn't always transfer.

§04
In Practice
Reading through the bounce, not with it
Never read your own recent fills as a momentum signal; a same-side print sequence at a stable spread is bounce, not conviction.
1

Work off mid-price, not last-trade, for signal generation

Any indicator built on last-trade price inherits the bounce as noise. Building signals off the quote midpoint (bid+ask)/2 removes the mechanical alternation entirely and leaves only real price movement.

2

Use the bounce to sanity-check your own fill quality

If your realized effective spread (via Roll's estimator or direct measurement) is consistently wider than the quoted spread you see on the book, you're paying more in adverse selection or slippage than the visible market suggests — a red flag on your execution venue or order type.

3

Treat a positive-covariance regime as a trend warning, not a data error

When Roll's formula goes undefined (positive serial covariance), that's not noise to discard — practitioner research reads it as a sign informed/directional flow has overwhelmed the mechanical bounce. That's often exactly when adverse selection risk for market makers, and slippage risk for you, spikes.

4

Widen your read during thin hours

Bounce amplitude scales with spread, and spread widens whenever depth thins — low-volume sessions, holidays, and off-peak hours all inflate the fake zig-zag. Don't apply the same last-price-reversal skepticism threshold at 3am Sydney that you'd apply during London/NY overlap.

Worked example — recognizing bounce vs. real reversal
SetupBTC quoting a tight $61,395–$61,415 spread through the 3 July US holiday session; last-trade tape prints seven alternating $20 swings in four minutes with zero net movement
Naive read (wrong)"Price is chopping violently, reduce size / flip bias every swing" — reacting to the last-trade sawtooth as if it were directional information
Bounce-aware read (correct)Mid-price across the same four minutes: $61,404 → $61,406 → $61,405 — essentially flat. The "chop" was buyers and sellers alternately crossing a stable spread, not a market making decisions
ActionHold existing bias; treat the last-price noise as expected market-making residue, not a signal to act on
Timeframe fit: the bounce is a tick/trade-level phenomenon — relevant to execution algos, market-making, and any 1m-or-faster signal; largely irrelevant once you're reading 15m+ candles where real price movement dwarfs a few ticks of spread noise.

§07
Common Traps
Where bounce-reading goes wrong
The single biggest mistake is treating last-trade-price reversals as a directional tell instead of the spread doing what spreads do.
1
Reading last-trade reversals as momentum signals

A print at the ask followed by a print at the bid is the single most common pattern the bounce produces — and the single most common false "reversal" retail momentum tools fire on. Confirm against the mid-price before trusting a last-trade-only signal.

2
Trusting Roll's estimator blindly across regimes

The formula is undefined whenever measured serial covariance is positive — which happens routinely in trending or momentum-driven windows. Treat a Roll spread estimate as regime-dependent, not a universal constant for an instrument.

3
Assuming crypto order books obey Roll's 1970s equity assumptions

Roll's clean math leans on roughly balanced buy/sell probability at any moment — an assumption practitioner research shows frequently breaks on perpetual futures books, where one side can dominate for extended stretches. The mechanism (bounce exists) survives the transfer to crypto; the precise 50/50 math underneath often doesn't.

4
Confusing bounce noise with real volatility in backtests

Computing realized volatility from raw last-trade prices at high frequency will systematically overstate true volatility because it's partly measuring spread-crossing noise, not price discovery. Use mid-price or trade-classified series for anything volatility-sensitive at short horizons.

5
[Overhyped] Treating Roll's estimator as a modern execution-quality tool

Roll's 1984 model is elegant and historically important, but it's a 40-year-old estimator built for daily-close equity data with known large-variance problems — Roll's own paper reports the squared spread comes out negative (i.e. the model breaks) in roughly half of yearly samples. For actual execution-cost measurement today, direct effective-spread calculation from timestamped trade and quote data (which crypto exchanges expose) is more accurate and far more available than it was in 1984. Know the estimator for what it teaches about the bounce mechanism, not as your primary cost-measurement tool.
