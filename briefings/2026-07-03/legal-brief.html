Update MiCA’s Morning After: Just 210 of 1,200+ EU Crypto Firms Cleared the Licensing Bar

"No waivers or deadline extensions will be granted. Unlicensed platforms must stop processing new transactions immediately after July 1." Comisión Nacional del Mercado de Valores (CNMV), Spain — statement, 29 June 2026 [paraphrased from CNMV guidance as reported]

Tuesday’s MiCA deadline was the headline; Thursday’s numbers are the story. Of the 1,200-plus firms that had been operating under national registration regimes (the old French PSAN model and its equivalents), only around 210 — roughly 17% — had converted to full Crypto-Asset Service Provider authorisation by the time the transitional window closed. Germany leads with 56 licences, the Netherlands 26, France 21; ten member states, including Poland, a major hub under the prior national regime, issued none at all.

That leaves roughly 1,000 firms in breach of EU law from 1 July. ESMA’s April supervisory statement gave them five options: obtain a licence, cease operating, wind down in an orderly fashion under AML/CFT-compliant plans, transfer clients to an authorised CASP, or merge with a licence-holder. Spain’s CNMV has already said publicly there will be no grace period. Maximum fines run to €15 million or 12.5% of annual turnover, whichever is greater, and France’s AMF has flagged criminal exposure for firms that keep operating without authorisation.

Binance is the marquee case: it withdrew its Greek application on 21 June after signalling the Hellenic Capital Market Commission was set to reject it, and is now in direct talks with France’s AMF with what it calls a "technically ready" filing — complicated by the fact that Changpeng Zhao, who owns roughly 90% of the exchange and pleaded guilty to US money-laundering violations in November 2023, is unlikely to clear MiCA’s fitness-and-propriety bar, and Binance is separately under French criminal investigation for aggravated money laundering. Tether’s USDT, meanwhile, has been delisted across the EEA entirely; Circle’s USDC and EURC remain the only top-ten stablecoins fully MiCA-compliant.

📎 Historical Precedent: Mirrors MiFID II’s 2018 implementation crunch, where a large share of investment firms missed the compliance deadline and national regulators applied uneven grace periods — the same fragmentation risk ESMA is now trying to head off by publicly ruling out waivers.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

A 17% clearance rate this low turns MiCA from a licensing regime into a market-structure event: five out of six previously-registered firms are now either winding down, migrating clients, or operating illegally. Norton Rose Fulbright’s disputes teams have already flagged 2026 as a year for digital-asset enforcement litigation, and this is where it starts — national regulators moving from guidance to fines within weeks, not months, given ESMA’s explicit no-extension stance. The Brief’s read: expect the first MiCA enforcement fines by late July, concentrated in jurisdictions (Spain, France) that have been most vocal about zero tolerance.

🔄 Contrarian Take

OKX’s Europe chief has argued publicly that up to 80% of crypto exchanges won’t survive MiCA regardless of licensing status, framing the shakeout as intentional consolidation rather than a compliance failure — a smaller, fully-licensed EU market was always the point of an 18-month transition window. The Brief’s read: that framing undersells the poll’s real signal: ten member states issuing zero licences suggests capacity-constrained national regulators as much as unprepared firms, which is a supervisory problem ESMA will have to answer for in its own review.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Coinbase, Kraken and OKX are the visible winners, holding EU-wide passporting rights while unlicensed rivals scramble. OKX is running an 8% deposit bonus specifically targeting users displaced from unlicensed platforms. Tether’s EEA delisting is a direct hit to euro-area liquidity for the largest stablecoin by market cap, pushing volume toward MiCA-compliant USDC and EURC. The European Commission’s own targeted MiCA review consultation, open to 31 August, will now be shaped by a compliance failure rate regulators cannot ignore.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish COIN, and OKX/Kraken by extension — direct beneficiaries of forced EU client migration from unlicensed rivals.

🔴 Bearish USDT/Tether EEA liquidity, and any firm still relying on a national PSAN-style registration to serve EU clients.

⚪ Neutral CRCL — USDC’s compliant status is a structural tailwind, but it’s fighting the separate Open USD Coalition threat to reserve-yield economics.

📋 What To Do — Before 1 July
TraderConfirm your EU-facing exchange holds a live CASP licence today, not a pending application — check the ESMA public register directly.
CounselFor any client still unlicensed, document a wind-down or client-transfer plan now; "still processing our application" is not a defence after 1 July.
ComplianceAudit exposure to Tether/USDT in EEA-facing products — the delisting is immediate, not phased.
FounderIf your MiCA application is still pending in a slow-moving member state, evaluate re-filing through a faster jurisdiction (Germany, France, Netherlands) before enforcement actions begin.

Update Trump’s CBDC-Ban Standoff Nears Automatic Enactment as the 10-Day Clock Runs Out

"[W]e pass the desperately needed SAVE AMERICA ACT, which I consider to be a National Emergency." President Donald Trump — social media post, 24 June 2026

The four-year Federal Reserve CBDC ban that passed Congress on 22 June by veto-proof margins (Senate 85-5, House 358-32) as part of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act landed on Trump’s desk around 29 June, and as of this week he still hasn’t signed it. Trump is instead tying his signature to the Safeguarding American Voter Eligibility (SAVE) Act, a documentary-proof-of-citizenship voting bill that failed in the Senate on 4 June by a 48-50 vote when Republicans Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski, Mitch McConnell and Thom Tillis joined Democrats to block it.

The mechanism matters more than the politics here: the Constitution gives the president 10 days, excluding Sundays, to act on a bill once presented. If Congress stays in session and Trump does neither — sign nor veto — the bill becomes law automatically, exactly as if he had signed it. TD Cowen policy analyst Jaret Seiberg has said flatly "there is no path for the SAVE Act becoming law" given the Senate filibuster math, meaning Trump’s leverage play is very likely to end with the CBDC ban enacted anyway, just without his signature and roughly a week later than the bill’s sponsors intended.

📎 Historical Precedent: Echoes past instances where a president let veto-proof legislation lapse into law rather than sign it — a symbolic protest with no practical effect on the statute’s force.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

For stablecoin issuers and Fed-adjacent payments infrastructure, the practical outcome is unchanged regardless of how the ban lands: no retail CBDC through 2030, reinforcing the GENIUS Act’s private-issuer stablecoin model as the only game in town for digital-dollar rails. The Brief’s read: counsel should treat the ban as functionally certain to take effect within days and advise clients accordingly — the political theatre around the SAVE Act is a distraction from a settled legal outcome.

🔄 Contrarian Take

A veto remains legally available to Trump even this late, and if exercised, Congress would need to hold override votes in both chambers — procedurally straightforward given the margins, but not instantaneous, meaning a veto could still delay enactment by weeks rather than days. The Brief’s read: a veto is the higher-friction, lower-probability path; Trump’s public statements read as leverage-seeking rather than genuine opposition to the underlying ban.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Circle, Open Standard’s Open USD coalition (Visa, Mastercard, Stripe, Coinbase, BlackRock), and every GENIUS Act-licensed stablecoin issuer benefit from a codified four-year moratorium on Fed retail-CBDC competition. Blockchain Association and Coin Center, both vocal CBDC-ban advocates, get their win regardless of the signature mechanics.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish Circle, Open Standard/Open USD coalition members, and GENIUS Act-licensed stablecoin issuers — a codified retail-CBDC moratorium removes a long-run competitive threat.

🔴 Bearish None directly — the ban’s enactment (by signature or lapse) was already priced as near-certain given the veto-proof margins.

⚪ Neutral Fed policy generally — a CBDC ban through 2030 doesn’t alter near-term monetary policy, only long-run digital-currency infrastructure options.

📋 What To Do — Before 1 July
TraderTreat the CBDC ban as functionally enacted for positioning purposes — the signature standoff is unlikely to change the outcome.
CounselConfirm the enactment date once the bill either gets signed or lapses into law around 9 July, since that date starts the four-year ban’s clock.
ComplianceNo immediate action required — this affects Fed-level CBDC issuance, not private stablecoin issuer obligations under GENIUS.
FounderFactor a locked-in four-year absence of Fed retail-CBDC competition into any digital-dollar-rail product roadmap.

EU AI Act’s Article 50 Transparency Duties Land in 30 Days — and the Digital Omnibus Didn’t Save You

"If your AI governance program treated August 2, 2026 as fully relieved, you have a compliance gap opening in under two months." ComplianceHub.Wiki analysis, drawing on the European Commission’s June 2026 draft guidelines [paraphrased]

The EU’s Digital Omnibus, provisionally agreed 7 May, has been widely read as a blanket delay of AI Act obligations — and for standalone high-risk systems under Annex III, that’s correct: enforcement is deferred to December 2027. But Article 50, the AI Act’s horizontal transparency regime, was not touched. It still takes effect 2 August 2026, thirty-one days from today, and it applies regardless of an AI system’s risk classification.

Article 50 requires that chatbots and virtual assistants disclose to users that they’re interacting with AI, at the latest at first interaction; that emotion-recognition and biometric-categorisation systems disclose their operation to affected individuals; and that generative AI providers mark synthetic text, image, audio and video output in a machine-readable, detectable format. A grandfathering rule gives systems already on the market before August 2026 until December 2026 for the watermarking piece specifically — but the disclosure obligations themselves are not deferred. Penalties reach €15 million or 3% of global turnover.

📎 Historical Precedent: Structurally similar to GDPR’s 2018 rollout, where general transparency/consent obligations took effect on schedule even as sector-specific guidance and enforcement priorities were still being finalised — "the deadline moved" was a widespread and costly misreading then too.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The European Commission’s Code of Practice, published 10 June, and its draft Article 50 guidelines are the operative documents right now, not the Digital Omnibus press coverage. Firms that deployed a customer-service chatbot years ago and never mapped it to AI Act scope are the highest-risk population — Article 50 transparency is cited as the second most common compliance trigger across surveyed organisations, affecting roughly a third of respondents. The Brief’s read: the Digital Omnibus’s real function was managing political optics around high-risk AI, not softening the transparency regime that most consumer-facing companies will actually feel first.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Some compliance advisers argue enforcement in the first months post-2 August will be lenient in practice, given the Commission’s own late guidance and the Code of Practice’s voluntary, not-yet-binding status for many provisions — a grace-period-in-substance if not in law. The Brief’s read: relying on informal enforcement leniency is a weak position given the statute’s text is unambiguous and national regulators, not the Commission, will run first-instance enforcement with varying appetite for restraint.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Every consumer-facing AI deployer with EU exposure — from crypto-exchange support chatbots to trading-signal generators using synthetic voice or video — needs an Article 50 gap assessment now. Generative-AI infrastructure providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) face pressure to build watermarking and disclosure tooling directly into their APIs so downstream deployers inherit compliance rather than building it themselves.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish AI-content-provenance and watermarking vendors with EU-ready tooling ahead of the 2 August deadline.

🔴 Bearish Consumer-facing fintech/crypto platforms with undisclosed AI chat or synthetic-media features and no EU compliance mapping in progress.

⚪ Neutral Pure B2B/enterprise AI tooling without direct EU consumer interaction — lower immediate Article 50 exposure.

📋 What To Do — Before 1 July
TraderNote that AI-generated trading commentary or synthetic-voice content aimed at EU users will need machine-readable disclosure labelling from 2 August.
CounselRun an Article 50 applicability check across every client-facing chatbot, voice assistant, and generative-content feature with EU reach, independent of the client’s high-risk classification.
ComplianceConfirm your disclosure banner or first-message notice for any AI chat interface meets the "before or at the first interaction" timing requirement.
FounderBudget for watermarking infrastructure now — the December 2026 grace period only covers systems already live before August, not anything you ship between now and then.

SEC and CFTC Open a Second Harmonization Front: Portfolio Margining Joins Swap Definitions on the Docket

"By further harmonizing our frameworks, we can ensure that jurisdictional overlap does not stifle innovation and efficiency." Paul S. Atkins, Chairman, U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission — SEC Press Release 2026-59, 26 June 2026

On 26 June the SEC and CFTC issued a joint request for public comment on harmonizing portfolio-margining and cross-margining frameworks across securities, security-based swaps, futures, swaps and related positions — published in the Federal Register on 30 June, with a 60-day comment window closing 31 August. CFTC Chairman Mike Selig framed it as an effort to "unleash untapped capital while ensuring a more robust risk management framework and market protections." The request itself does not name crypto or digital assets, but it lands two weeks after the CFTC’s 12 June no-action letter (26-19) letting Bitnomial Exchange and Coinbase Derivatives convert existing perpetual-style digital-commodity futures into true, expiration-free perpetual futures — the first onshore U.S. approval of a product class that has developed almost entirely offshore.

Together, the two actions extend Project Crypto’s pattern: joint SEC-CFTC processes are now running in parallel on swap-product definitions (comment period closing around 27 July), portfolio margining (31 August), and, separately but on the same institutional-infrastructure theme, crypto derivatives collateral treatment. None of these is crypto-specific by name, but all three shape the capital-efficiency environment crypto derivatives desks will operate in once true perpetuals go live onshore.

📎 Historical Precedent: Recalls the SEC-CFTC’s unsuccessful 2000s-era attempts at product-definition harmonization under the Commodity Futures Modernization Act era, which stalled on turf disputes for years — this round has explicit White House-level Project Crypto backing, a materially different political posture.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Gibson Dunn’s derivatives practice has been tracking this as part of a broader Q2-Q3 2026 wave of joint SEC-CFTC rulemaking activity; the portfolio-margining request specifically targets collateral efficiency for firms running both securities and derivatives books — directly relevant to any crypto-native prime broker or exchange seeking to offer cross-margined products across its securities-registered and CFTC-registered entities. The Brief’s read: the absence of explicit crypto language in the request is not a signal of irrelevance; expect crypto trading firms and exchanges to be among the most active commenters given the direct read-through to perpetual-futures margin efficiency.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Skeptics note the SEC and CFTC have announced multiple "harmonization" comment requests in 2026 without yet finalizing a single joint rule, and ARK Invest-style critics of regulatory process point to the 2000s CFMA-era harmonization push as a cautionary tale of comment requests that never became binding rules. The Brief’s read: the difference this time is explicit coordinated agency leadership (Atkins and Selig jointly signing statements) rather than competing turf claims, which historically is the actual blocker to finalization.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Coinbase Derivatives and Bitnomial are the immediate beneficiaries of the perpetual-futures no-action relief and stand to benefit further from any portfolio-margining harmonization that lets institutional clients net collateral across spot, futures and perpetual crypto positions. CME, mid-litigation with the CFTC over its own crypto perpetual-futures product (case 1:26-cv-02157), will be watching the margining docket closely given its direct bearing on competitive positioning against the newly-cleared DCM perpetuals.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish COIN (Coinbase Derivatives), Bitnomial — first movers on true onshore crypto perpetuals, positioned to benefit from any future margin-efficiency gains.

🔴 Bearish CME — competitive pressure from newly-cleared DCM perpetuals compounds its existing litigation exposure over crypto perpetual-futures treatment.

⚪ Neutral Traditional securities/derivatives prime brokers — the margining request is broad market-structure plumbing, not a crypto-specific catalyst yet.

📋 What To Do — Before 1 July
TraderWatch for follow-on margin-efficiency changes at Coinbase Derivatives and Bitnomial as the true-perpetuals conversion beds in post-12 June.
CounselFlag the 31 August portfolio-margining comment deadline to any client running cross-registered securities and derivatives books touching crypto.
ComplianceReview whether your firm’s crypto perpetual-futures products fall within Bitnomial/Coinbase Derivatives’ 12 June no-action conditions before relying on them.
FounderConsider submitting a comment on the portfolio-margining request if cross-margining crypto and traditional collateral is on your product roadmap.

California’s DFAL Shows Its Teeth: $100,000-a-Day Penalties Now Live for Unlicensed Digital-Asset Business

"This enforcement action should send a strong message to kiosk operators that California means business when it requires digital asset companies to follow the rules that help prevent scammers from taking advantage of unsuspecting Californians." KC Mohseni, Commissioner, California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation — DFPI press release, Coinme consent order

California’s Digital Financial Assets Law took effect 1 July, and its enforcement mechanics are now the story rather than the licensing deadline itself. Every exchange, custodian, stablecoin issuer, and Bitcoin ATM operator serving California residents must hold a Department of Financial Protection and Innovation licence or a complete pending application, or face civil penalties up to $100,000 per day. The DFPI already has a live precedent: a June 2025 consent order against Seattle-based ATM operator Coinme, which paid a $300,000 penalty after allowing customers to exceed the law’s $1,000-per-day transaction limit.

Applications have run through the Nationwide Multistate Licensing System since 9 March, giving firms roughly sixteen weeks to prepare before the deadline; the DFPI has signalled a baseline $100,000 tangible-net-worth requirement and a $500,000 surety bond, both scalable to a firm’s risk profile.

📎 Historical Precedent: Modelled closely on New York’s BitLicense regime, which similarly used a state money-transmission-adjacent framework with steep daily penalties to force industry consolidation around a smaller set of licensed operators.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Morrison Foerster’s DFAL guidance has flagged the Coinme consent order as the template for what DFPI enforcement will look like going forward: narrow, transaction-limit-based violations rather than sweeping unlicensed-activity sweeps, at least initially. The Brief’s read: the $100,000-per-day figure is a ceiling designed for deterrence, not a number DFPI is likely to charge in full against a first-time filer with a good-faith pending application — but firms with no application on file at all are exposed to it immediately.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Bitcoin ATM operators are the most exposed segment given the Coinme precedent and DFAL’s specific transaction-limit provisions; national exchanges with existing money-transmitter licences in other states face comparatively lighter lift. Ripple’s RLUSD California status remains formally unconfirmed as of this week, per DFPI’s public licensee list, leaving a live compliance question for any California-facing RLUSD distribution.
