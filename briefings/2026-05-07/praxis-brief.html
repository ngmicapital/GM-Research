Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

Four sources converge on a single discipline this week — identify the one thing that matters and protect attention for it. Daily Stoic deploys Don Draper's line ("I don't think about you at all") as a Stoic refusal to let antagonists rent mental space. Tuesday's Stoic essay reframes amor fati through Admiral Stockdale at the Hanoi Hilton: the part to love is not the tragedy but the version of yourself that emerges from it. Cal Newport applies Goldratt's Theory of Constraints to knowledge work — optimising anywhere but the bottleneck produces only pile-ups and distraction. Elad Gil on the Tim Ferriss Show: "What is the one thing I need to believe? If it's three things, it's too complicated."

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Daily Stoic · Ryan Holiday · 6 May 2026

"They're Not Thinking About You At All" — Don Draper as Stoic Discipline

Holiday's Wednesday entry takes one of the most enduring questions in moral philosophy — how to relate to people who have wronged you — and routes it through a piece of pop-cultural shorthand that lands harder than the classical sources usually do. The opening is unflinching: "They're awful. They're frustrating. They need to be stopped. These are the people who take up so much space in our head. We stew about them. We fret about them. We plot against them." The setup names the problem honestly before the resolution arrives.

"I feel bad for you."
"I don't think about you at all." — Michael Ginsberg and Don Draper, Mad Men (S5E10, "Christmas Waltz")

The exchange is doing real work. Ginsberg, the young copywriter, has spent months building a case against Draper in his own head — the slights, the indifference, the perceived betrayals. The case has consumed him. When he finally lands the line he has been rehearsing — "I feel bad for you" — Draper's response demolishes the entire structure with seven words. The injury that has reorganised Ginsberg's interior life is not even a memory in Draper's. The asymmetry is the lesson.

Holiday's gloss connects the line to the Stoic case studies: "as Nero surely did for Seneca, or Caesar and Cicero did for Cato." The pattern repeats across centuries. The antagonist whose presence dominates the philosopher's mind is, on the antagonist's side, an afterthought at best. Seneca's letters return obsessively to Nero; Nero's letters do not return at all. The energy flowed in one direction, and the cost was paid by the wiser man.

The discipline Holiday extracts is harder than the Stoic boilerplate suggests. It is not "forgive them"; it is not "rise above"; it is not even "let it go". It is colder and more useful: refuse to let them have rent-free space in your head when they are not paying it any attention themselves. The taking-it-personally is what extends the injury. The Stoic move is to recognise that in most cases, the original act was not about you in the first place — the person was acting from their own pattern, and you happened to be in the path of it. Carrying the grudge is volunteering for a sentence the antagonist never even noticed handing out.

The instruction Holiday closes with is direct: "They aren't thinking about us. We should not let them make us think too much about them. Let them be who they are. You be who you are. Save your energy for what matters." The last sentence is the operating principle. Mental real estate is finite, and every square foot rented to an antagonist is one not available for craft, family, the long arc. The audit is uncomfortable because most professionals discover they are paying the rent on three or four such properties at any given time.

Daily Stoic · Ryan Holiday · 5 May 2026

"This Is The Part To Love" — Stockdale at the Hanoi Hilton and the Real Meaning of Amor Fati

Holiday's Tuesday essay is the sharper of the two and answers a question the popular Stoic literature has been ducking for years. The opening is unusually honest about what the doctrine seems to ask: "The terrible diagnosis. The economic calamity. The trauma you experienced as a young person. The betrayal. The broken heart. Are the Stoics really saying you should love that?" The honest reader has been wondering this since the first time they encountered amor fati, and most popularisations evade the question. Holiday names it.

His answer is the one move that finally makes the doctrine workable. The part to love is not the tragedy. The part to love is yourself — yourself in and after the tragedy. This is a structural reframe. The Stoic instruction is not "develop affection for your suffering"; it is "develop affection for the version of you that meets the suffering and is changed by it". The two are easy to confuse and the difference is everything.

The case study Holiday chose is, again, sharper than the standard examples. Not Marcus on the throne but Vice Admiral James Stockdale, who spent seven and a half years as a prisoner of war in the Hanoi Hilton, four of them in solitary confinement, two in leg irons. Stockdale survived by, among other things, having read Epictetus's Enchiridion in graduate school at Stanford and having internalised the Stoic distinction between what is and is not in one's control. When he was shot down over Vietnam in 1965, he reportedly muttered to himself as he ejected: "I'm leaving the world of technology and entering the world of Epictetus."

"His time in bondage offered a chance at greatness, a chance at courage, a chance at justice, a chance at strength, a chance at wisdom — a chance to test the ideas of the Stoics, as he said, in the laboratory of human behavior." — Ryan Holiday, summarising Stockdale (Daily Stoic, 5 May 2026)

The Stockdale framing is doing two things at once. First, it inverts the question of what the prison cost him into the question of what the prison offered him — not because the cost was small but because the offer was the only side of the ledger that was responsive to action. Second, it reframes the testing-of-philosophy as the work itself, not as preparation for some later life when conditions improve. The seven and a half years were the laboratory. Stockdale's later observation — that he would not, in retrospect, trade that time away — is not denial; it is the recognition that the version of himself that emerged was the version he came to value most, and could not have been produced by any other instrument.

The translation to less extreme circumstances is uncomfortably direct. The redundancy, the failed marriage, the diagnosis, the betrayal — the Stoic question is not "can I learn to love this?", which is unanswerable for most operators most of the time. The Stoic question is "can I love the version of myself that meets this and is changed by meeting it?", which is answerable in principle and produces an actionable response: meet it well. The doctrine stops being a demand for emotional gymnastics and becomes an instruction about character formation under fire. The thing to love is what you become.

Holiday's closing line is the cleanest articulation: "It's not fair, it's not fun, but it is that chance. If there is anything to love, love that." The "that" is the chance, not the suffering. The two are easy to elide and impossible to trade. The operator who internalises the distinction stops resenting the difficulty and starts using it.

Essay · Cal Newport · calnewport.com · 4 May 2026

"On Bottlenecks and Productivity" — Goldratt's Theory of Constraints, Imported into Knowledge Work

Newport's Monday post is the most useful thing he has published in months and it lands precisely because it borrows from outside his usual bibliography. The frame comes from Eliyahu Goldratt's 1984 industrial classic The Goal, surfaced again in David Epstein's recent Inside the Box: the Theory of Constraints. Every system has a single limiting factor — the bottleneck — and improvements anywhere else in the system produce no overall gains. They produce, at best, larger pile-ups in front of the bottleneck.

Newport's example is deliberately concrete. A backyard chicken coop assembly line. If the slowest step is the roofing, no amount of speeding up the frame-building will increase the rate at which finished coops emerge. The frames will simply stack up faster behind the same unmoving roof crew. This is obvious in a factory and counter-intuitive in a knowledge-work context, where the dominant productivity vocabulary — "efficiency", "speed", "throughput" — quietly assumes the bottleneck does not exist.

"Don't seek speed, or efficiency, or the avoidance of hard things. What ultimately matters more than anything else is how well we perform the deep steps that actually move the needle." — Cal Newport, "On Bottlenecks and Productivity" (4 May 2026)

The diagnosis Newport reaches for is the one his readers will recognise from Deep Work and Slow Productivity — but the bottleneck framing sharpens it considerably. Email and AI office tools, in his read, are tools that optimise everything except the bottleneck. Faster correspondence does not produce more value; it produces more correspondence and more pile-ups in front of the actual deep work that creates value. "Workslop" is his coinage for the AI variant: a higher volume of mediocre output that still does not move the system's true rate-determining step. Newport calls email an "accidental disaster" on these grounds: it sped up everything but the part that mattered.

The implication for personal productivity is contrarian to the dominant 2020s discourse. The discipline is not to add more tools, faster tools, or smarter tools. It is to ask, before any tool decision, where is the actual bottleneck of my work? — and to recognise that for most knowledge professionals the bottleneck is a small number of hours per week of high-quality deep thinking, not the volume of correspondence, the speed of meetings, or the cleanliness of the inbox. Every minute spent optimising a non-bottleneck step is a minute not spent on the bottleneck. The opportunity cost is invisible until you name it.

The corollary, which Newport leaves implicit but is unmissable, is that AI tooling is heading the wrong way. The current generation of AI office assistants accelerates the inputs to the bottleneck without doing anything to widen the bottleneck itself. The output of a chicken-coop factory does not increase by handing the frame-builders an industrial-grade nail gun. It increases by hiring more roofers or training the existing ones — which, in the knowledge-work analogy, is the unfashionable advice to spend more uninterrupted hours doing the hard thinking, not the popular advice to triple your daily email volume.

Podcast · Elad Gil × Tim Ferriss · The Tim Ferriss Show #863 · weekly roundup 6 May 2026

Elad Gil on the One-Belief Question, the Misty AI Frontier, and the Value-Maximising Window

Tim Ferriss's Wednesday roundup pushed his recent Elad Gil interview back into the inbox — an appropriate second look, because the episode contains one of the cleanest pieces of investing-as-thinking-discipline of the year, and the framework generalises far outside venture capital. Gil has been an early investor or advisor to Airbnb, Anduril, Coinbase, Figma, Instacart, OpenAI, SpaceX, and Stripe, and his stated method for sorting the eight from the eight thousand is unusually compressible.

"What is the one thing I need to believe about this company that makes me think it's going to continue to be really big? Usually, it's one thing. If it's three things, it's too complicated." — Elad Gil, The Tim Ferriss Show #863 (29 April 2026)

The discipline is older than Gil — Charlie Munger called it the "one-line investment thesis" and Buffett practised a more austere version — but the formulation is the sharpest in current use. The corollary is the diagnostic: if you cannot state the thesis in one sentence, the thesis does not exist yet. The "three things" version is almost always a sign that the operator has stitched together post-hoc justifications around a feeling, and the feeling will not survive contact with reality. A single load-bearing belief, by contrast, is falsifiable. Either it turns out to be true or it does not, and the operator updates accordingly. The framework forces clarity by refusing complexity.

The "Misty AI Frontier" framing in the episode title is doing real work too. Gil's read is that the bottleneck for AI scaling is not, as the popular discourse insists, algorithmic; it is structural and physical — "Korean memory fabs", HBM and CoWoS packaging capacity through 2027. The constraint is in concrete and silicon, not in research. The implication for operators outside AI is the same one Newport reaches: identify the actual bottleneck rather than the rhetorically prominent one. Most public AI commentary attends to the prominent constraint (model architecture) while the binding constraint (advanced packaging) goes underdiscussed because it is less photogenic.

Gil's third framework from the episode is the most actionable for operators. Every company — and by extension every career arc — has a value-maximising moment, and it is usually a window of six to twelve months. Before the window, you have not yet earned the leverage; after the window, the headwind hits. The discipline is to recognise the window when you are inside it and to act decisively while you are. The honest version of this in venture is "sell into strength"; the honest version of it in a career is "take the bigger swing while the table is hot, because the table cools whether you swing or not".

The thread that joins Gil's three frameworks — one-belief, real bottleneck, value-maximising window — is the same thread that joins Newport's bottleneck post and Holiday's two Stoic essays this week: concentration of attention on the right thing, against the gravity of the easy things. The Stoic does not waste mental space on antagonists who are not paying any back; the philosopher does not waste affection on the tragedy itself but reserves it for the version of the self that emerges; the deep worker does not optimise the inbox while the bottleneck stands; the investor does not stitch three justifications together when one will do. Four sources, one operating posture. The convergence is not coincidence; it is what serious thinking about scarce attention produces from any starting point.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · From Elad Gil · Tim Ferriss Show #863

The One-Belief Test: Compressing Any Major Decision to a Single Falsifiable Sentence

Gil's framework converts directly into a personal operating discipline that takes about twenty minutes per decision and produces a result that survives months of subsequent rationalisation. The structure has four steps and the discipline is to do them in order:

  • Pick the decision — a job change, an investment, a relationship commitment, a product bet — and write it at the top of a page. The decision should be something where you have already started constructing arguments for or against. The goal is not to make the decision; the goal is to expose what you already, privately, believe is the load-bearing reason.
  • Write down every reason you are considering yes. Not curated — everything. Five to fifteen lines is normal. Include the embarrassing reasons (status, FOMO, "I just like the founder"). The audit fails if you self-edit at this stage.
  • Cross out everything that is downstream of one master belief. Most lines on the page are consequences of a single underlying claim. ("It will be big" is upstream of "the founder will recruit well", "the round will fill", "the press will be good".) Keep crossing out until the page contains exactly one sentence that, if true, makes most of the others true automatically. That is the one-belief.
  • Stress-test the one-belief: is it falsifiable in twelve months? If you can specify, today, a concrete observation that would tell you in a year that the belief was wrong, the thesis is real. If you cannot, the thesis is a feeling. Feelings are allowed, but they should be labelled correctly so the next decision uses them as data, not as analysis.

The discipline catches more bad decisions than any other practice in this newsletter's archive because it removes the hiding places. Most poor commitments are not made on a single bad belief; they are made on three mediocre ones, none of which would have survived the compression test. The forced reduction to one sentence either produces an actual reason or exposes the absence of one. Either outcome is more valuable than the unexamined yes.

Practice · From Cal Newport · "On Bottlenecks and Productivity"

The Personal Bottleneck Audit: Find Your Roof Crew Before Buying Another Nail Gun

Newport's Goldratt-derived framework lifts cleanly out of the factory floor and into a knowledge worker's calendar. The audit takes about ninety minutes, runs once a quarter, and reorders the weekly schedule more aggressively than any productivity book has ever managed:

  • List every output you produced last week that actually moved a real outcome. Not every task — every output. The deliverable that closed the deal, the analysis that changed the decision, the document that unblocked the project. Most professionals are surprised by how short the list is. Three to seven items per week is typical for someone in a senior role; one or two for someone caught in a busyness trap.
  • For each of those outputs, identify the rate-determining step. Where did the hours actually go? Almost always it is uninterrupted thinking time, design time, or judgement time — the deep step Newport names. The peripheral activities (meetings, coordination, correspondence) feel essential because they are continuous, but the value-producing minutes are concentrated in a small number of high-quality blocks.
  • Estimate, honestly, how many hours of bottleneck-step time you got last week. The number is almost always between four and twelve. The number you tell yourself you need is almost always twenty to thirty. The gap is the deficit Newport is naming, and it is usually the difference between work you are proud of and work you have rationalised.
  • Make exactly one structural change that protects two more hours of bottleneck time per week. A morning block before email. A no-meetings half-day. A standing decline of one recurring obligation. The change is unfashionable and uncomfortable because it requires saying no to the non-bottleneck steps that are loud, social, and expected. That is the point. Two more hours per week is twenty-four hours per quarter, which is more deep work than most operators have produced in a year.
Practice · From Daily Stoic · "They're Not Thinking About You At All"

The Mental Real Estate Eviction: Costing the Rent You Are Paying Antagonists

Holiday's "save your energy for what matters" is not a feeling; it is a budget. The practice operationalises the budget in three steps. It takes about an hour and is unusually durable because the data, once seen, is hard to look away from:

  • List every person currently occupying significant mental real estate — specifically those you stew about, plot against, or rehearse imagined confrontations with. Three to seven names is standard. Include the ex-colleague, the family member, the public figure, the neighbour. The discipline is to write the name even when it feels petty; the point is to see the inventory.
  • For each, estimate weekly hours of head-time. Not minutes — hours. The intrusive thought on the commute. The angry email mentally drafted in the shower. The replayed slight before sleep. Most operators discover a cumulative weekly total of three to eight hours, distributed across the list. Annualised, that is one hundred and fifty to four hundred hours of cognitive bandwidth.
  • For each name, ask: how many hours per week is this person spending thinking about me? Honestly. The answer is almost always zero. Occasionally a number above zero, in which case the asymmetry is at least two-sided. The Don Draper line lands when the operator finally calculates the ratio and sees it: 100:0, 200:0, 400:0. That is the rent being paid on space the antagonist has not even noticed renting out.
  • Pre-commit to redirecting one hour per week from the highest-rent name into a craft activity. Not "I will stop thinking about them", which is unenforceable. "When the intrusive thought arrives, it is the cue to open the file I have been avoiding." The substitution is the discipline. Over a year the redirected hour produces fifty-two hours of work on the thing that matters; the antagonist remains, by their own admission, not thinking about you at all.

The audit is the single highest-leverage exercise in this issue because most professionals run it at age fifty under duress — after a health scare, a redundancy, a divorce. Running it pre-emptively at thirty or forty saves ten to twenty years of paid rent and redirects the cognitive bandwidth into the work and relationships that compound. The numbers are quietly enormous.

Practice · From Daily Stoic · Admiral James Stockdale

The Stockdale Reframe: Treating the Current Difficulty as the Laboratory

Holiday's amor fati reframe converts into a daily practice that is shorter than it appears and works because it sidesteps the unanswerable demand to "love" the difficulty:

  • Identify the current chapter's central difficulty — the chronic illness, the demanding role, the strained relationship, the financial constraint. One sentence. The discipline is naming it specifically rather than carrying it as ambient weight. The naming itself reduces the load by an unexpected amount.
  • Reframe the question. Not "what does this cost me?" but "what is this difficulty offering me a chance to develop?" Stockdale's list is the template: courage, justice, strength, wisdom. Pick the one virtue this difficulty is uniquely positioned to develop in you. Most difficulties have an obvious answer; the unobvious answers are usually the more valuable ones.
  • Set one small concrete action this week that exercises that virtue inside the difficulty. Not "endure"; not "be brave"; a specific, falsifiable action. Have the conversation. Make the request. Say the no. Hold the line. The small action is the actual laboratory work; the rest is rationalisation.
  • Run a Sunday review: did you act in the direction of the chosen virtue this week, or did you avoid the action? The pattern over six weeks is the data. The point is not self-judgement but calibration. If the chosen virtue is wrong, change it. If the actions are not happening, the virtue is not actually the one being developed and the practice is theatre.

04 On the Horizon
Operating Discipline

"Bottleneck Thinking" Will Become the Successor Frame to Time Management in the Late 2020s

Newport's Goldratt revival is one of several signals that the dominant productivity vocabulary is shifting from volume to throughput — from "how much did I do" to "how much of the right thing moved". The same intuition surfaces in Cole Schafer's recent writing on slow output, in the renewed attention to Pat Helland's Mind the Constraint, in the post-Slack literature on "shallow work tax", and in the corporate efficiency narratives now dressing redundancies as bottleneck-removal exercises. The next eighteen months will see "find your bottleneck" become a coachable mid-career skill, possibly emerging as a structured exercise inside annual review cycles. The advantage accrues to operators who run it personally first — before the consultants productise it.

AI & Constraints

The "Real Bottleneck" Story Will Eclipse the "Capability Ceiling" Story for AI Through 2027

Elad Gil's Korean-memory-fab thesis is one piece of a larger reframing already underway among serious AI commentators. The capability discourse — "are we approaching AGI" — is increasingly seen as the photogenic constraint; the binding constraint is concrete: HBM3e and HBM4 supply, CoWoS packaging capacity, electrical grid interconnect lead times, sovereign data-centre permitting. The 2024–2025 era of "model wars" gives way in 2026–2027 to an era of "supply wars" where the bottleneck is silicon and watts, not algorithms. Operators outside AI should track this because the timing of any "AI is plateauing" narrative will be misleading: the plateau will be a packaging plateau, not an intelligence one, and the second derivative will resume sharply when the fabs catch up. Position accordingly.

Stoic Practice

The "Asymmetric Attention" Framing Is Becoming the Modern Stoic's Sharpest Weapon

Holiday's Don-Draper-as-Stoic move is part of a quiet reformulation of the classical Stoic teaching on injury. Marcus's "if you are pained by any external thing, it is not this thing that disturbs you" was always abstract; the modern restatement — "they are not paying you the rent you are paying them" — is concrete and arithmetical. Expect a wave of writing in the next twelve months, from the Stoic and Buddhist traditions both, on the asymmetry of mental attention as the actual mechanism of suffering — and the corresponding wave of practice tools (journaling apps, mental-load calculators) that surface the asymmetry as data. The concept is one of the few in popular philosophy that is simultaneously falsifiable and life-altering.

Career Strategy

The "Value-Maximising Window" Is Quietly Becoming the Core Concept of 2020s Career Theory

Gil's six-to-twelve-month window framing has the same shape as the better recent thinking on career arcs: Arthur Brooks on the fluid-to-crystallised intelligence handover, Annie Duke on quitting, Cal Newport's craftsman ethic. The shared thesis is that timing is the underrated variable: the quality of the swing matters less than whether it is taken inside the window. Senior operators are increasingly reading their own arcs as a series of windows rather than a continuous slope, and the implication is sharper than the standard career advice: the right move at the wrong time is worse than the second-best move at the right time, because the right move at the wrong time produces no compounding. Expect the language of windows, vintages, and seasons to displace the language of grind, hustle, and 10x in serious career writing.

Convergence

"Concentration of Attention" Is Emerging as the Single Operating Principle of 2026 Senior Practice

Four sources, four traditions, one position. Holiday (popular Stoicism): refuse to rent space to antagonists. Holiday again (classical Stoicism): love the version of yourself emerging through difficulty, not the difficulty itself. Newport (deep-work tradition): work on the bottleneck, not the periphery. Gil (venture investing): one belief, not three. Read alongside last week's Manson distinction (fulfilled vs busy) and the Athenodorus pause, the synthesis is unmistakable. The defining discipline of senior operators in 2026 is not "do more"; it is "do the one thing that matters and refuse the gravity of the easy things". The convergence has not yet been named in a single frame, but it is becoming the default posture across finance, technology, philosophy, and craft. Watch for the first book that names it explicitly — the form is open and the audience is large.
