§04 Open-Source Pulse

Streaming video generation is the trending category of the week — Stream-R1 and Stream-T1 (both May 7) led today's Hugging Face papers feed; document-parsing VLMs (SmolDocling, MinerU2.5, PaddleOCR-VL) are an under-noticed second wave with practical use today.

Stream-R1 + Stream-T1 (May 7) — reliability-aware reward distillation and test-time scaling for streaming video

Stream-R1 (105 upvotes, May 7) introduces reliability-perplexity aware reward distillation for streaming video generation — a technique that improves output reliability by training the model to be aware of its own uncertainty during streaming generation. Stream-T1 (90 upvotes, same day) is the companion paper on test-time scaling for the same generation regime — scaling compute at inference time rather than at training time, so the same model can produce higher-quality streams when given more inference budget. Both papers from the same authoring group; both shipped today.

⚡ Implication: Not directly relevant to the briefings stack today. The category is worth tracking for two reasons. (1) If Pudgy Food Map ever wants a "show a 5-second video of the dish" feature (carried from §04 Issue #39), streaming video models with explicit reliability guarantees are exactly the architecture you'd want — open weights + inference-time quality knob means a self-hosted demo is viable. (2) Streaming video is the open-source category most likely to compete with closed video models (Sora, Veo) over the next 3–6 months — May 7 papers landing this strongly is sector-confirmation. Status: track; revisit if a Pengu Asia / Pudgy Food Map video need surfaces.

Document-parsing VLM wave — SmolDocling 256M, MinerU2.5 1.2B, PaddleOCR-VL 0.9B (157/160/124 upvotes)

Three small-scale vision-language models for document parsing landed in the top-10 trending Hugging Face papers concurrent with the streaming video work. SmolDocling (256M parameters) — ultra-compact end-to-end multi-modal document conversion. MinerU2.5 (1.2B parameters) — decoupled vision-language architecture, state-of-the-art recognition with computational efficiency. PaddleOCR-VL (0.9B parameters) — multilingual document parsing with NaViT-style dynamic resolution. The three models cluster around the same use-case (PDF/document → structured text) but cover different size points (256M / 0.9B / 1.2B), which means there's now a Pareto frontier of document-parsing VLMs at sub-2B parameters.

⚡ Implication: For the legal-academic pipeline (Jason Allen co-authored work, citation-proofread skill, deep-academic-research skill), this is the most directly actionable open-source category of the week. dc's current document-parsing stack runs on Anthropic API for PDF reads — a sub-2B-parameter VLM running locally would replace that API spend for the bulk-extraction stage of long academic-paper review, with the trade-off being ~5–10% lower extraction quality. Worth a 30-minute test on one academic paper this week — pick MinerU2.5 (best quality/size ratio) and run it against an existing Anthropic-API extraction for comparison. EU AI Act Omnibus VII — provisional agreement reached today (May 7); high-risk obligations deferred to 2 Dec 2027 ⚡

Tracked across Issues #33–39. What changed: Council and Parliament negotiators reached a provisional agreement on the AI Omnibus VII today, May 7 — six days earlier than the May 13 trilogue Issue #39 was waiting on. Per the Council press release, the headline provisions:

  • High-risk AI systems with high-risk use cases (biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, law enforcement, border management) — obligations now apply from 2 December 2027 (a 16-month deferral from the original 2 August 2026).
  • AI systems as safety components covered by EU sectoral safety/market-surveillance legislation — obligations apply from 2 August 2028.
  • Transparency for AI-generated content — grace period reduced from 6 months to 3 months; new deadline 2 December 2026.
  • National AI regulatory sandboxes — establishment deadline pushed to 2 August 2027.
  • Annex I sectoral-law dispute (carried Issues #33–39) — resolved. A compromise mechanism resolves the interplay between the AI Act and sectoral safety law (medical devices, toys, lifts, machinery, watercraft).
  • AI Office competence clarified for general-purpose-AI-model-derived systems, with national-authority exceptions for law enforcement, border management, judicial authorities, and financial institutions.
  • New ban: AI systems generating CSAM or non-consensual intimate imagery of identifiable persons.

Path to formal adoption: the provisional agreement still requires formal adoption by both institutions, which the co-legislators intend to complete before 2 August 2026 — the original date the rules would have started to apply.

⚡ Implication: The largest single binary regulatory event of Q2 2026 has resolved in favour of deferral. The Annex I intersectionality dispute that was the natural paper topic in Issue #38–39 is now resolved at the political level — meaning the live academic question is no longer "how should this be resolved?" but "did the resolution actually work, and does the compromise mechanism hold up against the Article 6.1 + sectoral-safety overlap in practice?" That's a cleaner paper, and the timing — formal adoption text expected before August 2 — gives a natural 8–10 week drafting window before the political record stabilises. For market-context thinking: the deferral is mildly positive for AI-equity sector beta (large EU enterprise customers don't have to pay compliance costs in 2026) and approximately neutral for AI-token sector beta (EU compliance was never priced into TAO/RENDER/AKT). §07 Research Radar

Stream-R1 (May 7, today) is the most-upvoted new paper of the week and previews how streaming video generation gets reliable enough for production — a 6-month story that ends in commercial Sora/Veo competitors with quality knobs.

Stream-R1 (Hugging Face #1, May 7) — reliability-perplexity aware reward distillation for streaming video

Stream-R1 ships an auxiliary training objective that conditions a streaming video generator on its own predicted perplexity, allowing the model to abstain or retry on tokens it's unsure about. The result: meaningfully more reliable output across long-horizon generations — the failure mode of streaming video has historically been mid-stream collapse (the model loses the thread and produces garbage frames after second ~3). Stream-R1 reduces that failure rate sufficiently that long-form streaming becomes viable. If a self-hosted streaming-video model with a Stream-R1-style reliability objective and Stream-T1-style inference scaling lands at >80% of Sora-2 quality at 1/10th the per-second cost, the closed-source price ceiling drops. 🔬 Plain-English Explainer — what "multi-agent orchestration" actually is, and why it matters for compute demand

Imagine you have a single highly competent assistant. They can do most things, but for any complex task — say, researching, writing, and fact-checking a 20-page report — they're doing all of it themselves, sequentially, in their own head. They get fatigued, they lose track of details, and if they need expert review, they have to interrupt their own flow. That's what every AI agent has been until now: one Claude instance, doing everything in one continuous reasoning loop. Multi-agent orchestration replaces that single assistant with a small team — a project manager, a researcher, a writer, and a fact-checker — coordinating with each other. The project manager (the "orchestrator") breaks the task into subtasks, hands them to the right specialist, and aggregates the results. The specialists can be cheaper or smaller models for routine subtasks, with a more expensive frontier model called in only when needed. The practical result: Anthropic cited a customer ("eve") who got "frontier model quality at 5x lower cost" by using a small model that calls Opus only as an on-demand advisor — not for every step, just for the hard ones. For traders, this matters because it changes compute demand in two contradictory directions at once. Per task, you spend more tokens (multiple agents producing intermediate outputs adds up to more total tokens than one agent doing the same task in its head). But per-quality-unit, you spend less money (because the cheap agents handle 80% of the work and the expensive frontier model only weighs in on 20%). Aggregate across the whole AI economy and the net effect is more total inference, not less — what economists call Jevons paradox: when you make something cheaper to produce, total consumption goes up, not down. For the AI-DePIN compute basket (TAO, RENDER, AKT), this is structurally bullish on a 12-month horizon — every additional Anthropic customer that adopts multi-agent orchestration is generating 3–6× the inference tokens that customer was generating in single-instance mode. The decentralised-compute thesis isn't "decentralised replaces centralised"; it's "centralised gets so saturated that decentralised becomes the marginal supply." Today's announcement is the moment that thesis stops being theoretical.
