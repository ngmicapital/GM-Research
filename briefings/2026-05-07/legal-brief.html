White House Drafts AI Pre-Release Vetting Executive Order — Hassett Confirms "FDA-Style" Approval Will "Quite Likely" Apply to All AI Companies; Anthropic's Mythos Cited as Catalyst

"Possibly an executive order to give a clear roadmap to everybody about how this is going to go and how future AIs that also potentially create vulnerabilities should go through a process so that they're released to the wild after they've been proven safe, just like an FDA drug." — Kevin Hassett, Director of the National Economic Council, statement reported by Bloomberg, 6 May 2026

National Economic Council Director Kevin Hassett confirmed on 6 May that the White House is drafting an executive order that would create a vetting system for new AI models before their public release, modelled explicitly on the FDA's drug-approval architecture. Asked whether the framework would apply only to frontier developers or industry-wide, Hassett told Bloomberg the testing requirements would "really quite likely" apply to all AI companies. Anthropic's cyber-focused Mythos model — which the Trump administration has been seeking to deploy across federal agencies despite an active Pentagon supply-chain-risk designation against Anthropic itself — is named in reporting as the proximate trigger for the policy reversal.

The proposal lands inside an unusually crowded AI-policy stack. The 11 December 2025 executive order directs federal agencies to identify and challenge state AI laws inconsistent with national policy; the 20 March 2026 National AI Legislative Framework set out six legislative recommendations to Congress; and the EU's Digital Omnibus on AI faces its rescheduled third trilogue on 13 May, with the original 2 August 2026 high-risk obligations still standing if no deal is reached. A US pre-market approval regime introduced now would re-anchor the global debate from voluntary commitments toward gatekeeper regulation — a posture closer to Brussels than to the administration's previous deregulatory rhetoric.

The economic stakes are concrete. Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Meta AI, xAI and Mistral all release frontier-grade models on rolling cadences; an FDA-style filing window typically runs months, not weeks. Per the Holland & Knight read of the 20 March framework, any future statutory regime that adopts pre-release approval would also implicate the framework's intellectual-property and "AI-driven censorship" objectives — meaning approvals could turn on substantive output behaviour, not just security testing.

📎 Historical Precedent: The 1976 Medical Device Amendments to the FD&C Act introduced 510(k) pre-market notification for Class II devices — a regime that took 11 years to settle into stable case law and shifted enormous market power toward incumbents able to absorb the filing burden.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Buchalter's 11 December 2025 alert and Holland & Knight's 20 March 2026 read of the National AI Framework, the federal authority for a pre-release vetting EO is contested. There is no current statute giving a Cabinet-level body model-by-model approval power; an EO can mandate intra-government testing protocols (NIST AISI is the natural vehicle) but cannot lawfully prevent a private actor from releasing a model absent statutory authority — only condition federal procurement and contracting on it. Expect any final EO to use procurement leverage and Defense Production Act framing rather than direct prohibition.

The Brief's read: The deeper litigation risk is First Amendment. Per the ZwillGen analysis of the December 2025 EO, courts are already scrutinising executive-branch attempts to condition AI deployment on speech-related criteria. A pre-release approval that touches model outputs (not only cyber posture) invites NetChoice v. Paxton-style preemption challenges and a likely Fifth Circuit / Ninth Circuit split.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Per the IAPP and AOShearman reads of the EU AI Act trilogues, the contrarian view is that the EO is directionally aligned with the EU framework and therefore de-risks US frontier developers globally — they would face one approval pathway rather than a forking US-vs-EU regime. The Brief's read: the bigger contrarian point is that Hassett's "FDA-style" framing protects incumbents (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind) at the expense of open-source releases (Mistral, Llama derivatives) — open-weight developers have no single legal entity to register a 510(k)-equivalent filing, and the EO becomes a de facto open-source ban without saying so.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Anthropic (private, ~$170B last secondary mark per FT February reportage) and OpenAI both gain regulatory moat — they have the compliance infrastructure to absorb a pre-market approval regime. Microsoft (NASDAQ:MSFT) and Amazon (NASDAQ:AMZN) gain through their cloud-tied frontier partnerships (OpenAI and Anthropic respectively). Meta (NASDAQ:META) faces direct exposure on Llama open-weight strategy if the EO captures open-source releases. Per a recent Lawfare analysis, NIST AISI's 2026 budget request positions it as the natural pre-market evaluator — translating to an immediate hiring and contracting wave in Gaithersburg.

Operational-technology vendors (Schneider, Honeywell, Siemens) excluded from the Mythos federal rollout are already pressuring the White House for sectoral carve-outs (Nextgov, 5 May reporting). The Brief's read: expect a parallel FCC- or NTIA-led "critical infrastructure" carve-out for OT-embedded AI within 60 days of any signed EO.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish MSFT, AMZN, GOOGL, NIST AISI-adjacent compliance contractors (Booz Allen Hamilton: BAH; Leidos: LDOS) — gatekeeper regulation favours the closed-frontier stack.

⚪ Neutral NVDA, AMD — pre-market approval slows model releases but compute demand for evaluation, fine-tuning, and red-teaming rises.

CFTC Chair Selig Tells Consensus Miami Prediction-Markets Fight "Expected" to Reach Supreme Court — Minnesota Now Eyed as Next State Lawsuit Target After Five-Six State Suits Already Filed

"We expect these matters to go up to the Supreme Court." — Mike Selig, CFTC Chair, Consensus Miami 2026 panel, 4 May 2026 (per CNBC video and Crypto Times reportage 6 May)

CFTC Chair Mike Selig used a 4 May Consensus Miami panel to declare openly that the federal-vs-state battle over prediction markets is heading to the Supreme Court — the first time a sitting Commissioner has articulated SCOTUS review as the agency's expected endpoint, not a contingency. Selig confirmed the CFTC has already filed against "five or six" states (Arizona, Connecticut, Illinois, New York are public; New Jersey and Massachusetts are pending) and Semafor reported on 6 May that Minnesota is now the next likely target after a 1 May state-legislature ban bill.

The Selig statement crystallises three things at once. First, the CFTC's "exclusive jurisdiction" theory is now agency policy, not staff position — making any settlement with a single state operationally difficult. Second, the agency is choosing escalation over the narrower path the Coalition for Prediction Markets opened on 30 April when it conceded the need for a statutory "gaming" definition. Third, the prediction-market cases sit on the same federal-pre-emption fault line as the AI EO debate (Story 1), the EU AI Act, and the broader CLARITY Act — meaning a SCOTUS prediction-market ruling could re-anchor pre-emption doctrine for digital-asset law generally.

📎 Historical Precedent: Murphy v. NCAA (2018) — the SCOTUS ruling that struck PASPA's anti-commandeering provisions and opened legal sports betting state-by-state. The mirror-image question (federal pre-emption blocking state gambling enforcement) has never been squarely decided.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Cleary Gottlieb's 16 March prediction-markets primer and Holland & Knight's February jurisdictional analysis, the federal-pre-emption argument rests on the CEA's swap definition combined with the 7th Circuit's Kalshi reasoning. Against it sit (a) state criminal-gaming codes that survive ordinary preemption, (b) the Murphy anti-commandeering line, and (c) the Tenth Amendment police-power claim that gambling regulation is core state authority. Per Sidley's 2 February client alert, the agency's strongest case is a clean federal-court declaratory judgment action; the weakest is a parallel state-criminal track running against named officers.

Per Norton Rose Fulbright's preemption note, the timeline arithmetic favours the CFTC: the 19 February Trauger preliminary injunction, the 6 April Third Circuit Kalshi ruling, and the recent state suit-blocks have created a coherent federal track record. The Brief's read: a SCOTUS cert grant is more likely than not by Q1 2027 if the agency moves on Minnesota — three pending circuit splits would give the Court a clean vehicle.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Per DarrowEverett's analysis, the contrarian view is that Selig is over-stating SCOTUS appetite. The Court has been hesitant to take CEA-jurisdiction cases since CFTC v. Schor (1986), and a Roberts-era preference for narrow textual rulings could see a circuit split resolved without granting cert. The Brief's read: the more durable contrarian point is that the CFTC's posture survives only as long as the current Republican majority — a 2027 administration change could withdraw every active state suit and quietly settle the federal cases, leaving Polymarket and Kalshi exposed to fresh state action.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Polymarket and Kalshi are the headline beneficiaries through any near-term federal-pre-emption ruling. Hyperliquid's HIP-4 outcome markets — the subject of the 30 April Hyperliquid Policy Center comment letter — sit one regulatory layer below: even a CFTC win at SCOTUS leaves the question of whether a non-DCM, decentralised venue can host the same products. Per Galaxy Research, sports-event-contract volume on registered DCMs is running ~3× year-on-year. DraftKings (NASDAQ:DKNG) and Flutter (NYSE:FLUT) face the most direct competitive pressure if SCOTUS upholds CFTC pre-emption.

The Tribal-gaming sector (especially CNIGA-aligned operators) is the loudest industry counter-voice. Per Bloomberg Law, several tribes have signaled they will brief amicus on SCOTUS appeal — Indian Gaming Regulatory Act preemption is their threshold argument.

🔴 Bearish DKNG, FLUT — sports-event-contract substitution risk widens through SCOTUS docket.

The bifurcation creates a two-tier federal AI procurement regime — civilian-cleared, defence-blacklisted — and is the proximate political driver of the broader pre-release vetting EO Hassett confirmed on 6 May.

Treasury OFAC On-Chain Sanctions Action Freezes $344M USDT Tied to Iranian Central Bank

OFAC's late-April action against $344M of USDT linked to Iran's central bank set a new high-water mark for on-chain sanctions enforcement. Tether confirmed the freeze on its X feed; Mayer Brown's analysis flags this as the model FinCEN-OFAC will reference in finalising the GENIUS Act AML/sanctions NPRM (comments due 9 June). Permitted Payment Stablecoin Issuers face explicit, automated sanctions-screening obligations once final.

AUSTRAC "Tranche 2" Expansion Adds Legal, Accounting, Real Estate, Jewellery Sectors From 1 July

AUSTRAC's Tranche-2 reform — long delayed — brings tranche-2 designated services into the AML/CTF regime from 1 July 2026. Combined with the 30 May AML/CTF compliance-officer notification deadline (yesterday's Brief) and the 30 June ASIC AFS no-action expiry, Australia's three-cliff sequence is the most compressed compliance calendar in the Anglosphere this year.
