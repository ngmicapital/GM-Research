Driving Thesis

Three advances this cycle reshape longevity and performance protocols. The American College of Sports Medicine published its first Resistance Training Position Stand update in 17 years (March 16, 2026) — a synthesis of 137 systematic reviews and more than 30,000 participants that delivers a mechanistically clarifying verdict on what actually drives hypertrophy and functional strength: weekly volume (~10 sets per muscle group) is the primary driver, rep range has no independent effect on muscle growth when effort and total volume are controlled for, twice-weekly frequency per muscle group is the critical minimum, and training to failure provides no meaningful advantage over stopping 1–3 reps short. The position stand formally ends the myth that hypertrophy requires heavy loading or precise rep-range targeting, with direct implications for how PPL programs should be structured for longevity versus performance goals. On the biomedical side, a Buck Institute study (Aging Cell, May 8, 2026) identifies the long-sought mechanism behind APOE2’s protection against Alzheimer’s disease and premature aging: neurons carrying the APOE2 variant strongly upregulate DNA repair and genomic damage-response pathways, accumulate significantly less double-strand DNA damage, and resist entering cellular senescence — shifting the scientific understanding of APOE2 from a cholesterol transport variant to a genome integrity protector in neural tissue. The finding opens a new drug-discovery direction: APOE2-mimetic compounds or targeted DNA repair activators that could offer the same neuroprotection to APOE4 carriers at highest Alzheimer’s risk. In nutrition, a second major finding from Digestive Disease Week 2026 (University of Texas Medical Branch) demonstrates that restoring the gut microbiome of aging mice to a youthful state — via autologous fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) of their own banked young-mouse samples — reversed multiple hallmarks of liver aging at molecular and functional levels and prevented liver cancer in all treated animals versus 2/8 untreated controls, providing proof-of-concept that microbiome rejuvenation may be a viable systemic anti-aging intervention.

Section 01
Longevity & Performance Science

The APOE2 finding from the Buck Institute is mechanistically significant because it resolves a long-standing puzzle in Alzheimer’s genetics. APOE2 has been associated with longevity and Alzheimer’s protection for decades, but the mechanism was poorly understood — APOE’s canonical role in cholesterol transport and amyloid-β clearance did not fully explain why APOE2 carriers age better neurologically. This study, using RNA sequencing of human iPSC-derived neurons with APOE2, APOE3, and APOE4 genotypes, finds that the differentiation is in the genome integrity domain: APOE2 neurons mount a fundamentally stronger DNA damage-response, upregulating pathways including ATM/ATR signaling, base excision repair, and non-homologous end joining. Accumulated double-strand DNA breaks — the type that, if unrepaired, drive p53 activation, cell cycle arrest, and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) — are significantly lower in APOE2 neurons even under oxidative stress conditions that mimic aging. The downstream implication is that APOE2 neurons simply do not become senescent as readily, and since senescent cells in brain tissue are a primary driver of neuroinflammation and network dysfunction in Alzheimer’s, the genome integrity mechanism connects APOE2 directly to the aging cascade at a more proximal point than amyloid clearance alone.

The gut microbiome / liver aging reversal study (DDW 2026) is the most compelling FMT longevity finding yet in a mammalian model because it uses autologous transplantation — the mice received their own banked young microbiome, not a donor’s. This eliminates graft-host mismatch as a confounding variable and more directly models what a human “microbiome banking” intervention would look like. The MDM2 oncogene suppression finding is particularly notable: MDM2 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that directly inhibits p53 activity, and its elevation in aged livers essentially reduces the tumor suppressor pressure that normally prevents hepatocellular carcinoma. The fact that restoring a youthful microbiome suppressed MDM2 to young-mouse levels — and that none of the FMT-treated mice developed liver cancer — suggests a functional anti-cancer mechanism running through the gut-liver axis. The human translation is distant; the logistics of banking young fecal samples for autologous use decades later are formidable. But the biology is real and the signaling pathway now mapped.

APOE2 Longevity Gene Protects Neurons via DNA Repair Upregulation and Senescence Resistance — Buck Institute / Aging Cell, May 2026
[Mechanistic]

A study published in Aging Cell (DOI: 10.1111/acel.70494; May 8, 2026) by Gerónimo-Olvera et al. at the Buck Institute for Research on Aging identifies the molecular mechanism through which the APOE2 allele — the rare genetic variant associated with exceptional human longevity and ~40–50% reduced Alzheimer’s risk — confers neuroprotection against aging-driven cellular decline. The study used human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived neurons homozygous for APOE2, APOE3, and APOE4 — the three main variants differing only in two amino acids at positions 112 and 158 — and subjected them to identical oxidative stress and aging-mimetic conditions, then performed comprehensive RNA sequencing and DNA damage quantification.

The key finding: APOE2 neurons accumulate significantly less double-strand DNA damage (γ-H2AX foci, a quantitative DNA break marker) than APOE3 or APOE4 neurons under identical stress conditions. RNA sequencing revealed that this protection is not passive — APOE2 neurons are transcriptionally distinct, with strong upregulation of DNA repair pathway genes including those in ATM/ATR signaling, base excision repair, nucleotide excision repair, and non-homologous end joining. In contrast, APOE4 neurons showed downregulation of these same repair systems — explaining in mechanistic terms why APOE4 carriers have accelerated neuronal aging and 10–15× elevated Alzheimer’s risk. APOE3 (the most common variant) fell between APOE2 and APOE4 on all measures.

The consequence of reduced DNA damage accumulation: APOE2 neurons were measurably more resistant to entering cellular senescence. Senescent neurons expressing the SASP (senescence-associated secretory phenotype) release IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α — creating the neuroinflammatory microenvironment that drives progressive Alzheimer’s pathology. APOE2’s genome integrity advantage therefore reduces the rate at which neurons become the very cells that poison their neighbors. The researchers note this mechanism operates independently of (and additively with) APOE2’s known benefits in amyloid-β clearance — meaning APOE2 carriers likely benefit from at least two distinct protective mechanisms simultaneously.

Future research directions: the team is now screening APOE2-mimetic compounds — small molecules that could activate the same DNA repair signaling cascade in APOE3 or APOE4 neurons. This is a meaningful drug-discovery direction because APOE4 homozygosity affects approximately 3% of the population and is the strongest known genetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer’s. Lead investigator Dr. Cheng-Xin Gong stated: “Understanding why APOE2 protects against both aging and Alzheimer’s is crucial for developing new strategies to combat these conditions.” No drug candidates have yet been identified; this is exploratory mechanistic groundwork.

Autologous Young Gut Microbiome Transplant Reverses Liver Aging Hallmarks and Prevents Liver Cancer in Mice — UT Medical Branch, DDW 2026
[Mechanistic]

Research from the University of Texas Medical Branch, presented at Digestive Disease Week (DDW) 2026 and covered by ScienceDaily (May 9, 2026), provides proof-of-concept for a microbiome rejuvenation approach to systemic aging: collecting fecal samples from young mice, storing them, then transplanting them back autologously as the same animals aged. The autologous design — same animal’s own microbiome, not a donor’s — eliminates graft-host compatibility as a confounding variable and most closely models what a human microbiome banking intervention might look like in practice.

Results across the treatment group: reversal of multiple hallmarks of liver aging at both molecular and functional levels. Specifically, the study measured and found significant improvement in: hepatic inflammation (reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression), fibrosis markers (collagen deposition returned toward young-mouse levels), mitochondrial function (oxidative phosphorylation capacity improved), telomere attrition (telomere lengths stabilized), and DNA damage markers (γ-H2AX foci reduced). The most striking finding was the cancer outcome: zero liver cancers developed in the FMT-treated mice, while 2 out of 8 untreated aging controls developed hepatocellular carcinoma. The mechanism: MDM2 protein suppression. MDM2 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that degrades p53, effectively removing the tumor suppressor brake on cellular proliferation. Aged livers show elevated MDM2, reducing p53 activity and increasing cancer susceptibility. The young microbiome transplant suppressed MDM2 expression to levels matching young mice — restoring the p53 tumor suppressor activity that had declined with age. Lead researcher Dr. Zheng Li stated: “Restoring a more youthful microbiome can reverse several core features of aging at both the molecular and functional level.”

Three important caveats: First, this is a mouse model — the human gut microbiome is orders of magnitude more complex, and autologous FMT in aging humans raises significant logistical and regulatory questions about sample storage, viability, and what “youthful” microbial composition actually means for a given individual. Second, the DDW presentation format means full peer-reviewed methods have not yet been published; the mechanistic claims should be treated as preliminary until journal publication. Third, the sample size (8 treated, 8 controls) is small — the cancer outcome result (0/8 vs 2/8) is directionally strong but needs replication at scale.

Source: ScienceDaily · May 9, 2026 · University of Texas Medical Branch · Presented at DDW 2026 · Autologous fecal microbiota transplantation in aging mice · Dr. Zheng Li
⚡ Implication: The APOE2 study reframes the gene from “good cholesterol variant” to “genome integrity protector” — the Alzheimer’s protection mechanism is now mechanistically grounded in DNA repair, not just amyloid clearance. This is a research direction worth tracking: APOE2-mimetic or DNA repair-activating compounds for APOE4 carriers will be among the most closely watched neuroprotection drug targets of the decade. The FMT liver aging reversal is compelling proof-of-concept for microbiome rejuvenation as a systemic aging intervention — but the human translation requires infrastructure (microbiome banking services that don’t yet exist at scale) and peer-reviewed validation before drawing conclusions about actionability.
Section 02
Training & Performance

The 2026 ACSM Position Stand is the most clinically significant resistance training document in nearly two decades, and its key message for PPL practitioners is both clarifying and liberating: the “optimal” rep range that dominated training culture for thirty years — 8–12 reps for hypertrophy, 3–5 for strength, 15+ for endurance — has no independent mechanistic support in the evidence base when effort and total volume are controlled. Muscle growth occurs across a broad loading spectrum (from ~30% to 90% of 1RM) provided two conditions are met: (1) sufficient proximity to failure (within ~3 reps) and (2) adequate weekly volume per muscle group (~10 sets/muscle/week minimum). The rep-range independence finding matters for people who train at home with lighter loads, or who prefer higher-rep training for injury management — the hypertrophy signal is not attenuated. What does matter is progressive overload, weekly volume accumulation, and twice-weekly frequency as the primary structural variables.

The Syracuse University nerve conduction velocity study adds a dimension to resistance training’s longevity benefit that has been largely absent from the literature: peripheral neural health. Muscle mass and strength are well-established aging biomarkers, but the mechanism underlying age-related strength decline is not simply muscle atrophy — it also involves progressive denervation, specifically the loss of fast-twitch motor units (Type II fibers) that are innervated by large-diameter alpha motor neurons that are selectively vulnerable to age-related axonal degeneration. The finding that resistance training can improve nerve conduction velocity and potentially reactivate (reinnervate) these dormant fast motor units in older adults is mechanistically novel: it suggests strength training is not just preserving what remains but actively restoring neural infrastructure that age had begun to dismantle.

ACSM 2026 Resistance Training Position Stand: First Major Update in 17 Years — 137 Systematic Reviews, 30,000+ Participants — Rep Range Has No Independent Effect on Hypertrophy When Volume and Effort Are Controlled
[Guidelines]

The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) published its updated Resistance Training Position Stand on March 16, 2026 — the first comprehensive revision since 2009. The Position Stand, titled “Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults: An Overview of Reviews” (PMC12965823), synthesized 137 systematic reviews encompassing more than 30,000 participants across multiple training populations. The authors included researchers from McMaster University, the University of Täbingen, and multiple ACSM Working Group members.

Key finding 1 — Rep range and hypertrophy: The most practically significant update: “The rep range that a person uses has no independent effect on hypertrophy when effort and total volume are controlled for.” Muscle growth can be achieved across a broad loading spectrum — from approximately 30% of 1RM to 90% of 1RM — provided the set is taken within approximately 1–3 repetitions of failure (Repetitions in Reserve, or RIR, of ~0–3). The traditional 8–12 rep range is not mechanistically privileged; it is simply a convenient zone where moderate load and moderate proximity to failure naturally coincide. Lighter loads with higher reps, or heavier loads with lower reps, produce equivalent hypertrophy responses when effort and volume are equated.

Key finding 2 — Volume threshold: The minimum effective volume for hypertrophy is approximately 10 sets per muscle group per week. Below this threshold, hypertrophy is significantly attenuated. Above it, returns are progressive but subject to recovery capacity — very high weekly volumes (20+ sets/muscle group) show diminishing returns and elevated injury risk. For the typical PPL practitioner, this threshold is not difficult to meet: 2–4 sets per exercise across 3–5 exercises per muscle group, twice weekly, exceeds 10 sets comfortably.

Key finding 3 — Frequency: Training all major muscle groups at least twice weekly is the primary frequency driver for both hypertrophy and functional strength. Once-weekly training produces measurably inferior outcomes when total volume is equated, because protein synthesis elevation is time-limited (~48–72 hours per session); distributing stimuli across the week sustains the anabolic environment more continuously. PPL already achieves twice-weekly frequency for each muscle group (Push: chest/shoulders/triceps; Pull: back/biceps; Legs: quads/hamstrings/glutes) — the ACSM data validates this structure explicitly.

Key finding 4 — Failure training: Training to momentary muscular failure did not consistently outperform training close to failure across the evidence base. Stopping 1–3 reps short of failure (RIR 1–3) produces equivalent hypertrophy and strength outcomes while meaningfully reducing systemic fatigue and injury risk. Regular failure training adds recovery burden without adding muscle stimulus. The implication for long-term sustainable training is clear: training to technical failure (losing form) or absolute failure (cannot complete another rep) is not a necessary stimulus and comes at a cost.

Key finding 5 — Equipment flexibility: Elastic resistance bands, bodyweight exercises, and home-based programs produce hypertrophy equivalent to barbell and machine training when volume and effort are matched. The gym environment is a convenience, not a biological requirement. This is the clearest evidence-based rebuttal to “you need a gym to grow muscle.”

Source: PMC12965823 · ACSM Resistance Training Position Stand · March 16, 2026 · ACSM / McMaster University · 137 systematic reviews · 30,000+ participants · Overview of reviews · ACSM official release
Resistance Training Improves Nerve Conduction Velocity in Older Adults, Potentially Reactivating Fast Motor Neurons Lost to Age-Related Denervation — Syracuse University, Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise
[Experimental]

A study by JoCarol Shields and Professor Jason DeFreitas at Syracuse University’s Department of Exercise Science, published in Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise (September 2025; gaining broad clinical coverage through early 2026), tested whether age-related nerve conduction velocity (NCV) decline can be reversed by resistance training. The study enrolled 48 participants aged 18–84 across young and older adult groups, measuring NCV in the forearm before and after four weeks of progressive handgrip training (three sessions per week) in both arms. NCV — the speed at which an electrical impulse travels along a motor nerve — declines approximately 0.5–1.0 m/s per decade after age 40, primarily because large-diameter alpha motor neurons innervating fast-twitch (Type II) muscle fibers degenerate more rapidly than smaller-diameter neurons, causing progressive muscle denervation and contributing to the disproportionate loss of explosive strength and power with aging.

Results: both young and older participants who performed the handgrip training showed significantly faster post-training NCV versus their pre-training baselines; control groups (no training) showed no change. The effect was more pronounced in older participants — critically, every older adult who completed the training program showed NCV improvement, while none of the older controls did. The researchers hypothesize the mechanism is reinnervation of dormant fast motor neurons: age-related denervation causes fast motor axons to retract from their muscle fibers (orphaning the fibers, which then atrophy), but they do not immediately die — they may remain in a dormant, retracted state for years. Repeated contractile demand from resistance training may signal these neurons to re-extend and reinnervate the orphaned fibers, restoring both neural conduction speed and muscle activation capacity. Dr. DeFreitas stated: “If we’re reactivating those neurons that started to die, that can play a significant role in the preservation of strength and avoiding disability with aging.”

The handgrip design is both a strength and a limitation. Handgrip training is a tractable experimental model, but it’s unclear whether the NCV improvements generalize to other motor nerves and muscle groups — particularly compound lower-body movements (squat, deadlift) that use different neural circuitry. The four-week duration is short; longitudinal NCV tracking over months or years of consistent resistance training would be needed to assess whether the improvements are maintained or progressive. The finding is best interpreted as directional evidence that resistance training has a neural biology beyond muscle hypertrophy — one that may be particularly important for maintaining the explosive strength and reactive capacity that determine functional independence in older adults.

Source: EurekAlert · Syracuse University · Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise · September 2025 · Shields JC & DeFreitas JM · 48 participants, aged 18–84 · 4-week handgrip RCT
ACSM Position Stand — Integrate Now
  • Rep range is not the variable to optimize — volume (sets per muscle group per week) and proximity to failure (RIR 1–3) are the primary drivers. For PPL: confirm you’re hitting ~10 sets/muscle group/week minimum before worrying about rep targets
  • Stop at 1–3 reps before failure on most work sets. Training to absolute failure regularly adds recovery cost without adding hypertrophy benefit
  • Twice-weekly muscle group frequency is validated as the minimum effective structure — PPL achieves this by design
  • If training with lighter loads (travel, home setup), maintaining effort (close to failure) and total volume per week preserves the growth stimulus
What to Watch
  • The NCV / reinnervation finding is from a 4-week handgrip study — a mechanistically plausible result but not yet replicated for whole-body compound training or for longer durations
  • ACSM guidelines synthesize population averages; individual variation in recovery capacity, training history, and genetics means the 10-set/week threshold is a population minimum, not a ceiling or guaranteed prescription for everyone
  • “Volume drives hypertrophy” is true within ranges of recovery — adding volume beyond recovery capacity increases injury risk without additional muscle stimulus. The 10-set minimum is the floor, not the target to endlessly chase
⚡ Implication: The ACSM 2026 data formally ends the rep-range debate: train close to failure, accumulate ~10 sets per muscle group per week, hit each muscle twice weekly, and the specific load is a secondary variable. For PPL practitioners managing joint health or training away from heavy equipment, this is a full evidence-based license to adjust loads freely without sacrificing the growth stimulus. The neural health finding adds a second reason beyond hypertrophy to do resistance training as you age — preserving and potentially restoring peripheral nerve function is a distinct longevity mechanism that accumulates over years of consistent training.
Section 03
Supplements & Nutrition

The Nutrition Reviews systematic review on creatine and cognition (February 2026) closes an important evidence gap: prior creatine research has been heavily muscle-focused, but this systematic review specifically asks whether the creatine benefits known for muscle and bone in aging adults extend to cognitive function through the shared mechanism of the muscle-brain axis. The review finds limited but directionally positive evidence: older adults with lower baseline creatine stores (more common in individuals consuming Western reduced-protein diets, vegetarians/vegans, and people with reduced red meat intake — which describes most of the aging Western population) show the most consistent cognitive improvements with supplementation. The mechanistic logic is tight: creatine phosphate is critical for ATP regeneration in the brain as well as muscle; the brain consumes ~20% of total body energy from just 2% of body mass; and age-related reductions in dietary creatine intake alongside declining endogenous synthesis capacity (which requires arginine, glycine, and methionine) create a brain energy deficit that creatine supplementation directly addresses. What’s new here — beyond the “creatine builds muscle” narrative covered in prior issues — is the specific cognitive evidence base in aging adults and the mechanistic framing of the muscle-brain axis as a unified energy system whose decline underlies both sarcopenia and cognitive aging simultaneously.

The intermittent fasting hype watch entry this cycle is grounded in a rigorously designed Science Translational Medicine study (January 2026) that specifically tests whether time-restricted eating (TRE) — limiting caloric intake to an 8–10-hour window — produces cardiometabolic benefits independent of caloric restriction. The answer in this study: no. When calories are held constant between TRE and control groups (isocaloric design), neither insulin sensitivity, blood pressure, lipid profiles, nor inflammatory markers improved in the TRE arm. This does not mean TRE is useless — it means the popular narrative that “when you eat matters more than what you eat” is not supported when caloric intake is identical. The benefits of TRE that have been demonstrated in prior non-isocaloric studies are likely attributable primarily to the caloric restriction that TRE typically induces (by compressing the eating window, most people naturally consume less). This distinction matters for practitioners who are already managing caloric intake carefully and are adding TRE as an additional longevity lever — the evidence base for independent circadian timing benefits in humans is substantially weaker than the popular discourse suggests.

Creatine and Cognition in Aging: Systematic Review (Nutrition Reviews, Oxford, February 2026) Consolidates the Muscle–Brain Axis Case — Benefits Concentrated in Adults with Lower Baseline Creatine Stores
[Systematic Review]

A systematic review published in Nutrition Reviews (Oxford Academic; Volume 84, Issue 2; DOI: 10.1093/nutrit/nuaf135; February 2026; PMC12793482) by Marshall, Kitzan, Wright, Bocicariu, and Nagamatsu systematically assessed the evidence for creatine supplementation and cognitive outcomes specifically in older adults. This distinguishes the review from earlier work that pooled all-age evidence: the authors explicitly restrict inclusion criteria to populations aged 60+ and to studies with cognitive primary or secondary outcomes, producing a more focused and clinically relevant evidence synthesis than the broader muscle-focused literature.

Evidence summary: the review found limited but directionally positive evidence that creatine supplementation is associated with cognitive benefits in healthy older adults, with the most consistent signals appearing in working memory, processing speed, and tasks requiring sustained cognitive effort — all of which are energy-demanding cognitive functions relying on rapid ATP regeneration. The effect sizes were modest and heterogeneous across included studies, leading the authors to call for high-quality clinical trials before definitive conclusions; however, no included study showed creatine supplementation was harmful, and the mechanistic plausibility is strong: creatine supplementation raises brain phosphocreatine stores by approximately 5–15% (as measured by phosphorus-31 MRS), directly increasing the high-energy phosphate reserve available for rapid ATP regeneration during intensive neural firing.

The muscle-brain axis framing is the most conceptually valuable contribution of this review: muscle and brain share the same fundamental energy supply mechanism (phosphocreatine/ATP cycling), and their decline with aging is not coincidental. Age-related sarcopenia and cognitive decline co-occur at high rates; both are driven in part by declining creatine stores, reduced mitochondrial efficiency, and deteriorating neuromuscular junction (NMJ) function. Creatine supplementation addresses the shared energy substrate; resistance training addresses the NMJ and metabolic efficiency in parallel. The combination — creatine plus resistance training — is more mechanistically coherent as a longevity stack than either alone.

Who benefits most: the review identifies individuals with lower baseline brain creatine as the most responsive to supplementation. This population includes: vegetarians and vegans (who have ~20–30% lower muscle creatine vs. omnivores and likely lower brain creatine as well), older adults on reduced-protein diets, and people with metabolic conditions that impair endogenous creatine synthesis. For meat-eating adults with adequate protein intake, the marginal cognitive benefit of supplementation is less certain. Standard dose: 3–5 g/day creatine monohydrate — the evidence for cognitive effects does not require high-dose loading protocols. Onset of brain creatine elevation: approximately 4–6 weeks of consistent supplementation at this dose.

Source: Nutrition Reviews · Volume 84, Issue 2 · DOI: 10.1093/nutrit/nuaf135 · February 2026 · Marshall S et al. · University of Western Ontario · PMC12793482 · Systematic review of creatine and cognition in adults aged 60+
⚠ Hype Watch

Time-restricted eating as a cardiometabolic intervention independent of caloric restriction: A study published in Science Translational Medicine (January 2026; “Intended isocaloric time-restricted eating shifts circadian clocks but does not improve cardiometabolic health in women with overweight”; DOI: 10.1126/scitranslmed.adv6787) enrolled women with overweight in a rigorous isocaloric TRE protocol: participants in the TRE arm confined eating to an 8-hour daytime window while the control group ate the same calories spread across a normal eating window. Circadian clock gene expression confirmed that TRE did shift the circadian clock as expected. However: insulin sensitivity, blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, fasting glucose, triglycerides, and inflammatory markers did not differ between groups. TRE moved the clock but did not move the cardiometabolic needle when calories were held constant.

The implication for the broader TRE evidence base: most prior TRE studies are not isocaloric — participants in TRE arms naturally eat less because the eating window is compressed, and the observed benefits in those studies are driven primarily by that spontaneous caloric reduction. The TRE schedule itself adds minimal independent benefit in a controlled caloric setting. This does not mean TRE is useless — as a practical tool for reducing total caloric intake by compressing the eating window, it works well for many people. But the “circadian magic” narrative — that eating at the right times reshapes metabolism independent of how much you eat — is not supported by isocaloric evidence in humans. The animal data remains interesting mechanistically; human translation at isocaloric levels is not established. If you are using TRE as a structured way to naturally eat less, it is a valid behavioral tool. If you believe TRE is adding independent longevity benefit on top of already-controlled caloric intake, the evidence does not yet support that claim.

Supplement Protocol
  • Creatine monohydrate 3–5 g/day as a longevity maintenance dose — supports both muscle and brain phosphocreatine stores without loading protocol. Cognitive benefits most consistent in older adults and individuals with lower dietary creatine intake (vegetarians, reduced protein intake)
  • Combine with resistance training to maximize muscle-brain axis benefits: creatine addresses the energy substrate; training addresses neuromuscular junction integrity and mitochondrial efficiency in parallel
  • Onset for brain phosphocreatine elevation: ~4–6 weeks at 3–5 g/day. Once elevated, maintenance dosing sustains the effect; cycling off and on is not supported by longevity evidence
Caveats
  • The creatine cognition systematic review rates evidence quality as “limited” and calls for high-quality trials — the cognitive benefit direction is plausible and mechanistically grounded, but effect sizes in existing trials are heterogeneous and modest
  • Creatine supplementation causes a 0.5–1.5 kg increase in scale weight in the first 1–2 weeks (intramuscular water retention, not fat) — not a concern for health but relevant for people tracking body composition metrics short-term
  • The TRE cardiometabolic null result applies specifically to women with overweight in an isocaloric design — different populations and non-isocaloric conditions may differ; the animal longevity data is not invalidated by this human finding
⚡ Implication: Creatine’s longevity case extends beyond muscle: the muscle-brain axis framing formalizes why the same supplement and training combination that preserves lean mass in aging also supports cognitive aging resistance through shared energy mechanisms. The cognitive benefit signal is most consistent in older adults with lower baseline creatine stores — which describes most of the aging Western population eating reduced-animal-protein diets. On TRE: if you are using an eating window as a structured tool to naturally reduce caloric intake, it is effective. If you believe TRE is adding independent circadian metabolic benefit on top of an already-controlled diet, the isocaloric evidence does not support that.

Gut Microbiome Banking for Autologous FMT — Human Translation Pipeline
The UT Medical Branch DDW 2026 finding (autologous young microbiome transplant reverses liver aging and prevents cancer in mice) establishes the biological proof-of-concept for a human microbiome banking approach — collecting and cryopreserving your gut microbiome at a young age for re-administration as you age. Several companies have begun exploring microbiome banking services (e.g., Longevity Biotech startup space), but no peer-reviewed human trial data exists yet. The logistical challenges are real: long-term microbial viability, regulatory frameworks for autologous biological material preservation, and defining the right “age window” for collection. Watching for: the peer-reviewed paper from the DDW 2026 presentation; first human Phase 1 autologous FMT aging trial registration; commercial microbiome banking service launches with validated preservation protocols.

POLYCAD Trial — Spermidine in Coronary Artery Disease, Cardiovascular Endpoint Data
The POLYCAD trial (spermidine supplementation vs. placebo in CAD patients) completed recruitment in August 2025 and expects final patient visits in August 2026. Primary endpoints: cardiac remodeling, exercise capacity, muscle mass, systemic inflammation markers. This will be the first adequately powered cardiovascular endpoint trial for polyamine supplementation. The result will either validate the spermidine longevity biology in humans at a clinically meaningful scale or reveal that animal model findings don’t translate. Watching for: top-line cardiac remodeling and inflammatory marker data, expected late 2026.

COSMOS Multivitamin — Long-Term Disease Outcome Follow-Up
The COSMOS trial showed approximately four months of slowed epigenetic aging over two years with daily multivitamin supplementation (Nature Medicine 2026). The outstanding question is whether this translates to reduced incidence of age-related disease (cardiovascular, cancer, cognitive decline) over longer follow-up, and whether the benefit requires continuous supplementation or persists after cessation. The COSMOS-Mind cognitive substudy is ongoing. Watching for: disease incidence endpoints from extended follow-up and post-trial cessation data.

HMW-HA / Naked Mole Rat Longevity — Pre-Clinical Human Translation
University of Rochester researchers (Gorbunova/Seluanov lab) transferred the Has2 gene from naked mole rats into mice, producing 4.4% lifespan extension, reduced cancer rates, lower multi-tissue inflammation, and improved gut health (Nature, 2023). The team is now testing molecules that slow hyaluronan degradation in pre-clinical trials. Watching for: IND filing for first human trial of an HMW-HA modulator; Phase 1 safety data on hyaluronan-preserving compounds.

Rapamycin / PEARL Trial — Phase 2 Efficacy Data
The PEARL trial (rapamycin for longevity in healthy adults, Phase 2) results remain in the pipeline. mTOR inhibition remains the most clinically advanced pharmacological longevity approach, but the human efficacy question in healthy (non-diseased) populations is still open. Watching for: top-line biomarker outcome data, particularly on immune function and metabolic markers in the healthy adult population.
