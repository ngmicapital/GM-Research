The 2,000-Year-Old Computer: A Shipwrecked Device That Rewrote the History of Technology

In 1900, Greek sponge divers sheltering from a storm near the island of Antikythera stumbled on a sunken Roman ship. Among the bronze statues and marble sculptures they hauled up was an unremarkable lump of corroded metal. Two years later, a museum director glanced at the lump and noticed a gear. No comparable device would be built for another 1,400 years.

The Antikythera Mechanism is a 2,000-year-old analog computer made of interlocking bronze gears, recovered from a Roman shipwreck in 1900, that tracked the sun and moon, predicted solar eclipses decades in advance, and displayed the positions of all five planets known to antiquity. Built around 87 BCE in ancient Greece, it has no known predecessors and no successors for roughly 1,400 years — a gap so large that historians believe an entire precision-engineering tradition was simply erased.

TL;DR

The Antikythera Mechanism is an ancient Greek astronomical computer, built around 87 BCE, that uses a system of interlocking bronze gears to track the movements of the sun and moon, predict solar and lunar eclipses up to decades in advance, display the positions of the known planets, and indicate the schedule of the Panhellenic Games including the Olympics. It was recovered from a Roman cargo ship that sank around 60–70 BCE in 45 metres of water off the island of Antikythera. For over a century after its discovery, researchers could not agree on what it did or how it worked. Modern X-ray tomography has now decoded most of its inscriptions and reconstructed most of its gear trains. The machine has no known predecessors and no known successors for well over a millennium. It implies that a sophisticated tradition of Greek precision engineering existed, flourished, and was almost completely erased — leaving behind a single shipwrecked specimen.

Section 01

The Story

In the spring of 1900, a crew of Greek sponge divers from Symi island, captained by Dimitrios Kontos, was returning from Tunisia when a storm forced them to shelter near the small island of Antikythera in the Aegean. The divers had nothing to do but wait. One of them, named Elias Stadiatos, went down to look for sponges at around 45 metres depth and surfaced white-faced, telling Kontos he had found a heap of dead, naked women on the seabed. Kontos went down himself and found instead the remains of a Roman cargo ship, partially buried in sand, carrying one of the richest known cargoes of antiquity: bronze and marble statues, glassware, fine pottery, jewellery, and coins. When the Greek government organised a formal salvage with the Hellenic Royal Navy the following year, divers brought up the bronze Youth of Antikythera, the Antikythera Ephebe, dozens of marble figures including a Hercules, an Odysseus, a Hermes, and three marble horses, and, almost as an afterthought, several lumps of heavily corroded bronze metal that appeared to be nothing more than mineralised clumps of the ship’s fittings.

The metal lumps were deposited in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens. For two years no one paid them particular attention. Then, in May 1902, during a visit to the museum, the archaeologist Spyridon Stais noticed that one of the larger fragments appeared to contain a gear wheel. His cousin Valerios Stais, the museum director, examined it more closely. The gear had precisely cut triangular teeth. The fragment also contained inscriptions — ancient Greek text, densely packed, covering the interior surfaces of what appeared to be thin bronze plates. The object was obviously a device. Nobody had any idea what kind.

The initial assumption was that it must be a navigational astrolabe. The problem was that no navigational astrolabe had ever been found with gear trains. The closest known analogues were medieval. And those were a thousand years later.

Over the following decades, a series of researchers attempted to reconstruct what the mechanism did. The British science historian Derek de Solla Price published the first serious technical analysis in 1959, and a fuller treatment in 1974, concluding that the device was an analog computer for calculating astronomical positions. His reconstruction was partial and disputed. The real breakthrough came in 2006, when a multinational team using custom-built three-dimensional X-ray tomography equipment scanned all 82 surviving fragments at resolutions fine enough to read the inscriptions hidden inside the corroded bronze layers. The project, known as the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project, decoded thousands of characters of text and mapped the gear trains with a precision de Solla Price lacked. In 2021, a team at University College London led by Tony Freeth published a complete proposed reconstruction of the front mechanism in Scientific Reports, integrating the planetary display for the first time. The back of the mechanism — the eclipse prediction system — was already well understood. The front, which appeared to show the positions of the sun, moon, and all five planets known to antiquity, had remained uncertain. Freeth’s reconstruction used a system of epicyclic gearing — gears mounted on the back of other gears — a technique considered a product of medieval Islamic or European clockmaking. The Greeks used it two thousand years earlier.

As of 2024–2025, researchers are still refining the picture. A 2024 study using statistical techniques adapted from gravitational wave astronomy established that the mechanism’s calendar ring most probably had 354 holes — matching the lunar year precisely — rather than 365. An April 2025 analysis by engineers in Argentina examined whether the surviving gear fragments would actually have run smoothly given their manufacturing imperfections, and concluded that they probably would not have — suggesting either that the surviving pieces are damaged copies, or that the original mechanism was assembled with tolerances we cannot recover from what remains.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The Antikythera Mechanism was a wooden-cased bronze device approximately the size of a shoebox: about 34 centimetres tall, 18 centimetres wide, and 9 centimetres deep. It had a hand crank on the side. Turning the crank drove a system of interlocking bronze gears that moved a series of hands and dials on its front and back faces.

The front face displayed at least two concentric rings: an outer fixed ring marked with the Greek zodiac, and an inner moveable ring calibrated to the Egyptian civil calendar of 365 days. Hands indicated the positions of the sun and moon against the zodiac. The planetary display, as reconstructed by Freeth’s UCL team, used a system of at least five more gears — one for each of Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn — with pin-and-slot epicyclic mechanisms that reproduced the planets’ apparent retrograde motion as seen from Earth. This is the technically extraordinary part. Retrograde motion — the way planets appear to loop back on themselves as the Earth overtakes them in its orbit — is not a simple circular motion. Modelling it mechanically requires a gear whose rotation speed varies in a way that mimics the Ptolemaic model of epicycles. The Greeks built that model in bronze, at a scale of roughly one centimetre per gear, around 87 BCE.

The Metonic Cycle and the Saros

The back of the mechanism contained two spiral dials. The upper spiral tracked the Metonic cycle: the discovery, attributed to the astronomer Meton of Athens in 432 BCE, that 235 lunar months equals almost exactly 19 solar years (the error is about 2 hours per 19-year cycle). This cycle was used to coordinate lunar and solar calendars and to predict when the Panhellenic Games — the Olympics, the Pythian, Nemean, and Isthmian games — would be held. A sub-dial explicitly labelled the four games. The lower spiral tracked the Saros cycle: 18 years and 11 days, the period after which solar and lunar eclipses repeat in the same geometry. A pointer on the Saros dial indicated, month by month, whether an eclipse was predicted, and inscriptions specified whether it would be visible in the morning or evening and what colour the eclipse shadow would be. The mechanism could predict eclipses decades in advance.

The device was not just a calculator in the modern sense of a tool for doing sums. It was an analogue model of the sky: a machine that physically embodied the mathematical relationships between celestial cycles so that a user could turn a crank and watch the cosmos turn with it. Greek astronomers called this kind of device a sphaera or planetarium. Ancient authors including Cicero describe such devices as existing in the third century BCE, made by Archimedes and others. The Antikythera Mechanism is the only surviving example. Whether it was one of many or a unique specimen is unknown.

The ship that carried it was Roman but the cargo was almost certainly looted or purchased from Greece. The most plausible historical context is the aftermath of the Roman general Sulla’s sack of Athens in 86 BCE, after which large quantities of Greek art and technology were transported to Rome. The mechanism may have been manufactured in Rhodes — the island had a well-attested school of astronomer-engineers in this period, including the great astronomer Hipparchus, whose solar and lunar theories are reflected in the device’s gear ratios — or possibly in Corinth or Syracuse. It sank before it arrived.

Section 03

The Connections

The 1,400-year gap is the real mystery. The most intellectually disorienting fact about the Antikythera Mechanism is not what it could do but when comparable technology reappeared. The earliest known medieval mechanical clocks with equivalent gear complexity date from around 1300 CE. The earliest described astronomical clock with comparable astronomical computing capability — Richard of Wallingford’s clock at St Albans Abbey — dates from the 1330s. That is roughly 1,400 years after the Antikythera Mechanism. The standard explanation is that the tradition of Greek precision bronze-work was destroyed by the series of catastrophes that ended the classical world: the Roman conquests, the fires of Alexandria, the general collapse of Greek civic and intellectual life. This is almost certainly true in part. But it implies that a complex precision-engineering tradition can be entirely erased in a century or two. That is a sobering thought about the fragility of technological knowledge.

Cicero saw one. In his dialogue De Re Publica, written in 54 BCE — roughly contemporary with the Antikythera Mechanism — Cicero describes a bronze sphere made by Archimedes, brought to Rome by the general Marcellus after the sack of Syracuse in 212 BCE, that replicated the motions of the sun, moon, and planets. He calls it almost miraculous. In his Tusculan Disputations he mentions a second such device. Neither survives. Cicero is describing objects that were already antiques in his time, inherited from the generation of Greek engineers who built the Antikythera Mechanism. The tradition was clearly not a lone eccentric’s project but a recognisable genre of object — expensive, rare, and prestigious enough to be mentioned in literary sources and to travel as Roman war trophies.

What the Olympic Games Dial Tells Us

One of the back-plate dials is specifically labelled with the four Panhellenic Games: the Olympia, Pythia, Nemea, and Isthmia. The Olympics were held every four years; the other games at different intervals within that four-year Olympiad cycle. This dial served a practical scheduling function — in the Greek world, major games had religious and commercial significance, determining when travel was safe, when major markets operated, and when truces were declared between warring city-states. The inclusion of the Games dial confirms that the mechanism was not a pure astronomical research instrument but a practical tool for elite civic life: the kind of object a wealthy Roman or Greek patron would use to plan ahead across years. It is a portable, hand-cranked almanac and eclipse calculator combined.

The shipwreck is still giving up secrets. The Antikythera wreck has never been fully excavated. Modern archaeological campaigns — in 2012, 2014–2016, and ongoing — using advanced underwater excavation technology continue to recover objects from the site, including human skeletal remains (the first recovered in 2016, enabling DNA analysis that identified the individual as likely from southern Europe). Researchers believe a substantial portion of the cargo remains buried. The question of whether additional pieces of the mechanism or related instruments remain on the seabed is open. It is not implausible that the ship carried more than one such device: wealthy Roman patrons sometimes commissioned copies or sets of instruments, and the ship’s cargo was clearly assembled to order for a very specific, sophisticated customer.

The inscriptions are a user manual. The text covering the interior bronze plates of the mechanism is not technical documentation in the modern sense but something more interesting: it is explanatory prose written for a non-specialist reader. Decoded fragments describe what each pointer indicates, explain the meaning of each eclipse prediction, and identify the Games dial entries. Researchers interpret this as evidence that the mechanism was designed to be operated by someone who did not build it — a wealthy patron or their household staff — and that the maker felt it necessary to explain, in plain language, what the machine was showing. The device came with its own manual, engraved on its own surface, because it was not self-evident to the intended user. It was a consumer product for the highest end of the ancient market.

Further Down the Hole

📖
Jo Marchant — Decoding the Heavens (2008)
Marchant’s book is the best general account of the Antikythera Mechanism for a non-specialist reader, tracing the century-long effort to decode the device from Valerios Stais’s first examination in 1902 through Derek de Solla Price’s 1974 analysis to the 2006 X-ray project. She had access to the research team during the tomography scanning process and writes with authority about both the history and the science. The book is particularly good on the personalities involved in the reconstruction effort and why it took so long to converge on an answer.
⚙️
Tony Freeth — UCL Reconstruction (2021)
Freeth’s 2021 paper in Scientific Reports (“A Model of the Cosmos in the Ancient Greek Antikythera Mechanism”) is the most complete proposed reconstruction of the front plate planetary display. It is open-access and includes detailed gear train diagrams and a rendered animation of the full device in operation. The UCL team’s website has supplementary materials including a 3D model. Freeth has been the most persistent and methodical investigator of the mechanism over the past two decades, and his team’s reconstruction represents the current scholarly consensus, though not every detail is universally accepted.
🌊
The Antikythera Wreck — Ongoing Excavation
The wreck site is managed by the Greek Ministry of Culture and the Ephorate of Underwater Antiquities. The 2014–2017 Return to Antikythera project, a collaboration with the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, used custom-built dive suits allowing six-hour bottom times at 55 metres and recovered new objects including a bronze spear-arm and human skeletal remains. A third skull recovered in 2024 has enabled palaeogenomic analysis. The site is estimated to be less than half excavated. The possibility of more mechanism fragments remains officially open.
📍
Rhodes as the Engineering Centre
The island of Rhodes in the second and first centuries BCE was the pre-eminent scientific and technical centre of the Greek world — the equivalent, in some respects, of a modern university research hub combined with a precision manufacturing district. The astronomer Hipparchus worked there from around 162 to 127 BCE and produced the solar and lunar motion models whose values are embedded in the Antikythera Mechanism’s gear ratios. Cicero studied there under the rhetorician Posidonius, who also described mechanical planetary devices. The circumstantial case for Rhodian manufacture is strong. The island’s technical tradition does not survive Rome’s progressive integration of the eastern Mediterranean.
⏰
The Medieval Clockmakers’ Reinvention
When European clockmakers began building complex astronomical clocks in the fourteenth century — Richard of Wallingford at St Albans (1330s), Giovanni de’ Dondi in Padua (1364) — they did so with no known knowledge of the Antikythera Mechanism, which lay on the seabed and would not be found for another five centuries. They reinvented epicyclic gearing, calendar computation, and eclipse prediction from scratch. De’ Dondi’s astrarium took sixteen years to build and contained seven faces showing the positions of the sun, moon, and five planets — the same set as the Antikythera Mechanism’s proposed planetary display. The parallel reinvention is not coincidence: it reflects the same astronomical and mathematical knowledge base, transmitted through Arabic scholarship and recovered Latin texts, converging on the same mechanical solution independently.
