The Man Who Fed Half the World, Then Gassed It

Fritz Haber invented the process that sustains roughly half of all humans alive today. He also personally supervised the first large-scale chemical weapons attack in history. His institute’s pesticide research became Zyklon B. His wife shot herself in protest. The Nazis then expelled him for being Jewish. He died in a Basel hotel room in January 1934, en route to Palestine, broken. He remains one of history’s most difficult people to think about clearly.

Fritz Haber’s nitrogen fixation process — the Haber-Bosch method, demonstrated in 1909 — enabled synthetic fertilizer that sustains an estimated 3.5 to 4 billion people alive today, roughly half of humanity. The same scientist personally supervised the first large-scale chlorine gas attack in history at Ypres on 22 April 1915, developed Germany’s entire chemical warfare program in WWI, and left behind a research lineage that the Nazis converted into Zyklon B — the gas used to murder more than a million people in death camps, including members of his own family.

TL;DR

In 1909, Fritz Haber demonstrated a process for pulling nitrogen out of the air and converting it into ammonia — solving, at a stroke, the agricultural constraint that limited how many people the Earth could feed. The Haber-Bosch process that followed made synthetic fertilizer possible at industrial scale. Without it, roughly half of the humans alive today would not exist. The same mind that did this spent the First World War designing chemical weapons, personally stood at the front to supervise the first chlorine gas attack in history, and ran a weapons program that, by 1918, had introduced mustard gas, phosgene, and dozens of other agents into modern warfare. His wife — Germany’s first female chemistry PhD — shot herself in protest. He left for the Eastern Front the next morning. The Nobel Prize committee awarded him the prize for chemistry the same year the war ended. He died as a stateless exile, expelled by the country he had spent his life serving, carrying a German Iron Cross he had won helping Germany kill people with gas.

Section 01

The Story

Fritz Haber was born in 1868 in Breslau (now Wrocław, Poland) to a prosperous Jewish merchant family. He converted to Christianity as a young man, as many assimilated German Jews of his generation did, and pursued a scientific career with a single-minded ambition shaped partly by the desire to belong fully to German intellectual life. By the early 1900s he was a professor at the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, respected but not yet famous, working on physical chemistry problems that most colleagues considered too theoretical to matter.

The problem that changed everything was nitrogen. By 1900, the world’s agricultural system was running up against a hard ceiling: the supply of fixed nitrogen — nitrogen in forms that plants can actually absorb and use — was limited to natural deposits of Chilean saltpeter and guano, which were being depleted faster than they could be replaced. Without fixed nitrogen, soil exhausts itself. Without soil, crops fail. The German chemist Wilhelm Ostwald had calculated that the global population could not long exceed the nitrogen supply. Haber began attacking the problem of synthesising ammonia — a usable nitrogen compound — directly from the air.

In July 1909, Haber demonstrated his process to engineers from the chemical company BASF. It produced ammonia drop by drop, at 125 mL per hour. Within four years it was an industrial plant feeding the world.

The demonstration worked. Carl Bosch, an engineer at BASF, spent the next four years solving the industrial engineering problems that Haber’s laboratory apparatus could not — building reactors capable of sustaining pressures of 150 to 350 atmospheres and temperatures of 450 to 550 degrees Celsius at commercial scale. The Oppau plant began producing ammonia in 1913. By 1914, Germany was producing approximately 20 tonnes of ammonia per day, enough to begin transforming European agriculture. The rest of the world followed over the subsequent decades. The Haber-Bosch process now produces around 150 million tonnes of ammonia annually. Most of that becomes fertilizer. The nitrogen in approximately half of all humans alive today passed through this process before it entered the food chain.

Then the war came. The German military, facing a British naval blockade that cut off Chilean saltpeter imports, discovered that Haber-Bosch ammonia could be oxidised into nitric acid — which could then be used to make explosives. Haber’s invention, designed to grow food, was keeping Germany’s munitions factories running. Haber himself pivoted. He believed chemical weapons could shorten the war and ultimately reduce total casualties. He may have genuinely believed this. He threw himself into the German chemical warfare program with the same systematic intensity he had brought to nitrogen chemistry. By early 1915 he had assembled a team of over 150 scientists and 1,300 technical personnel. He was building a weapons program from scratch.

On 22 April 1915, near the Belgian town of Ypres, German forces released approximately 168 tonnes of chlorine gas from 5,730 cylinders along a four-mile front. The gas drifted toward Allied lines in a greenish-yellow cloud. French and Algerian troops, having never seen anything like it, broke and ran or suffocated where they stood. The attack caused tens of thousands of casualties. Fritz Haber was present at the front, supervising the operation. It was the first large-scale chemical weapons attack in history. When he returned to Berlin, there was a dinner party.

His wife Clara was also a chemist — Germany’s first woman to receive a doctorate in chemistry, from the University of Breslau in 1900, which she had completed with a dissertation on the solubility of heavy metal salts. She had been a working scientist who, after their marriage in 1901, had largely subordinated her career to assist her husband’s. She had publicly called his chemical weapons work “a perversion of the ideals of science” and “a sign of barbarity, corrupting the very discipline which ought to bring new insights into life.” On the night of 1–2 May 1915, after an argument with Haber, Clara went into the garden of their home and shot herself in the chest with his military pistol. Their twelve-year-old son Hermann ran to her and held her as she died. The following morning, Fritz Haber left for the Eastern Front to supervise Germany’s first gas attack against the Russians.

The war ended in 1918. In that same year, Haber received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry — formally awarded in 1920 when the prize ceremony resumed — for the synthesis of ammonia from its elements. Allied scientists protested vigorously. Ernest Rutherford, who would later refuse to shake Haber’s hand at Cambridge, was among those who objected to honouring a man they held responsible for mass atrocities. The Nobel committee declined to address the chemical warfare record in its presentation. The award was for the food, not the gas.

The final irony came with a precise and terrible efficiency. In 1933, Hitler’s government passed the Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service, requiring the dismissal of Jews from government positions. Haber had converted to Christianity decades earlier. He had served Germany with exceptional dedication. He held the Iron Cross. None of it mattered. He resigned from the directorship of the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Physical Chemistry in a letter that stated, with restrained dignity, that he had always selected his collaborators “on the basis of their intelligence and their character and not on the basis of their grandmothers.” He spent months at Cambridge, where Rutherford declined to meet him. He accepted a directorship at the Sieff Research Institute in Mandatory Palestine. He died of a heart attack in a Basel hotel room on 29 January 1934, aged 65, before he reached it. He had been expelled from Germany eight months earlier.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The core chemistry of the Haber process is conceptually simple. Atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) and hydrogen gas (H₂) react to form ammonia (NH₃): N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. The reaction releases energy — it is exothermic — which means thermodynamics is not the problem. The problem is kinetics. The triple bond in molecular nitrogen (N≡N) is one of the strongest chemical bonds known, with a bond energy of around 945 kJ per mole. At room temperature and pressure, atmospheric nitrogen is essentially inert: it simply will not react fast enough for any practical purpose.

The Kinetics Problem — and Haber’s Solution

Higher temperatures break bonds faster, which speeds the reaction — but the reaction is exothermic, so higher temperatures also shift the equilibrium toward the reactants, reducing yield. Higher pressures push the equilibrium toward ammonia (fewer moles of gas on the product side) and increase reaction rate — but extreme pressures require engineering that did not yet exist. Haber’s insight was to find the narrow operating window — around 450–550°C and 150–350 atmospheres — where the reaction runs fast enough and yields enough ammonia to be practical. His catalyst, refined by BASF’s Alwin Mittasch through thousands of experiments, was iron promoted with potassium oxide and aluminium oxide. It remains the catalyst used today, over a century later.

Carl Bosch’s engineering contribution was at least as significant as Haber’s chemistry. Building reactors that could hold 300 atmospheres of pressure while running at 500 degrees Celsius, processing corrosive hydrogen at scale, without catastrophic failure — that was a materials and mechanical engineering problem that required four years and thousands of failures to solve. The Oppau plant, when it opened in 1913, was the most complex chemical facility ever built. Bosch received his own Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1931 for his contribution to high-pressure industrial chemistry.

The population mathematics follow from the agriculture. Nitrogen is the limiting nutrient for most crop plants — the element that, when added, produces the most growth response per unit applied. Before synthetic fertilizer, the nitrogen available to agriculture came from manure, legume crop rotations (which fix nitrogen via root bacteria), and mined natural deposits. These sources could support a global population of roughly 3 to 4 billion people at the dietary standards of the early twentieth century. The Haber-Bosch process removed that ceiling. The global population reached 2.5 billion in 1950, 5 billion in 1987, and 8 billion in 2022. The nitrogen in approximately half of those 8 billion bodies’ proteins was fixed by the Haber-Bosch process before entering the food chain. Vaclav Smil, the leading analyst of global nitrogen flows, puts the proportion at around 40%; Jan Willem Erisman and colleagues, writing in Nature Geoscience in 2008, estimated 48%. The range is 40–50%. It represents the largest single human intervention in the global nitrogen cycle in Earth’s history.

The same chemistry that produces fertilizer produces explosives. Ammonia oxidises into nitric acid via the Ostwald process, and nitric acid is the basis of most modern high explosives — TNT, RDX, nitroglycerin. When the British naval blockade in 1914 cut off Germany’s Chilean saltpeter imports, Germany was facing the exhaustion of its pre-war explosives stocks within months. The Haber-Bosch ammonia plants pivoted to nitric acid production. Some historians argue the process extended the war by two to three years by keeping German munitions factories operational; others note the same technology was available to the Allies and argue the counterfactual is less clean. What is clear is that the same molecular reaction feeds half the world and arms its armies.

Section 03

The Connections

The Zyklon B lineage. After the war, Haber’s institute continued research into hydrogen cyanide as a pesticide — a civilian application of chemical warfare research. This work contributed to the founding of DEGESCH (Deutsche Gesellschaft für Schädlingsbekämpfung — German Pest Control Company) in 1919, which developed Zyklon A and later Zyklon B: hydrogen cyanide absorbed into a porous carrier, packaged in sealed canisters for safe handling as a fumigant. Haber died in January 1934 and had no personal involvement in Zyklon B’s weaponisation; the Nazis began using it to murder people in gas chambers in 1942, eight years after his death. But the institutional lineage runs directly from his institute to the product, and members of his own family — including his half-sister’s daughter, her husband, and their two children — perished in Nazi concentration camps. The historical record does not confirm whether they died specifically by Zyklon B, but the irony is present regardless of the precise mechanism.

Clara Immerwahr and the question of complicity. Clara’s story is not a footnote to Fritz’s. She received her doctorate magna cum laude, an achievement that required navigating a university system that was structurally hostile to women; male candidates completed the same programme in six semesters, hers took eight. She had the scientific credentials to mount a genuine technical critique of Haber’s chemical weapons work, not merely a moral one, and her characterisation of it as “a perversion of the ideals of science” has been quoted in philosophy of science literature as one of the earliest articulate statements of scientific ethics in the modern sense. Her death was ruled a suicide in protest. Haber never spoke about it publicly. He remarried in 1917.

The Scientist’s Responsibility Problem

Haber is the sharpest test case in the history of science for a question that has no clean answer: is a scientist morally responsible for what their discoveries are used for? He did not merely invent something that was later weaponised without his knowledge — he personally designed and supervised the weapons program. At the same time, the same work underpins the food supply of half the planet. The standard framing of “dual use” research — where a single discovery has both beneficial and harmful applications — does not capture his case well, because in his case the same individual actively pursued both applications simultaneously, with full awareness of what each entailed.

The German-Jewish assimilation tragedy. Haber’s biography is also a case study in the limits of assimilation as a survival strategy. He converted, Germanised, served Germany with extraordinary devotion, won its highest military decorations, and was still expelled the moment the racial definition of Jewishness superseded all other criteria. Chaim Weizmann, who would become the first president of Israel, visited Haber at Cambridge after his exile and described a broken man who could not understand what had happened to him — a man whose entire identity had been constructed around being German, who had been told by his country that he was not. His final destination was to have been Palestine. He did not make it.

The nitrogen cycle as a planetary boundary. The long-term consequence of the Haber-Bosch process is still unfolding. The nitrogen it introduced into the global agricultural system does not stay where it is applied: it leaches into groundwater, runs into rivers, flows into coastal waters where it causes algal blooms and dead zones, and volatilises into nitrous oxide — a greenhouse gas 273 times more potent than CO₂ over a 100-year horizon. The planetary nitrogen boundary — one of the nine Earth system boundaries identified by the Stockholm Resilience Centre — has been exceeded. The same process that made the current human population possible is now a driver of the ecological disruption that population is causing. Haber solved the nitrogen supply problem. The nitrogen disposal problem is, as of 2026, still open.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

🧪
Clara Immerwahr — Germany’s First Female Chemistry PhD
Clara Immerwahr received her doctorate from the University of Breslau in 1900, the first woman the university had ever awarded a chemistry PhD. Her dissertation, on the solubility of slightly soluble heavy metal salts, was completed in eight semesters when male candidates finished in six. She later translated Haber’s work into English for international audiences. After her death in 1915, she was largely erased from the historical record — her role reduced to a footnote in her husband’s biography. The Clara Immerwahr Award, given annually by the Helmholtz Association for outstanding early-career female scientists, was established in 2010.
💨
The Second Battle of Ypres and the Birth of Chemical Warfare
The chlorine gas attack of 22 April 1915 opened a new category of warfare that no existing Geneva Convention language covered; the Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907 prohibited projectiles designed to diffuse asphyxiating gases but said nothing about releasing gas directly from cylinders. Both sides quickly developed response programs. By 1918, approximately 30% of German shells contained some form of gas — chlorine, phosgene, or mustard gas, a Haber-program development that caused more casualties than all other gas agents combined. The Chemical Weapons Convention, which now prohibits these weapons, was not opened for signature until 1993.
🌾
The Green Revolution and the Limits of Nitrogen
The mid-twentieth-century Green Revolution — the rapid development of high-yield crop varieties pioneered by Norman Borlaug, who received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970 — was made possible partly by the existence of synthetic nitrogen fertilizer. The new wheat and rice varieties were specifically bred to be short and stiff, so they could support heavy grain heads without falling over under the weight of fertilizer-boosted growth. Without Haber-Bosch ammonia, Borlaug’s seeds would have produced unimpressive yields. The two Nobel Prizes — Haber’s in chemistry in 1918, Borlaug’s in peace in 1970 — represent two parts of the same transformation.
🏆
The Nobel Prize’s Unresolved Tension
Haber’s is one of the most contested Nobel awards in history. The prize was for ammonia synthesis, announced in 1918, presented in 1920. Allied scientists, including British chemists who had spent the war on the receiving end of Germany’s gas program, protested publicly. The Nobel committee’s position — that the award was for a specific scientific achievement, not a whole life — established a precedent still debated today. Similar questions have arisen about prizes for DDT (Paul Hermann Müller, 1948), lobotomy (António Egas Moniz, 1949), and others. Haber’s case remains the sharpest, because the same research program, under his personal leadership, produced both the prize-winning chemistry and the weapons the committee was implicitly setting aside.
🌍
Planetary Nitrogen Boundaries — What Comes Next
The Stockholm Resilience Centre’s planetary boundaries framework (first published 2009, updated 2023) identifies nine Earth system processes that must remain within certain limits to maintain the stable conditions humans have depended on. Nitrogen flows — specifically the rate at which humans introduce reactive nitrogen into the biosphere via fertilizer — is one of the boundaries already exceeded. Coastal dead zones caused by nitrogen runoff now number over 400 worldwide. Nitrous oxide (N₂O) from agricultural soils is the third-largest greenhouse gas contributor. Research into biological nitrogen fixation — engineering crops that can fix their own nitrogen the way legumes do — is the leading candidate for eventually reducing dependence on synthetic fertilizer without crashing the food supply.
