The Volcano That Wrote Frankenstein and Invented the Bicycle

In April 1815, a mountain in Indonesia exploded with the force of 170,000 Hiroshima bombs — and the shockwave kept travelling for two more years, reshaping literature, transportation, migration, and the very colour of sunsets across the world.

In April 1815, Mount Tambora erupted in what remains the largest volcanic explosion in recorded history — ejecting so much sulphur into the stratosphere that it blocked sunlight across the Northern Hemisphere for two years, triggering snow in June, famine from Vermont to China, and the deaths of at least 90,000 people. The downstream effects were astonishing: a trapped Mary Shelley wrote Frankenstein during the endless dark summer of 1816; a horse-killing food crisis prompted Karl von Drais to invent the bicycle; mass emigration from New England seeded the American Midwest; and the first global cholera pandemic was triggered by a disrupted Indian monsoon. J.M.W. Turner's glowing apocalyptic sunsets were not artistic licence — they were accurate reproductions of a Tambora sky.

TL;DR

In April 1815, Mount Tambora erupted in Indonesia — the largest volcanic explosion in recorded history. It pumped enough sulphur dioxide into the stratosphere to block sunlight globally for two years, producing the "Year Without a Summer" in 1816: snowfall in June, famine from New England to China, and at least 90,000 deaths. The shockwaves kept rippling: a rain-trapped Mary Shelley wrote Frankenstein on the shores of Lake Geneva; a shortage of horses driven by crop failure inspired Karl von Drais to invent the bicycle; mass migration from starving Vermont families seeded the American Midwest; and a disrupted monsoon triggered the first global cholera pandemic. Even J.M.W. Turner's famous glowing sunsets turn out to be volcanically accurate.

Section 01

The Story: The Night the Mountain Disappeared

On the evening of April 10, 1815, the island of Sumbawa in what is now Indonesia shook with a sound heard 2,600 kilometres away — mistaken for cannon fire by British colonial officers in Java, who dispatched troops to investigate a battle that wasn't there. Mount Tambora, a 4,300-metre stratovolcano that had been dormant for centuries, had just detonated.

The eruption ejected an estimated 150 cubic kilometres of material — roughly 150 times more than Mount St. Helens in 1980 — earning it a rating of 7 on the Volcanic Explosivity Index, a scale that tops out at 8. The mountain itself lost more than 1,400 metres of its peak. Three kingdoms on Sumbawa were buried under pyroclastic flows and ash. Approximately 71,000 people died on the island in the immediate aftermath from burns, suffocation, and tsunami. Forests were flattened for hundreds of kilometres in every direction.

But the truly catastrophic part was invisible, and it moved slowly. The eruption blasted an estimated 60 megatons of sulphur dioxide into the stratosphere — high enough to escape the weather systems that would normally wash it out. At that altitude, SO₂ reacts with water vapour to form sulphate aerosols: fine, reflective particles that act like a planetary sunscreen. Over the following months, these aerosols spread around the globe in a thin veil, dimming sunlight and scattering solar radiation back into space.

By the spring of 1816, the aerosol veil had thickened and spread. Average global temperatures dropped 0.4–0.7°C — modest-sounding, but catastrophic at the margins of growing seasons. In parts of Europe and North America the local effect was far worse: summer temperatures in New England ran 3–5°C below normal. What followed was a year that felt like the world had forgotten how to be warm.

Section 02

The Mechanism: How a Volcano Dimmed the Sun

The key is not the ash but the gas. Most people imagine volcanic winters as caused by clouds of ash blocking the sun — but ash particles are heavy and fall out of the atmosphere within weeks. Sulphur dioxide is different. At stratospheric altitudes (above 15 km), SO₂ reacts with water vapour to form fine sulphate aerosol droplets. These droplets are light enough to persist in the stratosphere for one to three years and spread globally through stratospheric circulation.

Once distributed, they do two things: they scatter incoming solar radiation back into space (reducing total surface insolation) and they absorb outgoing infrared radiation, creating a complicated thermal pattern — cooling the surface while slightly warming the stratosphere. The net effect at ground level is less sunlight, lower temperatures, and disrupted precipitation patterns, because rainfall is driven by evaporation, which is driven by surface heating.

The disruption to precipitation is what spread the damage globally. In South Asia, the Indian summer monsoon — which feeds the agriculture of more than a billion people — depends on a precise temperature differential between the Indian Ocean and the Asian landmass. Tambora's aerosols disrupted this differential, delaying and weakening the 1816 monsoon. The resulting crop failure in the Ganges Delta is believed to have stimulated a new strain of cholera — triggering the First Cholera Pandemic, which spread from Bengal to Moscow and eventually to North America.

"The fowls went to roost at noon, and the candles were lighted as at midnight." — Lord Byron, describing the summer of 1816 in Geneva, shortly before writing his apocalyptic poem "Darkness"

The aerosol veil also had one remarkable visual side effect. Sulphate particles in the stratosphere scatter sunlight in ways that shift the spectrum of sunset light toward warmer reds and yellows. Scientists studying the paintings of J.M.W. Turner discovered that the red-to-green ratio in his painted sunsets spiked dramatically after the 1815 Tambora eruption — and again after later eruptions in 1831 and 1835. Turner wasn't being poetic. He was painting what he actually saw: a sky stained amber by an Indonesian volcano.

Section 03

The Connections: What One Summer Built

Frankenstein. In the summer of 1816, Lord Byron rented the Villa Diodati on Lake Geneva in Switzerland. Percy Bysshe Shelley arrived with his 18-year-old companion Mary Godwin and her step-sister Claire Clairmont; Byron's personal physician John Polidori rounded out the group. What they intended as a idyllic summer became something stranger. The "incessant rain" of that wet, ungenial summer — Byron's own description — kept the group indoors for days. Bored and confined, they challenged each other to write ghost stories. Mary Godwin's contribution was a dream about a scientist who animated dead tissue. She published it two years later as Frankenstein, or The Modern Prometheus. Polidori's contribution became The Vampyre — the founding text of the literary vampire. Both genres of horror fiction were born in a single bad-weather week caused by a volcano 10,000 kilometres away.

The Bicycle. In 1816, horses across Europe were being slaughtered — not by disease, but by starvation. Oat and hay crops had failed across Germany, making fodder impossibly expensive. Horses, the only means of mechanical ground transport at the time, became a luxury few could sustain. A German civil servant and inventor named Karl von Drais set himself the problem of replacing the horse. On June 12, 1817, he demonstrated his solution in Mannheim: a two-wheeled wooden frame with a seat, steered by a handlebar, propelled by the rider pushing their feet along the ground. He called it the Laufmaschine — "running machine." His biographer Hans-Erhard Lessing traced the invention explicitly to the horse famine of 1816. The bicycle, arguably the most efficient transport machine ever built, was a direct technological response to a volcanic winter.

American Westward Migration. In Vermont and across New England, the summer of 1816 broke the backs of farming families who were already stretched thin. Crops failed completely in two successive growing seasons. The state of Vermont alone saw an estimated 15,000 people emigrate, many heading to the more promising soils of the Ohio Valley, Indiana, and Illinois. Among them was the family of a young boy named Joseph Smith, who moved from Norwich, Vermont to Palmyra, New York — an event that, within fifteen years, would produce the Book of Mormon and the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. The first great surge of American westward expansion was, in part, a flight from a volcanic famine.

Byron's "Darkness." While Mary Shelley dreamed of Frankenstein, Byron sat at his window in the Villa Diodati and wrote one of the most remarkable poems of the Romantic era. Darkness (1816) opens: "I had a dream, which was not all a dream. / The bright sun was extinguish'd..." It imagines the total extinction of the sun, the death of all life, and two survivors who eat each other in the dark. It reads like climate fiction. It was essentially climate non-fiction.

Further Down the Hole

🦠

The First Cholera Pandemic

The disrupted 1816 Indian monsoon created conditions for a novel cholera strain to emerge in the Ganges Delta. It spread along trade routes to Persia, Russia, and eventually North America — the First Cholera Pandemic (1817–1824). Epidemiologists now trace its origins directly to the Tambora monsoon disruption.

🌅

Turner's Volcanic Sunsets

Scientists analysed 548 Turner paintings and found a statistically significant shift in his red/green colour ratios after each of three eruptions he lived through (Tambora 1815, Babuyan 1831, Cosiguina 1835). His "artistic" sunsets were optical reports on atmospheric chemistry.

🌋

The VEI-8 That Could End Civilisation

Tambora was VEI-7. A VEI-8 "supervolcano" — like Yellowstone, Toba, or the Campi Flegrei caldera under Naples — would eject ten times more material. The Toba eruption ~74,000 years ago may have reduced global human population to as few as 10,000 individuals. The geological record shows we've been lucky.

📖

Shelley's Second Apocalypse

The 1816 summer didn't just produce Frankenstein. Mary Shelley returned to the theme of catastrophic civilisational collapse in her 1826 novel The Last Man — a pandemic apocalypse set in the 21st century. Written barely a decade after Tambora, it imagines a world destroyed not by monsters but by plague. She was ten years ahead of Darwin and 200 years ahead of COVID.

🌽

"Eighteen Hundred and Froze to Death"

New Englanders had a dark nickname for 1816. Some Vermont villages survived on boiled nettles and hedgehogs. The cold was so reliably catastrophic that the year entered regional folk memory as a proper noun. Old Farmer's Almanac issues from the period are full of desperate tone that now reads like historical dark comedy.
