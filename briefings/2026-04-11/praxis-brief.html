01 Key Ideas & Insights
Newsletter · High Performance Founder

You're Tracking the Wrong Fitness Metric — And So Is Almost Everyone

Dan Go (High Performance Founder, April 10) draws on coach and researcher Steve Magness to make a point that cuts through the wearable-device obsession: the major studies linking fitness to longevity — including research on 750,000 veterans and a separate 120,000-person study — did not measure VO2 max. They measured performance. Peak treadmill speed. Incline sustained. Mile time.

"VO2 max tells you the size of your engine. But the studies people cite when they talk about it? Most of them didn't measure VO2 max at all. They measured performance." — Dan Go / Steve Magness

The practical implication is liberating. You don't need a lab, a mask, or a $500 wearable producing an estimated number. You need to run a hard mile, do a 5K, or bike a timed distance — and track whether it gets easier. Magness argues that mile time is actually a better predictor of longevity than VO2 max because it captures not just central cardiovascular capacity, but also peripheral muscle efficiency, lactate threshold, and speed — a more complete picture of how your body handles sustained effort.

  • VO2 max is a proxy for a proxy. Fitness researchers use it because it was measurable in controlled settings. In the real world, performance is more accessible and more accurate.
  • Free tracking still works. A hard mile once a month. A 5K on a familiar route. These tell you more than a Garmin estimate.
  • Frequency > intensity. Magness: train as many days as possible at a pace you could hold a conversation, adding one moderately hard session per week. The research doesn't support going harder — it supports going more often.
Newsletter · High Performance Founder + Research

The Ozempic Dependency Trap: What the Meta-Analysis Data Actually Shows

Also from Dan Go's April 10 newsletter — and confirmed by a multi-source research sweep: a meta-analysis of 37 studies (published across Oxford, Cambridge, and Lancet eClinicalMedicine) found that people who stop GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic and Wegovy regain all lost weight within approximately 1.5 to 2 years, at a rate of ~0.4 kg per month (0.8 kg/month for newer semaglutide and tirzepatide formulations). Every cardiometabolic risk marker climbs back to baseline.

"The drug isn't the problem. It does exactly what it's supposed to do: kill your appetite, create a calorie deficit, drop the weight. The problem is what's not happening underneath." — Dan Go

The analysis is more damning than the headline number. Weight regain after stopping GLP-1s is 0.3 kg/month faster than after ending behavioral programs like diet and exercise support. The drug suppresses hunger signals without building any of the hormonal or behavioral infrastructure that would sustain results after discontinuation. Muscle mass lost during the low-calorie period isn't replaced automatically. The Cambridge analysis found patients retain just 40% of lost weight a year after stopping.

The implication isn't anti-GLP-1 — it's structural. The drug creates a window of metabolic flexibility. Whether that window is used to build new habits (protein targets, resistance training, meal structure) determines whether the intervention is permanent or cyclical. Dan Go's framework: use the window, don't mistake it for the result.

Newsletter · James Clear 3-2-1

Observation is a Trainable Skill — And Most People Are Training the Wrong Direction

James Clear's April 9 3-2-1 newsletter leads with a deceptively simple insight about how we filter reality. His argument: observation is a skill, and most people practice it unconsciously — in one direction. If you spend years noticing why things won't work, spotting the latest distressing news, or fixating on what went wrong, you become genuinely expert at those observations. The brain doesn't distinguish useful from harmful training.

"Even if you're not a negative person, you may be skilled at noticing negative things. But you can train your eye toward the opportunities each day quietly presents." — James Clear

The complementary quote from this issue: Seneca — "If you do not know which port you are seeking, no wind is favorable." Clear pairs these deliberately: without clarity of purpose, you can't direct your observational training. You end up expert at noticing things that don't serve you, pointed at destinations you never chose.

  • The asymmetry of reputation: "A good reputation is acquired by many actions; and can be lost by one." — Philip Dormer Stanhope. Consistency is the only strategy.
  • "I work harder, but you're busier." The sharpest productivity distinction in the issue — a single line that summarizes everything wrong with performative effort.
  • The question Clear leaves open: What is the most important conversation you are currently postponing? This is worth sitting with.
02 Strategy & Practice
Framework · Mark Manson

Five Productivity Principles That Actually Survive Contact With Reality

Mark Manson (April 9) writes as someone with ADHD who has been self-employed his entire adult life — which means his productivity framework has been stress-tested against decades of failure modes. He opens by cataloguing the failures: 4AM wake-ups, colour-coded calendars, mushroom coffee in cold showers. None of it worked. "It just turned my avoidance into an art project." His five surviving principles:

  • Work with your biology, not against it. If morning doesn't work for you, don't force it. What works for someone else is information, not instruction.
  • Embrace the minimum viable action. Take the smallest action that feels doable in this exact moment. Manson's key insight: "Motivation is not just the cause of action — it's also the effect." Start tiny; let the state follow the behaviour.
  • Only improve what you measure — but choose metrics carefully. Incentives are powerful, and it's easy to game your own system. Pick metrics that are hard to fake.
  • Don't fight your emotions; leverage them. When you're genuinely excited about your work, you'll do more of it. Let passion anchor direction rather than fighting to override it.
  • More is not always better. Working more hours creates negative returns beyond a threshold. Pushing yourself too hard degrades both output quality and emotional state. The marginal hour is often the worst one.
Framework

The Minimum Viable Action Principle Applied Beyond Productivity

Manson's minimum viable action principle deserves extension. It's most commonly framed as a productivity hack — start with 5 minutes, let momentum build. But the underlying mechanism is more fundamental: action changes internal state, and internal state is the actual constraint on almost every goal we fail at.

This connects directly to Dan Go's framing of GLP-1 drugs: the drug is the minimum viable action for weight loss (suppress appetite → create deficit), but without the underlying state change (new food relationship, protein habits, structural meals), the action is temporary. The same dynamic appears in fitness: the minimum viable action is showing up for a short, easy session — but without a state change in how you think about movement, the streak breaks the moment difficulty rises.

The question isn't "what's the smallest step?" It's "what's the smallest step that changes my identity about this?" Those aren't always the same thing.

James Clear identified this in Atomic Habits: the most powerful habits are the ones that carry identity evidence. Every time you run a mile and track the time, you're also gathering evidence that you're a person who measures their fitness. The metric and the identity reinforce each other.

Fitness · Tracking

The Free Longevity Metric: Your Mile Time

Per Steve Magness and the longevity research underpinning this week's Dan Go newsletter: track a hard mile (or equivalent aerobic effort) monthly. Log the time. That's it. No app required. The research that drives the "VO2 max" obsession was actually measuring this — they just called it something more technical. A timed mile captures VO2 max, lactate threshold, anaerobic capacity, and muscle efficiency simultaneously. It's a free, accessible, and more robust longevity proxy than any wearable estimate.

GLP-1 · Health Policy

The GLP-1 Permanence Problem Will Reshape Health Insurance

The meta-analysis data on Ozempic discontinuation (full weight regain within 1.5–2 years) creates a structural insurance problem: insurers covering GLP-1 drugs are essentially funding a continuous, indefinite subscription to weight management — not a course of treatment. As coverage mandates expand (particularly under ACA frameworks), expect cost-sharing structures and "maintenance protocol" requirements to emerge. The pharmacological answer to obesity is real; the sustainability and equity questions are still being written.

Work · Cognition

The "Busier vs. Harder" Distinction Is Becoming a Management Lens

James Clear's throwaway line — "I work harder, but you're busier" — is quietly becoming a diagnostic framework in productivity and management literature. Busyness is measurable (meetings, messages, visible effort); hard work is output-defined and often invisible. As remote and hybrid work makes visibility harder to fake, and AI tools handle more of the volume-based busywork, the remaining human value proposition concentrates in the "work harder" category. The organizations figuring this out now are ahead.
