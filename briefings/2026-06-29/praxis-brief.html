Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

The frame you use to read a situation determines what moves are visible to you — and this week three independent writers circle that truth from completely different directions. Cedric Chin argues that what separates experts from novices isn't raw knowledge but frame construction: experts see affordances and possibilities that trained observers literally cannot access. Ted Lamade shows that our inability to read probabilities isn't only a weakness — people who pursue long-odds dreams aren't irrational, they're operating from a different frame where the pursuit itself is the point. James Clear demonstrates it in practice: a hard constraint (2–4 hours instead of 20) forced a reframe of what 'writing' meant, and that reframe produced a more popular output than unconstrained grinding ever had. The common thread: the fastest leverage isn't working harder inside your current frame — it's learning to notice when the frame is the limiting factor, and building the skill to construct a better one.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Essay · Cedric Chin · “Sensemaking as the Heart of Expertise” · Commoncog · Jun 12, 2026

Experts don't just know more — they see a different situation entirely

Cedric Chin's latest Commoncog essay lands a precise diagnostic: what separates the expert from the novice is not years logged or facts memorised, but frame construction — the tacit, largely invisible process of deciding what counts as data before you start reasoning. “The designation of 'data' is something you assign during the process of framing,” Chin writes. Without the right frame, relevant information is simply not there for you — like a rock climber who cannot see handholds that an expert reads immediately.

The implications run deeper than they first appear. The Naturalistic Decision Making field, Chin notes — drawing on Gary Klein's work and Jared Peterson's documentation of it — found that “how people made sense of situations often mattered more than deliberating over predefined options.” Experts under pressure don't run through pros-and-cons lists; they construct a frame, which makes the right move obvious. The gap between novice and expert is not processing speed — it is the frame being applied upstream of any decision.

Chin's practical proposal: train frame construction by reading curated cases (both failures and successes), recording your own sensemaking before seeing the expert's read, then comparing the two. The dissonance between your frame and the expert's is where learning actually happens — not in the comparison of conclusions, but in the comparison of what each person chose to notice.

The bottleneck in expertise is earlier than most people look. You cannot reason your way to the right answer from inside the wrong frame. The work is upstream: building the perceptual vocabulary that makes the relevant information visible in the first place.

Essay · Ted Lamade · “Our Achilles Heel” · Collaborative Fund · Jun 10, 2026

We misread odds chronically — and the same blindspot powers our best decisions

Ted Lamade, writing at Collaborative Fund, opens with a sharp observation: most people are terrible at internalising statistical reality. With roughly 25,000 high school valedictorians graduating annually and only 15,000 Ivy League spots available, he notes, “40% of all valedictorians don't have a spot” — yet the families who receive rejections still experience them as anomalies. Sports franchises, investment managers, and college admissions coaches have all learned to profit from exactly this blindspot.

But Lamade resists the tidy conclusion that we should simply get better at probability. “Many of humanity's greatest achievements look irrational” through a probability lens alone. The American Revolution, the first transatlantic crossing, most category-defining companies — none passed a strict expected-value test at inception. The people who pursued them weren't ignoring the odds; they were operating from a different frame, one in which “the pursuit matters as much as the outcome.” Inside that frame, the calculation changes entirely.

The question Lamade leaves standing: when should you trust the odds, and when should you challenge them? His answer is not a formula but a frame-check — “If you're chasing a dream because you genuinely love the pursuit, the odds matter less.” The frame you're in (outcome-maximiser vs. pursuit-valuer) determines whether a low-probability bet is foolish or foundational.

Most advice about probability tells you to update your beliefs. Lamade's deeper point is that before you can reason well about odds, you have to know which frame you're reasoning from — because the frame determines what the odds are of.

Newsletter · James Clear · 3-2-1 Thursday · Jun 25, 2026

The constraint forced the reframe — and the reframe produced the better output

James Clear's June 25 issue contains one of the clearest case studies in frame construction you'll read this week. For years, he spent 20+ hours writing each article. Then he imposed a hard constraint: “If I only had 2 to 4 hours per week to write my newsletter, what would I do?” That artificial limit didn't just force prioritisation — it forced a reframe of what writing was for. The result was the 3-2-1 format, which became far more popular than the long-form articles that had consumed five times the effort.

The same issue carries a structural companion: “Your first attempt might not be very good, but nobody's early work is good. There will always be a gap between where you are and where you want to be. And the bridge between that gap is courage.” Taken together, these two ideas describe the same underlying move — the gap between current output and desired mastery is a frame problem as much as a skill problem. Applying the constraint reframes the question from “how do I make this better?” to “what does this actually need to be?”

His closing question for June 25 lands the point precisely: “Where is your energy better spent right now: pushing harder or planting yourself in better ground?” That is not a productivity question. It is a frame question. And it only becomes answerable once you step outside the frame you're currently in.

When constraints produce better outputs than abundance does, the constraint isn't the cause — the reframe it forced is. Clear's 3-2-1 existed as a possibility inside every 20-hour week; the constraint made it visible.

Newsletter · James Clear · 3-2-1 Thursday · Jun 18, 2026

Story and attention are the two levers — and both are frame operations

Clear's June 18 issue adds the psychological layer. “Getting what you want out of life largely boils down to (1) the story you tell yourself and (2) where you direct your attention,” he writes. “What story do you tell yourself about what has happened to you, what you're capable of, and what you hope to achieve?” Strip the self-help register and what remains is precisely Cedric Chin's frame-construction argument — the story is the frame, and the frame determines what data gets treated as relevant.

The convergence across all three writers this week is not coincidental. Chin's expertise research, Lamade's probability analysis, and Clear's practice advice are each describing the same upstream mechanism: before skill, before strategy, before optimisation, there is the act of deciding what situation you are in. That act — frame construction — is the leverage point most people skip over entirely, because it is invisible to you while you are inside it.

This is the week's convergence thesis, stated plainly: the frame is prior to the decision, the skill, and the outcome. Getting better at anything faster means getting better at noticing which frame you're in — and building the range to construct a better one.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · drawn from Cedric Chin (Commoncog) and Gary Klein's RPD research

Train frame construction, not just pattern recognition

Most deliberate practice focuses on execution — repeating actions until performance improves. Chin's research points to a different kind of training that sits upstream: practising the act of constructing the frame before you solve anything. Here is how to build that muscle this week.

  • Pick a domain you want to get faster in. Find 5–10 case studies from that domain — mix of failures and successes. Business strategy, trading setups, legal precedents, clinical diagnoses — the format matters less than the two-ended spread.
  • Write your read before you see the expert's. For each case, record what you notice, what you think matters, and what move you'd make. Commit it in writing. This is the exercise: you are making your frame explicit so you can examine it.
  • Compare frames, not just conclusions. When you read the expert's analysis, don't just check if your conclusion matched. Ask: what did they notice that you didn't? What did they weight that you ignored? The gap between your frame and theirs is where the expertise lives — and where the learning happens.
  • Run this for 30 days on the same domain. The goal isn't to memorise cases. It is to build the perceptual vocabulary that makes the relevant signals visible in real time, before you start reasoning.

Most skill-building happens after the frame is set. This practice trains the frame itself — which is what actually sets the ceiling on how fast you can improve.

Practice · drawn from Ted Lamade (Collab Fund) and James Clear (3-2-1)

Audit your frame before you audit your effort

When something isn't working — a project stalling, a goal elusive, a habit not sticking — the default response is to apply more effort. Lamade and Clear both suggest a prior question: are you in the right frame to begin with? This card turns that into a concrete audit.

  • Name the frame you're currently using. Write it out: “I am treating this as a _____ problem.” Outcome or pursuit? Productivity or identity? Competition or contribution? Making it explicit is the first move — you can't interrogate a frame you can't see.
  • Apply Lamade's pursuit test. Ask: “Would I still do this if I knew the probability of the outcome I want is under 10%?” If yes, you're in a pursuit frame and the odds are a secondary concern. If no, you're in an outcome frame — which is fine, but then the odds need to be in your favour before you commit significant resource.
  • Apply Clear's constraint test. Take your current approach and remove 80% of the resource (time, money, attention). What would you do instead? If the constrained version is actually better, you were in the wrong frame — the abundance was hiding the reframe you needed.
  • Ask Clear's ground question weekly. “Where is my energy better spent right now: pushing harder or planting myself in better ground?” This is a frame audit, not a motivation check. Run it every Sunday before the next week starts.

Book

Gary Klein — Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions (MIT Press, 1998)

The foundational text behind Chin's essay. Klein's Recognition Primed Decision model — built from studying firefighters, military commanders, and ICU nurses under time pressure — showed that expert decision-making is not deliberation between options. It is pattern-triggered simulation, driven by the frame the expert constructs. Nearly three decades old and still one of the most useful books written about how skilled judgment actually works under pressure.

🔁
Framework

Outcome Frame vs. Pursuit Frame (Lamade)

A simple two-by-two for any high-stakes decision under uncertainty. Ask: is this an outcome frame (success = hitting the target; failure = missing it) or a pursuit frame (the activity itself carries value independent of outcome)? The answer changes your relationship to odds, to timeline, to failure, and to what “quitting at the right time” means. Useful for investment decisions, career bets, creative projects, and long-duration personal goals. Clarifying which frame you're in before committing is one of the most underrated due-diligence steps.

🎯
Framework

The Constraint Reframe Test (James Clear)

A portable self-audit: take whatever you're currently working on and remove 80% of the resource (time, budget, attention, team). What would you do instead? If the constrained version is clearly better, you were inside a frame that was hiding the simpler, more effective approach. Clear discovered the 3-2-1 format this way. The constraint didn't cause the insight — it forced the reframe that made the insight visible. Run this test on any project that feels stuck or over-resourced.

04 On the Horizon
AI

AI as a frame-construction partner, not just an answer machine

If Chin is right that expertise is upstream frame construction, then the most valuable use of AI is not retrieving answers inside your existing frame — it is surfacing frames you haven't considered. The tools that make this possible (multi-perspective prompting, structured devil's-advocate, case-based comparison) are underused because most users interact with AI as a search engine. As AI gets better at constructing coherent frames from domain knowledge, the people who benefit most will be those using it to interrogate their own framing, not just to confirm it.

Mind

Expertise development will be redesigned around frame training, not repetition

The dominant model of skill acquisition — deliberate practice, spaced repetition, volume of output — addresses the execution layer. Chin's sensemaking research points to a different curriculum: structured exposure to failure and success cases with forced pre-expert sensemaking, so learners train the upstream frame rather than the downstream move. Expect this to become a more explicit part of professional training in medicine, law, trading, and strategy over the next decade — especially where AI is handling the execution-level tasks and human judgment is needed at the frame level.

Finance

The pursuit-frame argument is a rebuttal to expected-value investing — and it has limits

Lamade's “the pursuit matters as much as the outcome” framing is genuinely useful for long-duration personal bets where the journey itself compounds. But it is also the frame that allows bad financial decisions to feel meaningful. The risk: pursuit-framing can justify staying in a position far past the point where the probability calculus has definitively turned against you. Knowing which frame you're in is the first step; the second step is asking whether you're in that frame because you chose it, or because the loss has made outcome-framing too painful to sustain.

Work

Constraint design as a leadership skill

Clear's constraint-forced-reframe insight has an organisational parallel: teams with unconstrained scope and budget rarely produce the frame-shift that teams with tight constraints routinely do. As remote and async work expands, and as AI handles more of the execution layer, the managerial skill that will matter is constraint design — setting the frame conditions that force better frames from teams. This is different from goal-setting and different from resource allocation. It is a meta-skill: designing the environment so that the right frame becomes the natural one to construct.
