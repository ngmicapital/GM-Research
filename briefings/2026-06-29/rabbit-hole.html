Forgetting costs energy: the physics hidden inside every delete key

In 1961 an IBM physicist proved that erasing one bit of information must release heat — and it took fifty years for anyone to measure it.

Deleting a file generates heat, and this is not a bug in your laptop — it is a law of physics. In 1961 Rolf Landauer proved at IBM that erasing one bit of information must dissipate a minimum of kT ln 2 of heat into the environment, connecting the abstract world of information theory to the physical laws of thermodynamics for the first time.

TL;DR

In 1961 Rolf Landauer showed that information is not just a pattern — it is a physical thing, and erasing it has a thermodynamic cost. Every time a computer overwrites a bit, it must release at least kT ln 2 of heat (about 3 zeptojoules at room temperature). Modern chips exceed that minimum by roughly a billion times. The gap is not a quirk of bad engineering — it is where civilisation's entire energy budget for computation currently lives.

Section 01

The Demon and the Bookkeeper

In 1867 James Clerk Maxwell, the Scottish physicist who had just finished unifying electricity and magnetism, posed a thought experiment that would haunt physics for the next 115 years. Imagine a tiny demon, he wrote, stationed at a small trapdoor between two chambers of gas at equal temperature. The demon watches the molecules. When a fast one approaches from the right, it opens the door; when a slow one approaches from the left, it opens the door. Over time, all the fast (hot) molecules pile up on the left and all the slow (cold) ones on the right — two chambers of different temperature, from which a heat engine could extract work, all apparently for free. The second law of thermodynamics — the rule that entropy never decreases in a closed system — had seemingly been violated by nothing more than careful bookkeeping.

This logical irreversibility is associated with physical irreversibility and requires a minimal heat generation, per machine cycle, typically of the order of kT for each irreversible function.
— Rolf Landauer, IBM Journal of Research and Development, 1961

For sixty years the demon refused to die. In 1929 the Hungarian physicist Leo Szilard stripped the scenario to a single molecule bouncing in a box, inserted a movable piston in the middle, and showed that the demon's measurement of which side the molecule occupied could be used to extract exactly kT ln 2 of work from a heat bath — a tiny but real amount of energy, 2.9 × 10⁻²¹ joules at room temperature. Szilard identified this as the first place where information and physics touched: if the demon acquires information, that information must have a physical consequence. What he could not nail down was where exactly the entropy is paid.

The answer came in 1961 from Rolf Landauer, a physicist at IBM Research. Working not on thought experiments but on the practical question of how computing machines generate heat, Landauer identified the precise moment of physical irreversibility: not the measurement, but the erasure. When a bit is reset from an unknown state (0 or 1) to a known state (say, 0), two possible configurations of the world collapse into one. That collapse — the reduction of possibilities — is what costs energy. The demon's ledger must always balance, and the balance is paid in heat.

Section 02

The Mechanism: Why Forgetting Costs Energy

The logic is elegant and unavoidable. A single classical bit can be in one of two states: 0 or 1. When you write a bit — setting it to a known value regardless of what it was — you perform a logically irreversible operation: the output (say, 0) does not uniquely determine the input (it could have been 0 or 1). That loss of information about the past is not just a bookkeeping inconvenience. By the second law of thermodynamics, any process that reduces the number of distinguishable states of a physical system must increase the entropy of the environment — and the minimum way to do that is to release heat. The formula Landauer derived is simple: the minimum heat dissipated per bit erasure is Q ≥ kBT ln 2, where kB is Boltzmann's constant and T is the temperature of the surrounding environment.

The Landauer bound at room temperature

At 20 °C (293 K), kBT ln 2 ≈ 2.87 × 10⁻²¹ joules — roughly 3 zeptojoules, or 0.018 electron volts. A modern Intel or Apple Silicon transistor switching dissipates on the order of 10⁻¹² joules per operation — about one billion times the theoretical minimum. The entire gap between today's chips and the physical limit of computation lives in that ratio. (Source: Landauer, IBM J. Res. Dev., 1961; quantified by Bérut et al., Nature, 2012.)

The principle resolves Maxwell's demon definitively. In 1982, IBM physicist Charles Bennett showed that the demon can measure without paying an entropy cost — quantum mechanics permits measurement to be thermodynamically reversible in principle. But the demon accumulates a memory of every molecule it has observed, and at the end of each cycle, that memory must be erased to return the system to its initial state. It is the erasure of the demon's notes, not its measurement, that exacts the thermodynamic toll — and that toll precisely cancels the work the demon appeared to extract from the gas. The second law survives.

The principle sat unverified for half a century because the energy involved — femtojoules and zeptojoules — was simply too small to measure against the thermal noise of any real device. In 2012, Antoine Bérut, Eric Lutz, and colleagues at the École Normale Supérieure in Lyon trapped a single silica bead two micrometres wide in a laser beam shaped into a double-well potential — a physical analogue of a 1-bit memory. They erased the bit (drove the bead from an unknown well into a known one) using carefully controlled pressure ramps and measured the heat released using fluctuation theorems from stochastic thermodynamics. The result, published in Nature: in the limit of slow erasure, the dissipated heat converged to kBT ln 2, within experimental error. Landauer's 1961 conjecture was confirmed on its first direct experimental test.

Section 03

The Connections

Information is physical. Landauer's most famous slogan — "information is physical" — sounds like philosophy but is literally true: a bit is not an abstract symbol, it is a physical configuration of matter, and manipulating it obeys the same laws as operating a steam engine. This unification dissolved a century-old confusion in the foundations of statistical mechanics. Shannon's information entropy (the measure of missing information in a message) and Boltzmann's thermodynamic entropy (the measure of disorder in a physical system) are not just mathematically analogous — they are the same quantity, measured in the same units, exchangeable by the same formula. The demon was the proof that the two had always been one.

Reversible computing: the road around the limit. Bennett's 1973 paper in the IBM Journal showed that any computation can in principle be done reversibly — without ever erasing a bit — by carrying a "history tape" of every intermediate step. A reversible computer never collapses two states into one, so it pays no Landauer toll. The catch: you must store every intermediate result forever, or run the computation backwards (un-computing) to clean up. Reversible architectures and adiabatic logic circuits have been studied since the 1980s. None has reached commercial scale, because the engineering overhead of storing history typically costs more energy than the Landauer savings at current chip temperatures. But as transistors approach atomic scales, the Landauer bound is becoming the binding constraint rather than a curiosity.

Black holes and the holographic bound. The Landauer principle connects to the most extreme information-storage limit in physics: the Bekenstein bound, which states that the maximum entropy (and therefore information) that can be contained in any physical region is proportional to its surface area, not its volume. A black hole's event horizon is the densest possible information storage — and Hawking radiation, the slow leak of energy from a black hole, can be understood as the universe enforcing the Landauer cost of the black hole's gradual erasure of the quantum information it absorbed. Jacob Bekenstein and Stephen Hawking arrived at their entropy formulae from general relativity; Landauer's principle arrived from logic gates at IBM. They point at the same place.

The AI energy crisis has a theoretical floor. Training a large language model like GPT-4 consumed an estimated 1,300 MWh — enough to power roughly 1,450 US homes for a month. A single ChatGPT query dissipates around 2.9 Wh, about ten times a Google search. Against the Landauer limit, every one of those operations is performing billions of bit erasures at roughly one billion times the thermodynamic minimum cost. The gap is real engineering headroom, but it is also a hard physical boundary: kBT ln 2 is where the road ends regardless of how clever the architecture. The brain, by contrast, runs on roughly 20 watts total — and operates synaptic switching at energy scales estimated to be within two to three orders of magnitude of the Landauer limit, suggesting biology has already found most of the path to the floor that silicon chips have barely started down.

The 2012 Nature experiment

Bennett's reversible computing paper (1973)

Charles H. Bennett, "Logical Reversibility of Computation," IBM Journal of Research and Development, Vol. 17, pp. 525–532. Shows any Turing machine can be simulated reversibly, in principle avoiding the Landauer cost entirely — the theoretical blueprint for a future beyond silicon.
