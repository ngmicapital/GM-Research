The Cobra Effect: How Every Bounty Program Makes Its Problem Worse

In 1902, French colonial authorities in Hanoi offered one cent per rat tail to control the city’s rodent population. Within weeks, rat catchers were severing tails and releasing the rats back into the sewers to breed. Rat farms appeared on the city outskirts. The government cancelled the programme; the infestation got worse. This pattern has a name — and the story the name is borrowed from may never have happened.

In 1902, French colonial authorities paid 1 cent per rat tail in Hanoi to control an infestation; entrepreneurs promptly started breeding rats for their tails, making the problem worse. The phenomenon has a name — the Cobra Effect — borrowed from a British colonial bounty on Indian cobras that is almost certainly apocryphal, and a mechanism that explains why incentive-based solutions keep failing in predictable ways across centuries and continents.

TL;DR

In 1902, French colonial authorities launched a rat eradication programme in Hanoi by paying a bounty of one cent for each rat tail presented as proof of a kill. Within weeks, entrepreneurs had worked out that it was more efficient to trap rats, sever their tails, and release the living rats back into the sewers than to kill them outright. Rat farms — breeding operations designed to produce an unlimited supply of tails — began appearing on the city outskirts. The programme was cancelled; the rat population was worse than before. This exact failure pattern recurs so reliably across history that it has a name: the Cobra Effect. The name comes from a story about a British colonial cobra bounty in India that almost certainly never happened. The mechanism that makes the pattern so universal — and so reliable — was not formally described until 1975, when an economist named Charles Goodhart wrote down a deceptively simple principle.

Section 01

The Story

Hanoi in 1902 was a colonial showpiece project. Governor-General Paul Doumer had been transforming the city since 1897 — wide boulevards, European-style public buildings, and, most ambitiously, a modern underground sewer system. By the time Doumer left Vietnam in 1902, over 19 kilometres of tunnels had been excavated beneath the French Quarter, built by Vietnamese and Chinese labourers under French engineering supervision. It was a genuine feat of infrastructure. It also created a perfect habitat for rats.

The sewer system was massive, interconnected, and warm. Rats colonised it immediately and in enormous numbers. They emerged at night into the French Quarter, breaching the boundary between the European and Vietnamese parts of the city that the colonial administration had worked hard to maintain. Rats were a pest-control problem, but they were also a political embarrassment: the gleaming modern city had a visible rodent infestation that undermined the civilising narrative the French had built around their colonial project.

In April 1902, Hanoi’s new municipal government launched a rat eradication programme. Vietnamese workers — called “ratters” in the colonial records — were hired to enter the sewer system and kill rats. The original programme paid by the dead rat: workers presented whole carcasses and were paid accordingly. This was logistically cumbersome. The city administration decided to simplify the proof-of-kill: instead of presenting entire rat bodies, which had to be collected, counted, stored, and eventually disposed of, workers needed only to produce the severed tail.

French health inspectors began discovering something strange: live rats, running through the sewers, with their tails cut off. Someone was catching rats, docking their tails, and releasing them back to breed.

The rat tail system seemed efficient. It was easy to verify — tails were small, countable, and unambiguous. It decentralised the work: anyone could catch and kill a rat, not just the official ratters. The city began paying out on hundreds, then thousands of tails per day. The numbers looked impressive. The rat problem, on the ground, did not improve.

French health inspectors sent into the sewers to assess the programme made a disturbing discovery. They found live rats, running through the tunnels, with their tails missing. Someone was not killing the rats but trapping them, severing their tails as proof of “kills”, and releasing the animals back into the sewers alive — where they would continue to breed and provide an indefinitely renewable source of future tails. Further investigation revealed rat farms had appeared on the outskirts of Hanoi: operations breeding rats specifically for their tails, with no intention of killing them. The bounty had created a rat-farming industry.

The colonial administration cancelled the programme in the summer of 1902. The effect was predictable: the rat farmers, now holding large breeding populations of animals with no commercial value, released them. Hanoi’s rat population spiked. The city was left with a worse infestation than it had started with, a depleted budget, and a rat-farming sector where none had existed before. The French quietly abandoned any further bounty schemes and pivoted to direct pest control with poison.

What the Records Show

The Hanoi rat programme is well-documented in colonial administrative records and has been studied by historians including Michael Vann, whose 2003 paper “Of Rats, Rice, and Race” in French Colonial History provides the primary archival analysis. Vann notes that the rat-tail scheme was reported in French colonial newspapers at the time, including accounts of the tailless rats in the sewers. The Atlas Obscura and Wikipedia summaries all draw on this and related historiography. This is not a parable or an anecdote: it is a documented administrative failure with a paper trail.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The Hanoi rat programme failed for a structural reason that has nothing to do with colonial incompetence, Vietnamese cunning, or the specific properties of rat tails. It failed because of a fundamental mismatch between what the programme measured and what it actually wanted to achieve.

The real goal was to reduce the rat population. The proxy chosen to measure progress toward that goal was “number of rat tails presented.” This proxy was reasonable: in normal conditions, rat tails and dead rats are perfectly correlated — every tail implies a dead rat implies a reduction in population. The proxy seemed equivalent to the goal. It was not. Once people were optimising specifically for tails rather than for rat deaths, the correlation collapsed. The programme was now measuring something different from what it wanted, without realising it. The metric went up; the actual population remained flat or grew.

This is the structural failure that the British economist Charles Goodhart first formalised in 1975 in a paper about monetary policy. Goodhart was observing that the Bank of England was setting targets for monetary aggregates (measures of money supply) as a way of controlling inflation. His insight: once an aggregate becomes a target, the behaviour of the economy shifts specifically toward optimising that aggregate — and as a result it ceases to be a reliable indicator of the underlying economic conditions it was chosen to measure. In the original context it was about money supply statistics. The principle turned out to be universal.

Goodhart’s Law: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” It sounds abstract. The rat farms make it concrete.

The underlying dynamic is always the same. You have a goal (G) that is hard to measure directly. You select a proxy (P) that correlates with G under normal conditions. You set P as a target and attach rewards or penalties to it. People now optimise for P. If there is any way to improve P without improving G — and there almost always is, once people are actively looking — they will find it. The proxy diverges from the goal. The metric looks good; the world does not change accordingly.

In the rat case: goal = fewer rats; proxy = tails; divergence method = tail farming. In the Soviet nail case: goal = adequate nail supply; proxy = number of nails produced; divergence = make millions of tiny, useless nails. When the quota was switched to weight, the divergence became: make a small number of enormous, useless anchor-sized spikes. The metric changed; the structural failure did not. The factories simply found a new way to optimise for the measure rather than the goal.

Why “Just Use a Better Metric” Doesn’t Work

The intuitive response to Goodhart’s Law is to design a better proxy — one that is harder to game. The Soviet planners tried this: switching from count to weight was designed to close the loophole. It created a different loophole. This pattern repeats because there is no proxy that is perfectly equivalent to the goal: if there were, it would be the goal, not a proxy. Every proxy has at least one dimension along which it can diverge. The deeper implication is that bounty-and-measurement systems are structurally vulnerable regardless of the specific metric chosen — the vulnerability is in the architecture, not the details.

The further complication is information asymmetry. The people designing the bounty programme are not the same people executing it. The people executing it know the local conditions, the operational details, and — crucially — the gaps between the metric and the goal. The designers, watching the metric from a distance, see the numbers improving and assume the goal is being achieved. By the time the divergence becomes visible at the design level, the local actors have often been optimising against the measure for months or years. The metric is a one-way mirror: those inside see everything; those outside see only the reflection.

Section 03

The Connections

The cobra story may never have happened. The Cobra Effect takes its name from a colonial Indian anecdote: British authorities in Delhi, concerned about the density of venomous cobras in the city, offered a bounty for dead snakes. Enterprising locals began breeding cobras to collect the reward. When the British discovered this and cancelled the bounty, the breeders released their now-worthless snakes, increasing the cobra population. The story is clean, instructive, and almost certainly fiction.

The term was coined in 2001 by German economist Horst Siebert in his book Der Kobra-Effekt, where he presented the cobra breeding anecdote as a historical fact. Siebert did not provide a primary source. Subsequent attempts to trace the story to documentary evidence have found nothing: no contemporary newspaper accounts of cobra breeding operations in British India, no prosecutorial records, no government inquiries. In 1887, the Bombay Natural History Society investigated precisely these rumours and concluded that breeding cobras in captivity was “highly improbable” — cobras had never, to their knowledge, bred successfully in confinement. British bounty records from Bengal showed the programme was described in 1874 as an “almost complete failure,” with most districts reporting zero cobras caught — which contradicts a scenario in which cobra farming had become widespread enough to produce excess supply.

A Story That Explains Itself

There is an obvious irony in naming a theory about perverse incentives after a story that spread through the internet and academic writing because it was so useful as an illustration — without anyone checking whether it was true. The story was optimised for virality: it was punchy, counterintuitive, and had a satisfying colonial-era setting that made it easy to present as fact. The proxy for “is this true” was “does this illustrate the concept well?” The two turned out not to be the same thing. A myth about Goodhart’s Law propagated by Goodhart’s Law.

The pattern has better-documented modern examples than the colonial legend. The UN’s Clean Development Mechanism, established under the Kyoto Protocol, offered carbon credits to companies in developing countries for destroying HFC-23, a potent greenhouse gas produced as a byproduct of refrigerant manufacturing. The credits were worth far more than the refrigerant itself. By 2011, it had emerged that refrigerant manufacturers in China and India were deliberately producing more HFC-23 — and thus more of the refrigerant it was a byproduct of — in order to destroy it and claim the credits. The total damage to the climate from the excess production exceeded the benefit of the destruction. The European Union suspended the credits in 2013 after independent audits confirmed the manipulation.

Mexico City’s Hoy No Circula. In 1989, Mexico City implemented a vehicle restriction policy: cars could not be driven on certain weekdays based on their licence plate’s last digit, designed to reduce traffic and air pollution. The measure worked briefly. Within a year, wealthier residents had bought second cars with different licence plate numbers — often older, less fuel-efficient vehicles specifically purchased to dodge the restriction. The city’s vehicle fleet grew; air quality did not improve. The metric being targeted (specific plate numbers on specific days) was not equivalent to the goal (fewer total vehicle kilometres driven).

Hospital readmission penalties. In 2012, the US Affordable Care Act began penalising hospitals financially for excessive patient readmission rates within 30 days of discharge. Readmission rates were a reasonable proxy for care quality: patients who returned within a month after discharge suggested something had gone wrong. Hospitals immediately began optimising the metric. Some improved care. Others found that patients brought to the emergency department could be treated in the observation ward rather than formally “admitted,” so that if they returned within 30 days it did not count as a “readmission.” Observation ward stays tripled. The metric improved; patient outcomes were more complicated. A 2019 study in JAMA Cardiology found an association between the penalties and increased mortality among heart failure patients — suggesting that some hospitals may have been discharging high-risk patients earlier to avoid being blamed for readmissions.

Why the pattern persists. Each of these cases involved people who understood, intellectually, that incentive design is difficult. The UN negotiators who designed HFC-23 credits were sophisticated environmental economists. The Mexican traffic planners were aware that regulation-evasion was possible. The ACA designers knew hospital gaming was a risk. None of it helped, because Goodhart’s Law is not a problem of naivety. It is a problem of complexity: real systems are always more multidimensional than any single metric can capture, and any single metric, once targeted, will be optimised against from every dimension the designers failed to anticipate.

Further Down the Hole

🐍
Horst Siebert — Der Kobra-Effekt (2001, English: The Cobra Effect, 2003)
Siebert was a German economist and president of the Kiel Institute for the World Economy. His book used the cobra anecdote to argue against heavy-handed government regulation and poorly designed incentives in economic policy. The book popularised the term; the anecdote he used as the founding example almost certainly predates him and cannot be traced to a verified primary source. The concept he described, however, is real and important regardless of whether his chosen illustration is.
📊
Goodhart’s Law and Campbell’s Law
Charles Goodhart stated his principle in a 1975 Bank of England working paper on monetary policy. The sociologist Donald T. Campbell arrived at essentially the same insight independently in 1976: “The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor.” Both laws describe the same phenomenon from economics and social science respectively. Together they constitute one of the most robust empirical observations in policy design.
🗺️
Michael Vann — “Of Rats, Rice, and Race” (2003)
Historian Michael Vann of California State University Sacramento published this paper in French Colonial History, providing the most thorough scholarly treatment of the 1902 Hanoi rat programme from colonial archival sources. Vann’s analysis situates the rat massacre in the context of French colonial anxieties about racial separation, public health, and the management of Vietnamese and Chinese labour populations. The pest-control failure was also, in his reading, a failure of the colonial project’s logic more broadly: the belief that sufficiently systematic management could reshape an urban environment in the colonisers’ image.
🧩
Mechanism Design: Solving the Problem from First Principles
The branch of economics known as mechanism design (or “reverse game theory”) tries to work backwards from the desired outcome to design incentive structures that produce it. Leonid Hurwicz, Eric Maskin, and Roger Myerson won the 2007 Nobel Prize in Economics for foundational work in this field. The core insight is that rather than assuming people will behave as a planner wishes, a good mechanism should make the desired behaviour the rational choice regardless of individual motivations. When it works, it sidesteps Goodhart’s Law; when it fails to anticipate how actors will respond, it reproduces the same failure mode in a more sophisticated-looking wrapper.
🧪
The UN HFC-23 Carbon Credit Scandal
Between 2001 and 2013, the Clean Development Mechanism issued credits for destroying HFC-23, a greenhouse gas 14,800 times more potent than CO2 by weight. Independent auditors found that companies were manufacturing the gas specifically to destroy it for credits — a net increase in total emissions. The European Union suspended acceptance of HFC-23 credits in 2013; the CDM registry closed them entirely in 2015. It is among the most clearly documented modern cobra effects: a market mechanism designed to reduce greenhouse gases that briefly became a mechanism for producing them.
