She Found Sea Monsters Nobody Believed Could Exist. They Took the Credit.

Mary Anning of Lyme Regis excavated the first ichthyosaur, plesiosaur, and pterosaur found in England — discoveries that proved extinction was real and cracked open deep geological time. She was self-taught, working-class, and never permitted to attend the Geological Society that displayed her specimens. Women weren't admitted until 1904, fifty-seven years after her death.

Mary Anning discovered the first ichthyosaur at age 12, then the first plesiosaur and pterosaur — three finds that demolished the idea that species couldn't go extinct. The Geological Society of London held a special meeting to authenticate her plesiosaur without inviting her, bought her fossils without crediting her, and didn't admit women as members until 57 years after she died.

Section 01

The Story

In the autumn of 1811, a boy named Joseph Anning spotted a large skull protruding from the Blue Lias cliffs near Lyme Regis on the Dorset coast. He told his sister Mary. She was eleven years old, their father had died the year before, and the family was surviving by selling small fossils — ammonites and belemnites — to tourists visiting the seaside town. The skull was too large and too deeply embedded for Joseph to excavate alone. Mary took it on.

Over the next year, working in the gaps between tides, after storms when fresh rock had fallen, she extracted a five-metre skeleton from the cliff face using a hammer and chisel. It had the elongated snout of a crocodile, enormous eye sockets, and paddle-like limbs. It was unlike anything living. A local lord purchased it for £23. It eventually reached London, where the physician Everard Home published a series of papers on the creature in the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society. He called it a new genus of crocodile. He did not mention Mary Anning.

The creature was eventually recognized as an entirely new order of reptile — Ichthyosauria, the fish-lizards — predating dinosaur terminology by three decades. By the 1820s it was understood that the animal had been extinct for tens of millions of years, longer than any human chronology had imagined the Earth to have existed. Mary was twelve when she completed the excavation. She had found the first evidence that the world had once been populated by creatures so alien they had left no living descendants.

She did it again in December 1823. Another specimen, stranger than the first: a small skull on an absurdly long neck, with broad paddle limbs and a barrel body. It was so anatomically bizarre that when she described it to the gentlemen geologists who circulated through Lyme Regis, the great French comparative anatomist Georges Cuvier declared it likely a fake — a composite assembled from multiple animals. The Geological Society of London convened a special meeting to settle the question. Mary was not invited. Cuvier, having reviewed detailed illustrations of the skeleton, reversed his position. The specimen — a plesiosaur, a creature unknown to science — was authenticated. The Society published the finding. Mary Anning's name appeared as the vendor, not the discoverer.

She excavated, prepared, identified, and described the specimens. The Geological Society of London displayed them, debated them, and published papers about them — and did not admit women as members until fifty-seven years after she died.

In December 1828 she found a pterosaur — the first discovered outside Germany — embedded in the same Jurassic cliffs. Then a fossil fish in 1829 that appeared to represent a transitional form between sharks and rays. She pioneered the interpretation of coprolites, the spiral-valved fossilized feces that appear throughout the Blue Lias, identifying them as the gut contents of ancient marine reptiles and opening an entirely new analytical window on prehistoric ecology. She corresponded with the leading geologists of the age — William Buckland, Charles Lyell, William Conybeare, Gideon Mantell — advising them on the anatomy of specimens and the stratigraphy of the cliffs. Richard Owen, who would coin the word Dinosauria in 1842, visited Lyme Regis in 1839 and spent time in the field with her. His 1840 paper describing a plesiosaur she had found credited the wealthy gentleman who purchased it.

When Mary Anning died in March 1847 — breast cancer, age forty-seven, still dependent on a small pension arranged by her geological acquaintances because her income from fossil sales was insufficient — the president of the Geological Society of London mentioned her in his annual address. It was the first time a woman had been so acknowledged. She was not a member. She could not be. The rule against female membership would stand for another fifty-seven years.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The Blue Lias is not just a geological formation. It is a machine for making fossils available, one storm at a time. Understanding why Lyme Regis produced Mary Anning requires understanding why that particular stretch of English coastline is one of the most productive Jurassic fossil sites on Earth — and why collecting there has always required a specific combination of geological knowledge, physical nerve, and precise timing.

The Blue Lias consists of alternating layers of blue-grey limestone and darker shale, deposited on a shallow seafloor during the late Triassic and early Jurassic periods, roughly 200 million years ago. At that time, what is now Dorset lay at a latitude roughly equivalent to present-day southern Spain, submerged beneath a warm shallow sea. Marine reptiles — ichthyosaurs, plesiosaurs, the creatures that would later be called sea dragons — lived and died there, sinking into fine seafloor sediment, where gradual mineralization preserved their bones over geological time. The seafloor became rock. Tectonic forces tilted the strata. Ice ages and sea-level changes brought the sea back to cut new coastlines. The cliffs at Lyme Regis expose this Jurassic seafloor almost vertically, a cross-section of 200 million years pressed into a few hundred metres of cliff face.

The Fossil Hunter's Window

The best time to search the Blue Lias is the first calm morning after a winter storm. Rain saturates the shale layers between the limestone beds; the clay swells, becomes unstable, and fails — rockslides that expose fresh limestone surfaces and the fossils buried within them. The same process that reveals fossils also destroys them: a skeleton left in place through another winter will be battered to fragments by wave action. Mary Anning worked this window repeatedly, often in dangerous conditions. In October 1833 a landslide at her usual collecting ground killed her dog Tray. She was standing nearby.

The shale layers are the key. Limestone is harder and more resistant; shale is soft, clay-rich, and crumbles readily in wet weather. When waves and rain erode a cliff section, the shale layers fail first, undercutting the limestone above and triggering collapse. Each collapse exposes new limestone surfaces — and whatever fossils they contain. The process is continuous and seasonal. The Jurassic Coast, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site stretching 150 kilometres from Exmouth to Swanage, produces new fossil discoveries every year for exactly this reason: erosion is not a problem to manage but an engine of discovery. Stop the erosion and you stop the exposure.

Mary Anning understood this intuitively and then analytically. She knew which storms opened which cliff sections. She knew how quickly a good specimen would be destroyed by the next tide. She knew that the spiral-valved internal structure she could see in coprolites indicated gut anatomy that matched the marine reptiles — a conclusion she reached before any professional zoologist had thought to look. Charles Lyell, whose Principles of Geology (1830–33) established the doctrine of uniformitarianism — that geological change operates by the same slow processes observable today — corresponded with her about precisely this coastline. She told him what she observed; he built theory from it. His name is on the doctrine. Hers is not.

Section 03

The Connections

The nursery rhyme almost certainly has nothing to do with her. The story that "She sells seashells by the seashore" was written in tribute to Mary Anning is widely repeated and almost certainly false. The folklorist Stephen Winick of the Library of Congress traced the tongue twister back to a print appearance in December 1871, thirty-seven years before Terry Sullivan's 1908 music hall song that most people know. More fundamentally, Mary Anning did not sell seashells — she sold fossils, from a shop on Lyme Regis's Broad Street, not from the beach. The myth persists because it fills a genuine cultural need: a way of acknowledging that a pioneering woman scientist was overlooked. But fabricating a connection that didn't exist doesn't honour her work; it replaces her actual story — which is stranger and more consequential — with a tidy fiction.

Her discoveries were evidence in the most contested scientific argument of the nineteenth century. When Mary Anning pulled an ichthyosaur from the Blue Lias in 1812, the dominant scientific and theological view held that species did not go extinct — that God's creation was complete and immutable. Thomas Jefferson had sent Lewis and Clark partly to find living mammoths, operating on exactly this assumption. Georges Cuvier had argued for extinction based on elephant fossil comparisons, but his work was contested. The ichthyosaurs and plesiosaurs Anning found were not marginal cases: they were creatures of such bizarre anatomy — the plesiosaur's small skull on a neck comprising almost half its body length — that no one could argue they still existed somewhere undiscovered. They were gone. The world had contained living things that no longer existed. This was a direct challenge to the prevailing chronology of creation, and it could not be dismissed because the specimens were sitting in London's museums for anyone to examine.

"Duria Antiquior" was the first image of deep time — and it was painted to keep her solvent. In 1830, the geologist Henry De la Beche — a childhood friend who remained one of Anning's genuine advocates — commissioned an artist named Georg Scharf to paint a reconstruction of the prehistoric Dorset sea based on the fossils she had found: ichthyosaurs hunting, plesiosaurs swimming, pterosaurs in the air above. It was the first scientifically grounded reconstruction of a prehistoric scene ever made. De la Beche had prints made and sold them to geologists across Britain and Europe, donating the proceeds to Anning to supplement her income. The painting — Duria Antiquior, meaning "a more ancient Dorset" — established the entire tradition of palaeoart, the genre of scientifically informed prehistoric illustration that runs from Victorian museum prints to modern documentary CGI. Every image you have ever seen of Jurassic sea life is a descendant of that painting, created because a woman who discovered the fossil evidence couldn't make ends meet from selling what she dug up.

Richard Owen named the dinosaurs using science she built — and spent his career not crediting her. Owen coined Dinosauria in 1842 as a formal taxonomic category for the great extinct terrestrial reptiles. His anatomical framework for understanding these creatures rested on comparative work with the marine reptiles Anning had uncovered; her plesiosaurs and ichthyosaurs established what a Mesozoic reptile's skeleton could look like, creating the conceptual vocabulary Owen used. He visited Lyme Regis in 1839, went fossil hunting with her guidance, and came away with specimens he later described in professional papers — crediting the collectors who purchased them. The pattern was so consistent and so unremarkable to contemporaries that it required no explanation. That was simply how natural history worked: gentlemen described, women and working people dug.

Further Down the Hole

🦕
The Jurassic Coast Today
The 150-kilometre stretch of Dorset and Devon coastline where Anning worked is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Lyme Regis runs guided fossil walks after winter storms — the same erosion cycle that drove her collecting still operates. The Lyme Regis Museum, built on the site of her childhood home, has a dedicated Anning gallery. New ichthyosaur specimens are still found on the beach most winters.
🐬
Ichthyosaur Convergent Evolution
Ichthyosaurs were reptiles that evolved fish-like and dolphin-like body forms independently — a classic case of convergent evolution. Their fossils show the same streamlined torso, crescent tail, and dorsal fin seen in modern dolphins and tuna, despite no shared ancestry. Pregnant ichthyosaur specimens show they gave birth live, like whales — another convergence. The Natural History Museum's collection includes Anning specimens that revealed this biology first.
🗣️
The Nursery Rhyme Myth
Stephen Winick's 2017 analysis for the Library of Congress Folklife Center is the definitive takedown of the "she sells seashells" claim — tracing the earliest print sources and explaining why the connection is folklore invented in the twentieth century. Worth reading not just for Anning but as a case study in how satisfying myths attach themselves to real historical figures who lack adequate documentation of their own.
🎨
Duria Antiquior — The First Palaeoart
The Natural History Museum holds the original Georg Scharf watercolour painted for De la Beche in 1830. High-resolution versions are available in the NHM's digital collection. Its direct descendants include the Crystal Palace prehistoric sculptures (1854), Zdeněk Burian's twentieth-century paintings, and every BBC/Netflix prehistoric documentary. Tracing the visual genealogy from Duria Antiquior to modern CGI is a full art-history rabbit hole in itself.
🏛️
The Geological Society's Women Problem
The Geological Society of London admitted its first female fellows in 1904, after a legal challenge forced the issue. Even then, some fellows resigned in protest. The Society has produced a detailed online exhibition — "Mary Anning and the Geological Society" — documenting the exclusion mechanism: she could correspond with members, sell to members, be thanked in footnotes, and be eulogized posthumously, but never join. An institutional history of how science constructed and enforced its gentlemen's club in the nineteenth century.
