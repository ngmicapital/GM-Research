01 Key Ideas & Insights
Essay · Reid Hoffman · Theory of the Game · April 2026

Faith in the Possible: Silicon Valley as a Religious Movement (and the Eight Denominations Inside It)

Reid Hoffman — co-founder of LinkedIn, partner at Greylock, board member at Microsoft and OpenAI — launched a Substack this month called Theory of the Game. Its first major essay, "Faith in the Possible," argues something that anyone who has spent time in San Francisco intuits but rarely sees stated outright: Silicon Valley is not primarily an industry. It is a religious movement. Its central creed is that scalable technology, deployed through companies, is the primary engine of human flourishing — more impactful than government, churches, or NGOs.

Hoffman's framing is deliberately theological. Humans, he argues, are homo techne — "tool-users, toolmakers, tool-evolved" — and creation is not a Silicon Valley invention but the fundamental human impulse expressed through better instruments. The printing press didn't replace meaning; it restructured authority and made the Enlightenment possible. AI, in his telling, sits in the same lineage. Technology is "the Archimedean lever of human flourishing," responsible for roughly a third of US economic growth over the past decade.

The most useful part of the essay is not the defense of techno-optimism but the taxonomy. Hoffman identifies eight distinct denominations inside the broader faith — each sharing the central creed but disagreeing sharply on implementation:

  • Purists: Code alone matters. Society is downstream of better systems.
  • Humanists: Technology must be embedded in social context to be meaningful.
  • Missionaries: Technology should elevate everyone — distribution is a moral obligation.
  • Venture Capitalists: Returns justify pulling the lever; capital allocation is the proof.
  • Narcissists: Personal aggrandizement drives the building. The mission is the founder.
  • Accelerationists: Pull the lever now, hard, without hesitation.
  • Ethicists: Pull carefully — or sometimes not at all.
  • Determinists: Technology self-directs. Our role is to hold on.

The diagnostic value is immediate: when reading any AI debate, ask which denomination each participant is preaching from. Most "disagreements" between accelerationists and ethicists are not about facts. They are about which rituals the church should observe — and which heresies should be cast out.

The piece is most worth reading for the comments section, where critics push back on the founders-as-clergy framing and offer alternative lenses on whether startups are genuinely engines of progress or merely the most efficient capital-aggregation mechanism currently available. Hoffman has built a publication that rewards reading the bottom-half of the page as much as the top.

Podcast · Greg Brockman × Shane Parrish · The Knowledge Project · 22 April 2026

"We Are in the Phase Where AI Develops AI": Greg Brockman's Parabolic Forecast

Greg Brockman — OpenAI co-founder and President — sat down with Shane Parrish for two hours on The Knowledge Project in what is the most candid public conversation he has given since the November 2023 board crisis. The 72-hour Sam Altman firing and reinstatement gets a first-person retelling. But the more strategically important admission is buried in the middle of the conversation, almost as an aside.

Asked how much of OpenAI's own code is now being written by AI, Brockman didn't give a percentage. He said: "It's hard to know what percent is not." The qualitative inversion is the point. Inside the company building the frontier model, the assumption has flipped from "AI helps engineers write code" to "engineers oversee code that AI writes." This is not a future scenario. It is the operating reality of the lab itself.

Brockman's framing of where this leads:

"I would say we are in this phase where you apply AI to its own development process and it's going to go faster and faster." — Greg Brockman, The Knowledge Project, April 2026

This is the recursive self-improvement loop AI safety researchers have theorized about for two decades, described in plain language by the President of the lab where it is happening. The asymmetric implication: if the development cycle of frontier AI is itself accelerated by frontier AI, the gap between the leader and everyone else compounds in ways that linear forecasting badly underprices.

Two other claims worth tracking. First, Brockman expects specialized data centers — "a huge data center in North Dakota and it's just solving cancer" — to emerge as the second wave of compute deployment, after the general-purpose buildout currently underway. Second, his framing of the geopolitical race is deliberately understated: he does not predict a US victory; he predicts that the consequences of falling behind in AGI are categorically larger than the consequences of falling behind in any prior technology, and acts accordingly. The interview is the closest thing to an unfiltered briefing on OpenAI's worldview that has been recorded this year.

Essay · Kevin Kelly · KK Substack · April 2026

The Heresy Test: How to Stress-Test Your Own Orthodoxies

Kevin Kelly — founding editor of Wired, author of The Inevitable, and one of the more consistently calibrated thinkers about technology over the past forty years — published an essay this month called "Some Contemporary Heresies." His definition is what makes the piece useful as a thinking tool, not just a list:

"A heresy is something you believe that the people you most admire and respect don't believe and reject out of hand." — Kevin Kelly, "Some Contemporary Heresies," April 2026

Kelly catalogues 84 heresies — collected from people he respects, not asserted as his own — that pass the threshold of "plausible, not insane." The list includes positions like: war can be eliminated entirely; death is a disease that can be cured; modern humans will diverge into separate non-interbreeding species; representatives should be selected randomly rather than elected; consciousness is a cognitive artifact rather than a fundamental feature of reality; intelligence is highly heritable and predictive across life domains. Some are uncomfortable. That is the point.

The strategic value is the framework, not the catalogue. The "heresy test" is a personal stress-test of intellectual humility: What do I currently believe that the people I most respect would reject out of hand? If the answer is nothing, the more probable explanation is that you have outsourced your worldview to consensus rather than that consensus happens to be correct on every contested question.

The corollary test is harder: identify three things the people you most respect believe that you suspect may turn out to be wrong. The discomfort of that exercise is the diagnostic — orthodoxies are invisible from inside the church. A practitioner who can name the heresies they hold is calibrated. A practitioner who insists they hold none is, almost by definition, captured.

Kelly's framing pairs naturally with second-order thinking: identifying a heresy is the easy part; tracing its consequences if it turned out to be true is where the actual analytical work lives. Most people stop at "that's controversial." The useful step is "if true, what falls?"

02 Strategy & Practice
Behavior Design · Health Psychology · April 2026

Willpower Is a Battery, Not a Renewable Resource: The 37% Routine Effect

A new study published in Health Psychology (Hagerman et al., advance online publication, 2026) followed 112 adults through a 12-week behavioral weight-loss program. Researchers tracked two variables: how consistent participants' daily caloric intake was, and how often they repeated the same meals. The finding that travelled: participants who ate repeat meals lost 37% more weight than those who ate variety, holding caloric targets constant. The mechanism is not metabolic. It is decisional.

Every food choice is a willpower withdrawal. Across a day, the cumulative cost of small choices — what to make for breakfast, what to grab between meetings, what to order for dinner — drains the budget that has to be available at 8pm when the trigger food appears. People who eat the same 3–4 meals on rotation aren't being more disciplined. They have removed the decision from the equation entirely. The discipline lives in the system; the day requires almost none.

The principle generalizes far beyond food. Anywhere a recurring decision is being made fresh each time, willpower is being spent unnecessarily. The question for any practitioner is not "how do I have more discipline?" — it is "which of my recurring decisions can be moved out of willpower and into routine?"

  • Eating: Pick 3–4 default meals. Rotate. Reserve "real cooking" for weekends or social occasions.
  • Wardrobe: The Steve Jobs / Obama uniform principle isn't about minimalism; it's about preserving the day's first decision budget for higher-value choices.
  • Calendar: Block deep-work hours at the same time daily. Schedule once, not 200 times a year.
  • Meetings: Default agenda template. Default time-of-day. Default attendees. Each defaulted parameter is a withdrawal you don't make.
  • Communication: Standard email response templates for the 5 most common request types. Reply once, paste forever.

The mental model: treat willpower as a fixed daily budget that depletes whether or not it produces useful output. The goal is not to spend it more wisely on small things — it is to refuse to spend it on small things at all, so the budget is intact when a high-stakes decision actually arrives.

Cognitive Science · Beaudoin · Sleep Research

The Paradox of Effort: Why Trying to Sleep Is the Reason You Can't

One of the more useful psychological frameworks of the past decade has surfaced in mainstream attention this week through the work of Dr. Luc Beaudoin, a cognitive scientist at Simon Fraser University. His research, published as the Serial Diverse Imagining Task (and popularized as "cognitive shuffling"), starts from a simple observation: the more effort you apply to falling asleep, the further sleep retreats. Sleep is one of a class of outcomes that effort actively prevents.

The technique itself is mechanical: pick a neutral word, then for each letter, generate a different concrete image (B → bicycle, balloon, bench…). The brain interprets this scattered, semantically-disconnected image stream as a signature of pre-sleep cognition rather than waking thought. Cortical arousal drops. In a 2016 controlled study (n=150), Beaudoin's group found cognitive shuffling produced sleep onset comparable to written worry-dumping — and participants rated it more usable.

The strategic generalization is the part worth holding onto. There is an entire category of high-value outcomes that share sleep's structure: the harder you try, the worse the outcome.

  • Creativity: The breakthrough idea arrives in the shower, on the walk, in the moment after you stop trying. Effort produces variations on what you already had.
  • Authentic conversation: Trying to be charismatic produces the opposite. The release of effort is the charisma.
  • Deep focus: Forcing concentration narrows attention but kills synthesis. The "soft focus" state is where pattern-recognition lives.
  • Recall: The name on the tip of your tongue arrives the moment you stop reaching for it.
  • Falling in love, healing, grief processing: All paradoxical. All resistant to direct effort. All responsive to creating conditions and waiting.

The practitioner question that follows: which outcomes in my work are paradoxical? Trying harder is the default response in any high-performance culture. For a meaningful subset of valuable outcomes, it is precisely the wrong response. The skill is recognizing which is which — and having the discipline to back off when effort is the obstacle.

Sleep Tool

mySleepButton — Beaudoin's Cognitive Shuffling, Productized

Dr. Luc Beaudoin's lab built mySleepButton (iOS / Android) as a guided implementation of the Serial Diverse Imagining Task. The app reads neutral, unrelated words to you, allowing your brain to do the imaging work without the friction of generating words yourself. For practitioners whose sleep is destroyed by overactive cognition (the common failure mode for high-performers), this is the most evidence-backed non-pharmaceutical intervention currently available. The technique works without the app — but the app removes the willpower cost of remembering to use it.

04 On the Horizon
AI / Recursive Loop

Recursive Self-Improvement Has Quietly Become the Operating Reality

For two decades, "recursive self-improvement" was a thought experiment in AI safety circles — the moment when an AI system becomes capable enough to improve its own architecture and training, triggering an exponential capability ramp. Greg Brockman's offhand admission this week that "it's hard to know what percent is not" of OpenAI's code is now AI-written collapses the timeline for that scenario from speculative to operational. The implications: capability gaps between frontier labs and second-tier labs will compound, not converge; the cost of training the next-generation model will fall faster than infrastructure can keep up; and the talent moat — the assumption that elite human researchers are the rate-limiting input — is weakening. Watch for which labs publish data on AI-generated code's contribution to model improvement. The ones that disclose first will be the ones whose lead is large enough to make the disclosure costless.

Enterprise / Strategy

The 11% Production Rate Is the Story of Enterprise AI in 2026

The Stanford Enterprise AI Playbook's headline finding — that 86–89% of enterprise AI pilots stall before reaching production — is the more important data point than any model benchmark this quarter. It explains the gap between AI's measured capability (rapidly improving) and AI's measured economic impact (concentrated in a small minority of firms). The bottleneck is not model quality. It is the integration, governance, and operational stack that turns a working pilot into a production system. The companies that will pull away in 2027 are not the ones running the most pilots. They are the ones with the highest pilot-to-production conversion rate. Watch for "AI productionisation" emerging as a distinct enterprise discipline — somewhere between MLOps and platform engineering — with its own job titles, tooling, and consulting layer.

Compute / Capex

Specialized Single-Problem Data Centers Are the Next Capex Wave

Brockman's prediction in the Knowledge Project interview — "a huge data center in North Dakota and it's just solving cancer" — describes a structural shift in how compute will be deployed once general-purpose AI capacity is sufficient. Today's data centers are time-shared across thousands of workloads. Tomorrow's may be vertical-integrated: one data center, one model, one objective, running continuously for years against a single problem. The economic logic is straightforward — if compute is the binding constraint on solving a high-value problem, dedicating compute end-to-end removes contention, scheduling overhead, and context-switching costs. The first credible single-problem data center announcement (likely in oncology, materials discovery, or fusion modeling) will reset how every major enterprise thinks about AI capex strategy.

Philosophy / Practice

Marcus Aurelius's 1905th Birthday and the Quiet Stoicism Resurgence

Yesterday — 26 April 2026 — was the 1905th anniversary of Marcus Aurelius's birth. Daily Stoic, the Ryan Holiday property that has done more than any other single channel to bring Stoicism to a mainstream audience over the past decade, marked the occasion with its largest-ever cohort working through Meditations. The meta-pattern is more interesting than any single product: in a year of accelerating AI capability and economic uncertainty, the philosophy that has gained the most cultural ground is the one most explicitly built around accepting what is outside your control and acting decisively on what is inside it. The market is voting with its attention. Watch for Stoicism's vocabulary — amor fati, dichotomy of control, premeditatio malorum — to migrate further into mainstream business writing as the AI transition surfaces more "things outside your control" than at any point in living memory.

Neuroscience / Longevity

The Glymphatic System Is Quietly Becoming the New Frontier in Brain Aging

Tim Ferriss's 5-Bullet Friday this week pointed to a paper in Investigative Ophthalmology & Visual Science (Francis & Abramson) on the ocular glymphatic system. The broader story is the underlying biology: the glymphatic system is the brain's waste-clearance network — a glial-based perivascular drainage system that flushes metabolic byproducts (including amyloid-beta, the protein implicated in Alzheimer's) primarily during deep sleep. Dysfunction is now linked to most neurodegenerative diseases. The clinical implications, if the model holds, are large: deep sleep architecture, sleep position, and now potentially pharmacological enhancement of glymphatic flow may turn out to be the most important modifiable risk factors for cognitive aging — more so than the standard "Alzheimer's prevention" advice currently in circulation. Watch this space; it is moving from preclinical to translational fast.

Media / Discourse

The Substack Migration of Senior Tech Voices Is Becoming Structural

Reid Hoffman launching Theory of the Game this month follows Marc Andreessen, Vinod Khosla, Patrick Collison, and a dozen other senior figures publishing directly to Substack rather than through legacy outlets or even their own firms' channels. The pattern matters: when the most senior decision-makers in technology choose direct, owned distribution over earned media, the information asymmetry shifts. Reading the right ten Substacks now provides a better picture of where capital and attention are going than reading any traditional tech publication. The corollary: the companies that succeed in the next five years will likely have founders who can write — not because writing is intrinsically valuable, but because direct distribution is the new moat for narrative control.
