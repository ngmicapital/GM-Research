Alpha — Sweep Orders

Monday · 27 April 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #5 11-min read
TL;DR

A sweep is an aggressive order that takes liquidity at multiple price levels in one shot — mechanically, the inverse of yesterday’s absorption. On the chart, the same intent shows up as a "liquidity sweep": price jabs through a known high or low, harvests the stops sitting there, and reverses. The practical edge: stops cluster in obvious places (prior swing highs/lows, round numbers, range extremes), and the real entry is not the breakout — it’s the failed close back inside the range that confirms the sweep was a hunt, not a trend.

§01
What It Is
Two meanings · one mechanic · aggression eating resting liquidity
A sweep is a single aggressive order that walks the book through multiple price levels — on the chart it surfaces as a sharp wick that pierces a key level, runs the stops sitting there, and reverses.

"Sweep" carries two meanings in modern trading and they describe the same physical event at different zoom levels. At the microstructure layer, a sweep order is a market order so large it cannot fill at the inside ask or bid alone — it consumes every resting limit order at level 1, then level 2, then level 3, until the size is filled. The order walks the book. In equities the formal name is the Intermarket Sweep Order (ISO), an SEC-defined order type that bypasses Reg NMS price-protection to take liquidity simultaneously across every venue. In crypto perps and spot, the same behaviour happens any time a participant fires a market order that exceeds top-of-book size.

At the chart layer, traders use "liquidity sweep" to describe what that aggression looks like when it is targeted: price pushes deliberately through a prior swing high or low, harvests the cluster of stop-loss orders sitting just beyond it, and reverses. The vocabulary is borrowed from ICT/SMC, but the mechanic is much older — Wyckoff called the same event a Spring (below support) or an Upthrust After Distribution / UTAD (above resistance) a century ago. Different language, identical behaviour: a false break engineered to convert resting orders into counterparty for a bigger move the other way.

Why the same word for both? Because both describe aggression eating resting liquidity — the engine of every directional move. Sweep is the verb of the order book. Whether it’s a single ISO printing through three exchanges or a coordinated stop-run on a daily high, the trader is paying spread to get filled now, against passive size that didn’t know it was about to be hit.

§02
Mechanics
Walking the book · stop pools · the full sweep cycle
The mechanic is two-step: (1) an aggressive order consumes resting liquidity at multiple levels until size is filled or a price cap is reached; (2) the resulting price displacement triggers stops, which become further fuel for the move — or get absorbed at a level, flipping direction.

Mechanically a sweep order is straightforward: a market or marketable-limit order with size greater than the resting bid (or ask) volume at the inside. Match-engine logic takes whatever sits at level 1, then level 2, then level 3 — in price-time priority — until the order is filled or hits its limit price. Each level cleared widens the spread for an instant before the book refreshes. The aggressor pays a worse average price than the inside — that’s the cost of immediacy.

Sweep order fill (price-walking): size_remaining = order_size fill_price_avg = 0 for level in book[ask_or_bid_side]:   take = min(level.size, size_remaining)   fill_price_avg += level.price × take   size_remaining −= take   if size_remaining == 0: break Slippage: slippage = fill_price_avg / inside_price − 1 → the cost of consuming resting liquidity beyond the top Liquidity sweep (chart pattern): 1. Price approaches prior swing high H (or low L) 2. Aggressive flow takes out H by Δ (often 0.05–0.5%) 3. Stops triggered → stop-market sells fire (if it was a high) or buys (if a low) 4. If absorption present at extension: reverse & reclaim level 5. Reclaim → SFP confirmation → entry trigger
Note: In equities, ISOs are flagged in the trade tape (SEC Rule 611). In crypto there is no formal sweep tag — you infer sweeps by watching: (a) a large taker print eating multiple book levels on the L2 feed, (b) a sudden CVD spike with proportionate price displacement, or (c) on the chart, a wick that pierces a level cleanly and closes back inside.

The chart-layer sweep adds a behavioural step: stops are not random — they cluster. Retail discretionary traders place stops just beyond obvious reference points: prior swing highs/lows, equal-highs / equal-lows, round numbers, session highs/lows, prior-day high/low, and pre-market range edges. Algorithmic and institutional desks know where these clusters sit and treat them as liquidity reservoirs. When a desk needs to fill a large position, sweeping the cluster gives them the counterparty they need without leaving evidence in the spot tape ahead of time.

The line between sweep and genuine break is the single most expensive distinction in tape reading. The differentiator is what happens after the level is taken: a sweep reclaims within 1–3 candles on the operating timeframe; a genuine break holds, retests, and continues. Zeiierman frames the boundary cleanly: "a stop hunt describes the intent; a liquidity sweep describes the mechanical action on the chart" — both result in the absorption of orders to fuel a move in the opposite direction. The sweep that doesn’t reverse was a real break dressed in stop-hunt clothing.

§04
In Practice
Trade the reclaim, not the wick · entry checklist · worked example
The one rule: never trade the sweep itself — trade the reclaim. Entering on the wick puts you in front of the move; entering on the close back inside the level puts you with it.
1

Map the Liquidity Pools Before the Session

Mark prior-day high/low, equal-highs/equal-lows, the weekly high/low, session highs/lows, and the round numbers within range. These are the only places stops cluster meaningfully. A sweep at a random price is not a sweep — it’s noise. Pre-marked levels are non-negotiable: do the map before the candle, not after.

2

Wait for the Reclaim — Not the Wick

Entering on the wick alone is the most common way to lose on this setup. Real reversals close back inside the level on the operating timeframe. Use the rule: wait for one full candle to close back below the swept high (or above the swept low) before entering. If price stays outside, it was a real break — stand down, don’t fade.

3

Demand Confluence at the Level

A sweep at a level with no other reason behind it is fragile. The high-conviction setups stack: prior swing high plus daily VWAP plus a CVD divergence plus high funding (crowded longs about to be unwound). At least two non-correlated reasons should agree. Without confluence, size down or skip.

4

Stop Above the Wick · Risk Asymmetry Is the Edge

Stop placement is the whole reason this setup pays. Stop sits just above the sweep wick high (or below the sweep wick low) — price has already proven it cannot hold there. That tight invalidation buys you 3R–5R targets to the opposite side of the range. If your stop has to be wider than the wick, the level wasn’t the right one.

Worked Example — BTC Short on UTAD at $80,000
SetupBTC compresses below $80,000 for 5 sessions. Funding has been negative for 46+ days — shorts crowded. Prior session high = $79,580. Round-number magnet at $80,000.
Sweep candle1h candle wicks to $80,420, harvests buy-side stops above $79,580 + the round-number cluster. Body closes at $79,720. Volume on the candle = 2.4× the 20-bar average.
Reclaim triggerNext 1h candle closes at $79,510 — back below the prior swing high. Enter short on close at $79,510.
Stop$80,500 (just above sweep wick). Risk = $990/BTC (~1.25%).
Target 1$77,200 (range mid + prior support). +$2,310/BTC (R:R ~2.3:1). Trail stop to entry.

Timeframe fit: 5m–15m for the sweep candle & reclaim trigger, 1h–4h for the level mapping, daily for bias. Sweeps on the daily are rare but high-conviction; intraday sweeps are frequent but lower-quality — demand more confluence at lower TFs.

The $73,800 ATH Sweep — Textbook UTAD

March 14, 2024: BTC printed an all-time high at $73,835 on Coinbase — the precise round-number-adjacent level above which most algorithmic and retail buy-stops were placed. Within four days price had failed back below $69,000; within nine weeks it had drawn down to $56,500. The wick on the daily candle that touched the ATH was the cleanest UTAD on a major asset in years.

The mechanics were identical to the current setup: the move into the high coincided with funding rates flipping aggressively positive (longs piling in), open interest at a then-record, and the round-number cluster at $74,000 telegraphed for weeks. The daily wick swept buy-side stops, the body closed back below; the reclaim never came. Anyone who shorted the wick blind got stopped on the spike; anyone who waited for the daily close back below $73,000 captured the entire 23% drawdown that followed.

§07
Common Traps
Five ways traders get sliced trying to trade sweeps
The single biggest mistake: calling every wick a sweep. A wick that doesn’t reclaim isn’t a sweep — it’s a real break, and fading it is how accounts die.
1
Trading the Wick, Not the Reclaim

The wick is the warning, not the entry. Entering on the spike puts you in front of momentum that may continue for several more candles before reversing — if it reverses at all. Always wait for the body close back inside the swept level on the operating timeframe. The cost is a marginally worse entry; the saving is not getting stopped on the candle that doesn’t reverse.

2
Confusing a Sweep With a Genuine Breakout

Most "sweeps" are real breakouts in disguise. The differentiator is what happens after the level is taken: a sweep reclaims within 1–3 candles; a real break holds and continues. If price stays outside the level for 3+ candles on the operating timeframe, abandon the fade thesis — the move is real and you’re fighting genuine demand or supply.

3
Sweep Setups at Random Levels

Stops cluster at obvious places — prior swing highs/lows, equal-highs/equal-lows, round numbers, session/daily/weekly extremes. A "sweep" at a random mid-range price is just a wick. If you can’t articulate which liquidity pool the move was hunting, the setup isn’t real. No pool = no sweep.

4
Ignoring Funding & OI Context

The highest-quality sweeps fire at positioning extremes — sustained negative funding (shorts crowded, BSL above) or sustained positive funding (longs crowded, SSL below). A sweep without crowded positioning behind it is a pattern without a fuel source. Check funding history (30+ days) and OI direction before sizing into a sweep thesis.

5
Treating "Liquidity Sweep" as Novel SMC Alpha

The mechanic is a century old — Wyckoff documented Springs and UTADs in the 1930s; Larry Williams was teaching it as the Specialist Trap in the 1980s. The current ICT/SMC vocabulary repackages the same observation in slicker language. The sweep concept is real and useful; the marketing claim that it’s "smart-money-only knowledge" is hype. Every algo desk maps these levels too — that’s why obvious sweeps now front-run themselves on minute charts. Use the framework as one input, not a magic system.
