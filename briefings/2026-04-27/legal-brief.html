Prediction Markets Open Three New Fronts in 72 Hours: Brazil Bans Kalshi & Polymarket, Nevada Court Forces May 4 Geofence, US Soldier Indicted for $400K Maduro Insider Trade
"Kalshi's sports outcome contracts are indistinguishable from gambling." — Nevada District Court Judge Jason Woodbury, granting preliminary injunction in Nevada Gaming Control Board v. Kalshi (Nev. Dist. Ct., April 20, 2026)

The federal-versus-state preemption fight covered in Sunday's Brief is now one front in a much larger reckoning. Between April 20 and April 26, prediction markets faced three new escalations on three different vectors — international regulatory rejection, state injunctive relief that survived Kalshi's preemption arguments, and the first federal criminal indictment for insider trading on a CFTC-registered event-contract platform.

On April 24, Brazilian Finance Minister Dario Durigan announced at a Brasília press conference that Polymarket and Kalshi were out of compliance with federal gambling laws. Telecoms regulator Anatel acted within hours, blocking access to both platforms across Brazil. This is the first time a G20 jurisdiction outside North America has taken coordinated infrastructure-level action against the platforms.

On April 20, a Nevada state judge granted the Nevada Gaming Control Board a preliminary injunction blocking Kalshi from offering sports, entertainment, and election-related contracts in Nevada. Crucially, the court ruled against Kalshi's federal-preemption defence at the preliminary-injunction stage — the opposite outcome of the Arizona TRO of April 10 that the CFTC has been citing as its template victory. Kalshi must implement mandatory Nevada geofencing by May 4.

On April 25, federal prosecutors unsealed an indictment against Gannon Ken Van Dyke, a US soldier alleged to have used classified intelligence to win more than $400,000 betting on the capture of Nicolás Maduro in Venezuela on Polymarket and Kalshi. Under direct pressure from Justice Department investigators, both platforms have rolled out emergency restrictions barring politicians from trading their own campaigns, athletes from trading their own leagues, and government employees from trading on contracts tied to their employment.

The composite picture: prediction markets that won the legal-jurisdictional argument with the CFTC's preemption suits are now losing the legitimacy argument across three independent vectors — international, judicial, and ethical. The federal-preemption shield does not cure any of these problems.

📎 Historical Precedent: Closest analog is the 2018 Murphy v. NCAA aftermath — federal sports-betting preemption fell, but states then split on integrity-based restrictions targeting insider proximity (player betting bans, athletic-staff prohibitions). The pattern suggests integrity-policing survives federal preemption regardless of the underlying jurisdictional outcome.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
The Nevada preliminary injunction is the single most damaging development for the CFTC's preemption strategy. Where Arizona's TRO favoured Kalshi at the same procedural stage, Nevada Judge Woodbury found the public-interest factor weighed against preemption. That is not a winning trial outcome — but it is a clear circuit-level signal that not every federal court will reflexively defer to CEA § 2(a)(1). Combined with 37 state AGs in the Massachusetts SJC amicus, the CFTC's litigation-victory streak is no longer assured. Brazil's ban is meanwhile a template other jurisdictions can copy at low cost — Anatel's takedown was administrative, not judicial. Expect Mexico, Argentina, India, and the EU to evaluate similar action. The Van Dyke indictment is the existential one: it puts CFTC-registered DCMs on the hook for insider-trading market-surveillance under CEA Core Principle 3, and it puts platform employees within reach of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 securities-and-commodities fraud. Watch DOJ's next moves — co-conspirator charges or platform-level enforcement would be a structural shock.
🔄 Contrarian Take
The Brazil ban and Nevada injunction may actually accelerate Kalshi and Polymarket's path to legitimacy. Insider-trading scandals create the political space for these platforms to voluntarily adopt the same exclusion lists that derivatives exchanges have run for decades — bringing prediction markets into structural alignment with mainstream commodity venues. Two years from now, the April 2026 reckoning may be remembered as the moment prediction markets stopped pretending they were "different" from CME-style infrastructure and started looking like mature regulated venues with actual surveillance budgets. The losers in that scenario aren't Kalshi or Polymarket — they're the offshore prediction markets that can't afford the surveillance regime.

EU AI Act Digital Omnibus: Second Trilogue Tomorrow Could Push High-Risk Compliance to December 2027 — But If Talks Fail, August 2 Deadline Stands
"If trilogue collapses or endorsement is delayed past 2 August 2026, the original high-risk obligations apply as written, and organizations should treat August 2 as the operational deadline until Official Journal publication confirms otherwise." — A&O Shearman trilogue analysis (April 24, 2026)

The European Commission, Parliament, and Council resume the second three-way "trilogue" negotiations on the Digital Omnibus AI proposal on Tuesday, April 28, 2026. The Omnibus is the EU's response to industry pressure that AI Act high-risk-system requirements coming into force August 2, 2026 — a deadline three months away — are operationally unworkable for most providers. The package proposes three distinct delay tracks:

Track 1 (standalone high-risk AI): Compliance deadline pushed from August 2, 2026 to December 2, 2027 — a 16-month extension.
Track 2 (high-risk AI embedded in regulated products): AI in medical devices, machinery, vehicles, etc., subject to AI Act and sectoral product-safety law gets pushed to August 2, 2028 — a full 24-month delay.
Track 3 (watermarking): AI-generated audio, image, video, and text watermarking holds at November 2, 2026 — no relief.

The political math is tight. Council and Parliament rapporteurs are reportedly aligned on Track 1 timeline relief but split on whether to use the Omnibus as a vehicle to also weaken substantive obligations (risk management systems, data governance, fundamental rights impact assessments). Commission negotiator Roberto Viola has signalled that the Commission will not accept substantive weakening — only timeline relief.

The legal risk for AI providers is now bimodal. If trilogue agrees by mid-May, the August 2026 deadline melts into late 2027 and the Atlantic regulatory gap widens. If trilogue fails or slides into June, providers must execute the original August 2 timeline — meaning conformity assessments, technical documentation, post-market monitoring systems, and notified-body engagement are all needed within roughly 90 working days from now.

📎 Historical Precedent: Closest analog is GDPR's 2018 "soft-launch" enforcement — original deadline held, but regulators publicly signalled forbearance for first 12 months. AI Act has no equivalent forbearance mechanism; enforcement is pre-set to bite at the deadline absent legislative change.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
The Digital Omnibus is the largest political stress-test of the AI Act since enactment. Failure of trilogue does not just mean providers face a tight August 2 deadline — it signals that the EU's tech-policy coalition (Commission + ESRB-aligned member states + Parliament Greens) cannot be moved even when industry has the Commission's own Director-General publicly pushing for relief. That outcome would harden enforcement posture and reduce private-letter forbearance prospects for the next 24 months. Conversely, agreement signals that the EU is now fully in "competitive realignment" mode against the US, and we should expect parallel relief packages on DSA fundamental rights audits, DMA gatekeeper obligations, and the GDPR Article 22 automated-decision-making rules. Watch the Council Presidency's negotiating mandate document — leaked draft, if any, will tell the market within hours whether trilogue will agree or stall.
🔄 Contrarian Take
Even if trilogue agrees, US providers should not assume they have until December 2027. National competent authorities (CNIL in France, BfDI in Germany, Garante in Italy) retain residual enforcement powers under the existing GDPR/eIDAS/Product Safety stack — and have publicly signalled they will use those tools to apply AI-Act-equivalent standards regardless of the Omnibus timeline. The "delay" is therefore primarily a notified-body and conformity-assessment timing relief — not a substantive enforcement holiday. Plan capability for August 2026 even if the formal deadline slides.
🏗️ Industry Implications
Beneficiaries (if trilogue agrees): OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Mistral — frontier labs gain 16+ months of runway. Microsoft Azure AI services and AWS Bedrock buy time on EU customer deployment certifications. Medical-device AI (Aidoc, PathAI) and automotive-AI providers (Mobileye, Cerence) gain 24 months. Losers if trilogue agrees: Notified bodies (TÜV SÜD, BSI, Dekra) lose the August 2 revenue cliff. EU compliance-tech vendors (Credo AI, Holistic AI) lose the deadline-driven sales pull. Crypto x AI cross-impact: any project using LLMs to assist DeFi user-interface decisions, on-chain compliance, or KYC/AML automation will face EU AI Act constraints regardless of MiCA status — the regulatory stack is additive, not substitutable.

Australia DAF Act Receives Royal Assent — 14-Month AFSL Compliance Sprint Begins, Class No-Action Cover Drops July 1
"Operators of digital asset platforms and tokenised custody platforms must obtain an Australian Financial Services Licence from ASIC, bringing them under the same core rules as brokers or fund managers." — Treasury policy summary, Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Act 2026

The Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Act 2026 received Royal Assent on April 8, 2026, completing Australia's first comprehensive digital-asset law. The Act creates two new financial product categories — Digital Asset Platforms (DAPs) and Tokenised Custody Platforms (TCPs) — and brings them within the AFSL (Australian Financial Services Licence) regime. Commencement is set for April 9, 2027 — but the operational sprint starts now.

The compliance cliff is the June 30, 2026 expiry of ASIC's class no-action letter that has shielded unlicensed crypto exchanges and custody providers from formal enforcement under existing Corporations Act provisions. Once the no-action expires, platforms operating without an AFSL application on file face ASIC enforcement risk for nine months until the new framework takes effect. ASIC has been explicit: there is no automatic continuation.

The Act includes a low-value carve-out: providers processing under A$10 million annually or holding under A$5,000 per customer are exempt from AFSL requirements. This is a meaningful threshold — it functionally protects most peer-to-peer DEXs and small-scale OTC desks while forcing institutional-scale exchanges (Independent Reserve, BTC Markets, Swyftx, CoinJar, plus Binance Australia, Coinbase Australia, Kraken Australia) into the licensing perimeter.

Estimated economic impact, per Digital Finance Cooperative Research Center modelling: A$24 billion annually in tokenised markets, payments, and digital assets — roughly 1% of GDP. The Treasury's accompanying Tranche 1b consultation on custody, ePayments, and APRA prudential treatment is expected mid-2026 and will define how the new framework interacts with existing depositary regulation.

📎 Historical Precedent: Closest analog is the UK's 2017 MiFID II authorisation transition — the FCA's "temporary permissions regime" (TPR) provided structural cover during the gap year. Australia's DAF Act provides no equivalent — the June 30 no-action drop is a hard step.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
The June 30 cliff is the immediate risk. ASIC's class no-action is the only thing protecting unlicensed crypto exchanges from existing Corporations Act § 911A unlicensed-financial-services prosecutions. Once it expires, every exchange operating without an AFSL application has nine months of enforcement-eligible exposure before the DAF Act framework actually takes effect April 2027. ASIC Chair Joe Longo has already signalled that platforms "operating in bad faith" — i.e. without engagement with the licensing roadmap — should expect enforcement action during the gap. Practitioners advising Australian crypto exchanges must file (or evidence a pre-application) by June 30. The A$10M / A$5,000-per-customer exemption is the design feature most likely to be litigated — Treasury's drafting language is novel and untested. Expect ASIC information-gathering powers (Reg Guide 167) to be used aggressively against borderline-exemption claims.
🔄 Contrarian Take
The DAF Act is more permissive than its surface suggests. Australia chose AFSL — a single financial-services licence with proven adaptability — rather than building a crypto-specific regime like MiCA or the FCA's perimeter rules. This means existing AFS licensees (banks, fund managers, brokers) can extend their licences to cover DAP/TCP activities without a fundamentally new authorisation track. Macquarie, ASX, NAB Wealth, and AustralianSuper are operationally closer to running compliant DAPs than any pure-crypto exchange. Australia may have just handed its banks the institutional crypto-custody opportunity rather than the crypto-native players.
🏗️ Industry Implications
Direct exposure: Independent Reserve, BTC Markets, Swyftx, CoinJar, Coinspot — all crypto-native AU exchanges face a 14-month compliance build with no parallel revenue stream. Beneficiaries: Existing AU financial institutions (Macquarie, ASX, NAB, CBA, Westpac) — they can extend AFSL coverage to DAP/TCP at marginal cost. Tokenisation infrastructure providers (Zerocap, DigitalX, Fireblocks AU) — they sell into the new licensee build-out. Compliance and audit firms (KPMG AU, EY AU, Deloitte AU) — fee acceleration through 2027. Losers: Offshore-based platforms targeting Australian users without intent to license (e.g. Bybit, KuCoin, OKX without AU subsidiaries) face § 911A unlicensed-services exposure from July 1. Capital flow: A$24bn annual addressable market is now functionally restricted to AFSL-holding entities — institutional capital flows toward incumbent banks rather than crypto-natives.

🔴 Bearish: Crypto-pure-play AU listed names without AFSL pathway (DigitalX/DCC.AX has one; many smaller listed crypto names do not)

GENIUS Act: Three Open Comment Periods Define US Stablecoin Future — FinCEN/OFAC AML Rule (June 9), Treasury State-Similarity (June 2), FDIC Application Procedures (May 18)
"[The proposal] makes clear that transactions in payment stablecoins are covered by the Travel Rule." — Sullivan & Cromwell client memo on FinCEN/OFAC GENIUS Act NPRM (April 14, 2026)

The implementation phase of the GENIUS Act is now fully visible across three concurrent rulemakings, each with a confirmed comment deadline. Together they define what it means to be a Permitted Payment Stablecoin Issuer (PPSI) in the United States — the BSA-equivalent obligations, the prudential framework, and the federal-versus-state regulatory map.

FinCEN / OFAC Joint NPRM — Permitted Payment Stablecoin Issuer AML & Sanctions Compliance Program Requirements. Published in the Federal Register April 10, 2026 (2026-06963). Treats PPSIs as financial institutions under the Bank Secrecy Act. Imposes: AML program, sanctions compliance program, technical capability to block / freeze / reject specific transactions, and confirmation that payment stablecoin transactions are subject to the Travel Rule. Comments due June 9, 2026.

Treasury State-Similarity NPRM. Treasury seeks public comment on the methodology for determining whether state regulatory regimes are "substantially similar" to the federal GENIUS Act framework — the gating question for whether a stablecoin issuer can rely on a state licence rather than seeking direct federal approval. Comments due June 2, 2026.

FDIC Application Procedures NPRM. The FDIC Board approved on April 7, 2026 a proposed rule establishing the application process for FDIC-supervised insured depository institutions seeking to issue payment stablecoins via subsidiaries. Comment period was originally set to close February 17 then extended; new deadline May 18, 2026. The NPRM addresses authorised vs. prohibited PPSI activities, reserve assets, capital, and risk-management standards tailored to PPSI size, complexity, and risk profile.

The American Bankers Association has formally requested a 60-day extension on all GENIUS Act rulemakings, citing the volume and complexity of overlapping comment periods. Treasury and FDIC have not granted further extensions as of April 26 — but a coordinated 60-day push is plausible given the bank-lobby pressure documented in Sunday's Brief.

📎 Historical Precedent: Closest analog is Dodd-Frank Title VII swap rules (2011–2012) — multiple parallel CFTC and SEC rulemakings forced industry to staff dedicated multi-rule comment teams. Same dynamic now applies to stablecoin issuers and their bank counterparties.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
The three rulemakings are not independent — they interact in ways that change the strategic calculus for every prospective issuer. The Treasury state-similarity rule determines whether NY-DFS, Wyoming SPDI, or California DFPI licences can serve as substitutes for federal authorisation. If Treasury sets a high "substantially similar" bar, state licensure becomes a dead letter and OCC trust charters become the only practical route — favouring Coinbase, Ripple, Circle, and Anchorage Digital, all of whom have OCC applications pending. If Treasury sets a low bar, the existing state-licensed issuer pipeline (Paxos via NY, Anchorage via OCC, BUSD's NY-DFS pathway) gets functional federal recognition — favouring the existing market and slowing OCC migration. The FinCEN/OFAC Travel Rule confirmation is the operationally heaviest rule — it forces every payment stablecoin to implement counterparty-data exchange between issuers, exchanges, and custodians, which is currently absent from most US stablecoin transfers above the $3,000 threshold.
🔄 Contrarian Take
The bank lobby's 60-day extension push is a red herring. Even with 60 more days, the substantive content of the rules is already settled — this is bureaucratic delay, not a substantive challenge. The real fight is in the CLARITY Act stablecoin-yield definition that the Senate Banking Committee will mark up in May. If banks lose the yield fight, the GENIUS Act rules become operational frosting on the cake — issuer compliance burdens that all parties already accept. If banks win the yield fight (no rewards above 0.01% on stablecoin balances), the GENIUS Act rules become a structural moat against Coinbase / Ripple / Circle revenue models. Watch CLARITY, not GENIUS, for the strategic outcome.

Direct exposure: Circle (USDC), Tether (USDT — though offshore status complicates GENIUS coverage), Paxos (USDP, BUSD legacy), Ripple (RLUSD), PayPal USD, MakerDAO USDS — every regulated stablecoin issuer is in the comment-period window. Beneficiaries: Travel Rule infrastructure providers (Notabene, Sumsub, Veriff, Sygna). Bank counterparties of stablecoin issuers (BNY Mellon for USDC reserves, BlackRock for USDC reserves) — clarified prudential treatment supports counterparty risk pricing. Losers if banks win 60-day extension: Stablecoin issuers with revenue tied to expedited federal authorisation — Circle's IPO timing assumes Q2 federal recognition. Capital flow: Treasury's state-similarity rule is the single highest-leverage factor for state-licensed issuers' equity valuation.
📊 Portfolio Impact
🟢 Bullish: Circle (CRCL — public-listed; faster federal recognition path), Coinbase (COIN — USDC partner), BNY Mellon (BK — reserves custody)
🔴 Bearish: Bank trade-association-aligned regional banks (KeyCorp, M&T, Comerica) — extension push is rear-guard; the framework is winning
➡️ Neutral: Tether (private) — offshore status creates GENIUS coverage ambiguity that is feature, not bug, for management

DeFi Education Fund + 30 Organizations Press SEC to Convert April 13 User-Interface Safe Harbor Into Formal Rulemaking — Peirce Backs Permanent Overhaul
"Legacy definitions risk misclassifying software providers and infrastructure participants. Formal rulemaking, rather than guidance, is essential for long-term regulatory clarity." — SEC Commissioner Hester Peirce, April 2026 remarks on DeFi user-interface framework

On April 21, 2026, the DeFi Education Fund led a coalition of more than 30 organisations in a public comment letter to the SEC backing the Division of Trading and Markets' April 13 staff statement on broker-dealer registration relief for "Covered User Interface Providers" — but pressing for the relief to be converted from informal staff guidance into a formal rule with notice-and-comment status.

The April 13 staff statement — covered in last Friday's Brief as Story #2 — relieved certain technology providers operating software interfaces for cryptoasset securities transactions from broker-dealer registration, subject to conditions: pre-disclosed objective parameters, no exercise of control over transaction outcomes, no routing decisions, and no market-making functionality. The DeFi Education Fund letter argues these conditions are workable, but staff guidance can be withdrawn at any time without process — leaving builders exposed to retroactive enforcement risk.

SEC Commissioner Hester Peirce, in separate remarks, called for a "permanent overhaul" of broker-dealer rules to align with decentralised technologies — explicitly endorsing notice-and-comment rulemaking over staff guidance. Peirce's position aligns with Chair Atkins' stated April 21 intention to roll the safe harbor into the broader Regulation Crypto Assets package later in 2026.

📎 Historical Precedent: Closest analog is the SEC's 1996 No-Action Letter regime for online broker-dealers, which lived as informal guidance until codified in Reg ATS (2000). Same trajectory for DeFi front-end providers: guidance now, formal rule by 2027.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
The April 21 letter is a procedural escalation, not a substantive disagreement with the SEC. DeFi Education Fund and the 30+ co-signers want certainty — and Peirce's "permanent overhaul" framing suggests the SEC is willing to deliver it via the upcoming Reg Crypto Assets rulemaking. The legal mechanism is to convert the April 13 staff statement into an interpretive rule (no notice-and-comment required) or a substantive rule (notice-and-comment required). Practitioners advising DeFi front-end providers should preserve current operations under the staff statement — but document compliance contemporaneously, because if the SEC pivots to an interpretive rule, the conditions in the April 13 statement become the binding test.
🏗️ Industry Implications
Beneficiaries: Uniswap Labs, dYdX, GMX, Aerodrome, PancakeSwap, Jupiter — every major DEX front-end provider gains operational certainty. Open-source DEX-aggregator providers (1inch, ParaSwap) gain similar coverage. Crypto-native compliance vendors: Chainalysis, TRM Labs, Elliptic gain a regulatory tailwind for "DeFi compliance" SaaS. Risk: Front-ends that exercise routing or market-making functions (some MEV-extracting front-ends, certain order-book DEXs) fall outside the safe harbour — face higher enforcement risk.

Medium Japan FSA / Diet PRIMARY Japan Crypto Regulation
🟢 High — Japanese Diet enacted FIEA reclassification April 10, 2026
Japan Reclassifies Crypto Under FIEA — Criminal Penalties Up to 10 Years Prison and ¥10 Million Fine; APAC Enforcement Hardens
"Japan approved reclassifying crypto under FIEA on April 10, 2026, with penalties increased to 10 years prison and ¥10M fine." — Japan FSA / Diet enactment summary (April 2026)

The Japanese Diet enacted on April 10, 2026 a reclassification of crypto-assets under the Financial Instruments and Exchange Act (FIEA) — moving them out of the Payment Services Act (PSA) framework that has governed Japanese crypto since 2017. The reclassification subjects unlicensed offering, insider trading, and market manipulation in crypto to criminal penalties of up to 10 years imprisonment and ¥10 million in fines.

The FIEA reclassification is the most consequential Japanese crypto regulatory change since the original PSA framework. It moves crypto from a payments-utility regulatory posture to a financial-instruments enforcement posture — explicitly importing securities-style market-abuse rules. JFSA Director-General Kazumasa Kobayashi has signalled that early enforcement will focus on insider trading at Japanese exchanges (bitFlyer, Coincheck, GMO Coin, BitBank) and on cross-border manipulation patterns.

The reclassification also reshapes the tax treatment: gains on crypto-asset trading shift from miscellaneous income (taxed up to 55%) to capital gains (taxed at 20% flat) — a major tax-policy concession in exchange for the heavier enforcement framework. Industry response has been broadly supportive of the tax change while wary of the new criminal exposure.

⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
Japan's FIEA reclassification is the strongest signal yet that APAC regulators are converging on a hybrid model — institutional-friendly tax treatment paired with hard criminal enforcement against market abuse. The model contrasts sharply with the EU's MiCA (authorisation-driven) and the US's market-structure framework (jurisdictional-classification driven). Practitioners advising Japanese exchanges face an immediate compliance lift: insider-trading detection programs, market-surveillance infrastructure, and pre-clearance regimes for employees and large customers. The 20% flat capital gains rate is the carrot — the 10-year prison exposure is the stick.

Medium Pakistan Crypto Council / PVARA PRIMARY Emerging Market Pivot
🟢 High — Pakistan Crypto Council and PVARA established as of April 2026; framework formally replaces prior ban
Pakistan Establishes Virtual Asset Regulatory Authority — Reverses Prior Ban, Creates Crypto Council; Emerging-Market Regulatory Pivot Continues

Pakistan in April 2026 formally replaced its prior crypto ban with a new regulatory framework — establishing the Pakistan Virtual Asset Regulatory Authority (PVARA) and a parallel Crypto Council to advise government on digital-asset policy. The PVARA assumes licensing and supervisory powers for crypto exchanges, custody providers, and stablecoin issuers operating in Pakistan.

The reversal is a significant emerging-market regulatory pivot. Pakistan joins Egypt, Argentina, Nigeria, Kenya, and the Philippines in the cohort of large emerging markets that have moved from prohibition or ambiguity to structured licensing in the past 18 months. The cumulative effect is to shift the global regulatory map: the OECD-sphere (US, EU, UK, Japan, Australia, Singapore) is converging on heavy-regulation frameworks while emerging-market jurisdictions are converging on lighter authorisation regimes — competing for capital flow.

Per NFT Plazas regulatory tracker data, 68 countries have now enacted or proposed crypto-specific legislation — a 62% increase in two years. Over 92% of jurisdictions have tightened rules in some form.

⚖️ Practitioner Analysis
Pakistan's pivot is operationally meaningful for global stablecoin and remittance flows. Pakistan is one of the top-five remittance destinations globally; legitimising crypto rails creates a regulated channel for what is already a substantial stablecoin-and-Bitcoin remittance corridor (USD/PKR via Binance, Bybit, OKX historically). For practitioners advising emerging-market crypto businesses, the pattern is now clear: bans are being replaced by light-touch authorisation regimes that compete on speed-to-licence rather than substantive permissiveness. Expect Pakistan's PVARA framework to look operationally similar to the UAE's VARA, the Philippines' BSP regime, and Argentina's CNV approach.
