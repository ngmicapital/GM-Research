The Other Mind

How the octopus built intelligence from scratch, entirely without our blueprint — 550 million years after our paths diverged

The octopus built intelligence completely independently from vertebrates, 550 million years after our last common ancestor. Two-thirds of its neurons live in its arms, not its brain, suggesting that mind itself might be far stranger and more distributed than we have ever assumed.

TL;DR

Octopuses and vertebrates last shared a common ancestor over 550 million years ago — a simple, soft creature with barely a nerve net. Since then, cephalopods have independently evolved large brains, problem-solving, delayed gratification, individual recognition, and what might be dreams. Their neural architecture is built on entirely different molecular principles from ours. They are the closest thing to an alien intelligence we have ever encountered, and they live in our oceans.

Section 01

The Story

In 2008, staff at the Sea Star Aquarium in Coburg, Germany grew baffled by a recurring electrical fault. Lights in the aquarium kept shorting out overnight. When they set up cameras, they found the culprit: an octopus named Otto, who had learned to climb to the edge of his tank and shoot jets of water at the overhead spotlight until it shorted. He did it repeatedly. Some staff speculated he found the light annoying. Others thought he was bored. No one could rule either out.

This is not a cute animal trick. It is evidence of something stranger and more philosophically troubling: a mind shaped by 550 million years of evolution completely separate from the line that produced fish, reptiles, birds, mammals, and us — arriving at something that looks, from the outside, disturbingly like intent.

The story of cephalopod intelligence begins in the Cambrian period, before complex eyes, before jaws, before skeletons. The last common ancestor of octopuses and humans was almost certainly a small, flat, worm-like creature — soft-bodied, without a centralized brain, possibly without bilateral symmetry. From that simple start, two wildly different experiments in nervous system design began. The vertebrate line built up: spinal cord, brainstem, cerebellum, cortex. The cephalopod line went sideways: losing its protective shell over millions of years, gaining instead extraordinary flexibility, camouflage, and, eventually, a strange and distributed form of intelligence.

The Neapolitan Experiments

In the 1990s, neurobiologist Graziano Fiorito at the Stazione Zoologica Anton Dohrn in Naples ran a series of experiments that changed how scientists thought about invertebrate learning. His octopuses not only learned to open jars to retrieve live crabs — they improved with repetition. More striking: naive octopuses that had merely watched trained individuals perform tasks subsequently completed them faster. Cephalopods could learn by observation. The implications for a supposedly “primitive” animal were unsettling.

By the early 2000s, reports had accumulated from aquariums around the world: octopuses escaping tanks at night to raid adjacent fish tanks, then returning before morning. An individual at the National Aquarium of New Zealand did this regularly for several weeks before staff caught on. Individually named octopuses recognized and responded differently to different keepers — a finding eventually confirmed by a 2010 study at the Seattle Aquarium, where eight octopuses (Enteroctopus dofleini) were shown to distinguish between two humans who had treated them differently, using distinct body-pattern responses for each.

We tend to think of intelligence as a single thing that evolved once, culminating in us. The octopus is the strongest possible argument against that view.
Section 02

The Mechanism

Here is what makes the octopus genuinely strange: it has approximately 500 million neurons — roughly comparable to a dog. But only about 180 million of those are in the central brain, a donut-shaped structure wrapped around the esophagus (food literally passes through the middle of the octopus’s brain). The remaining 350 million neurons — roughly two-thirds of the entire nervous system — are distributed across the eight arms, about 40 million per arm.

This is not a division of labor the way we have peripheral nerves that report to a central command. Octopus arms can solve problems autonomously. Research by Binyamin Hochner’s group at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem showed that when an octopus reaches toward food, the arm extension is largely self-organized — the central brain sends a “reach” command, and the arm works out the kinematics itself. The arms can taste and touch simultaneously through chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors in their suckers. They are, in a meaningful sense, semi-independent agents.

Key Finding — Gutnick et al., 2020 (Current Biology)

A long-running claim held that octopuses effectively had “nine brains” — one central, one per arm. Dr. Tamar Gutnick’s 2020 study showed the picture is more nuanced: the central brain can learn a maze and transfer that learning to untrained arms, confirming centralized coordination does occur. The better description: one brain with eight very clever arms that negotiate rather than simply obey.

At the molecular level, the divergence from vertebrate design runs deep. In vertebrate visual systems, the cells that process the first stages of retinal output are predominantly inhibitory — using GABA or glycine as neurotransmitters to regulate signal flow. In the octopus optic lobe (the primary visual processing center, located behind the eyes rather than in the retina itself), the analogous cells are predominantly dopaminergic — an entirely different chemical logic. The octopus visual system arrives at similar capabilities via a different molecular toolkit.

The convergence goes even further with the eye itself. The octopus independently evolved a camera-type eye — a lens, a retina, an iris — structurally similar to the vertebrate eye. But the retina is wired in reverse: vertebrate photoreceptors point away from incoming light (a quirk that creates the blind spot); octopus photoreceptors point toward the light. The octopus built the same instrument and corrected our design flaw, then routed the output through a completely different neural architecture.

The octopus eye and the vertebrate eye arrived at near-identical solutions to the same physics problem — and then diverged completely in everything downstream.

This matters for theories of consciousness. Much of what philosophers use to ground claims about subjective experience in animals relies on shared neural homology — similar brain structures, similar evolutionary heritage. Octopuses have neither. Their intelligence, their apparent curiosity, their individual personalities, their probable pain responses: all of it was built on a completely different foundation. If consciousness requires a cortex, octopuses should be philosophical zombies. Most people who have spent time with them do not believe that for a moment.

Section 03

The Connections

The marshmallow test revisited. In 2021, Alexandra Schnell and colleagues at Cambridge published a study in Proceedings of the Royal Society B in which cuttlefish — close cephalopod relatives — were offered a choice: eat a piece of dead shrimp now, or wait for a live shrimp later. All tested cuttlefish demonstrated delayed gratification, waiting up to 130 seconds for the better reward. Crucially, the cuttlefish that waited longest also performed best on separate learning tasks — the first documented link between self-control and intelligence in any animal outside humans and great apes. The marshmallow test, once a marker of human cognitive development, now maps onto a creature with no backbone, three hearts, and blue blood.

They may dream. Octopuses show two distinct sleep states: a quiet phase with pale, motionless skin, and an active phase in which the skin flashes through rapid color and texture changes while the eyes flicker. Researcher David Scheel at Alaska Pacific University filmed octopuses cycling through what appeared to be the chromatic signatures of prey, camouflage patterns, and mating displays — all while asleep. Whether this constitutes dreaming in any experiential sense is unknown. But the neurological parallel to REM sleep is striking enough that researchers are taking it seriously.

The solitude problem. One of the deepest puzzles in cephalopod cognition is why octopuses are not smarter. They have the neural substrate for remarkable intelligence, yet most species live only two to five years and are almost entirely solitary. The two great accelerators of vertebrate intelligence — long childhoods with parental learning transfer, and dense social environments requiring political cognition — are largely absent. Cephalopods appear to have evolved intelligence for individual ecological flexibility (hunting diverse, unpredictable prey in complex environments) rather than social problem-solving. It is a different use case for the same hardware.

Peter Godfrey-Smith — Other Minds (2016)

The philosopher of biology at the University of Sydney CUNY has become the most influential thinker on cephalopod consciousness. His book Other Minds: The Octopus, the Sea, and the Deep Origins of Consciousness argues that octopuses represent the best available window into an alternative evolutionary path toward mind. His framing: the Cambrian explosion produced the first eyes, first predator-prey arms races, and the first selection pressure for rapid, flexible behavioral response — the conditions that made intelligence worth building. Vertebrates and cephalopods both answered that pressure; they just used different materials.

What it means for the search for extraterrestrial intelligence. The octopus makes a compelling case that complex cognition is not a fluke of vertebrate evolution but something closer to a convergent attractor — a solution that biology finds repeatedly given sufficient selection pressure. If intelligence can evolve twice on one planet via completely different mechanisms, the prior probability of it arising elsewhere increases substantially. The octopus is not just an interesting animal. It is data about the universe.

The ethics problem nobody has solved. If octopuses are intelligent in a morally relevant sense — capable of something like suffering, curiosity, boredom, fear — then the fishing industry’s current treatment of them is deeply problematic. Approximately 350,000 tonnes of octopus are harvested globally each year, almost entirely without welfare considerations. The UK extended animal welfare legislation to cover cephalopods in 2021 after a Cambridge review found “strong evidence” of their sentience — making Britain one of the first countries to do so. Most of the world has not followed.

Further Down the Hole

📚
Other Minds — Peter Godfrey-Smith

The definitive account of cephalopod consciousness from a philosopher who dives with them off the Sydney coast. Best non-fiction written about what it might be like to be an octopus.

🧬
The Cambrian Explosion Connection

The rapid diversification of complex animal life ~541 million years ago produced the first eyes and first predator-prey arms races — the selection pressure that made intelligence worth building, for both vertebrates and cephalopods simultaneously.

🌈
Chromatophores: Skin as Display

Octopus skin contains up to 2 million chromatophores (pigment sacs), iridophores (reflective cells), and papillae (texture bumps), all under direct neural control. The skin is essentially a pixel display wired directly to the nervous system — capable of sub-second full-body pattern changes.

🧬
The MicroRNA Parallel

A 2022 study found that cephalopods experienced a dramatic expansion of microRNA genes — small regulatory molecules involved in neural development — paralleling a similar expansion in vertebrates. Two lineages, same molecular trick, same outcome: complex brains.

🔬
The Octopus Genome Surprise

When the California two-spot octopus genome was sequenced in 2015, researchers found it contained 33,000 protein-coding genes — significantly more than humans (approximately 20,000–25,000). Many were novel genes expressed specifically in the brain and suckers, with no vertebrate counterparts.
