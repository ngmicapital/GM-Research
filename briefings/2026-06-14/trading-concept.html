Alpha — Liquidity Heatmaps

Sun · 14 June 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #49 11 min read
TL;DR

A liquidity heatmap is the order book rendered over time: bright horizontal bands are large resting limit orders, and price is drawn toward them like a magnet — until they hold (support/resistance), get eaten (absorption), or vanish (spoof). Trade the reaction at the band, not the band itself — and never confuse this map of real resting orders with a Coinglass-style liquidation heatmap, which is a model estimate of where leverage gets force-closed, not actual orders.

§01
What It Is
The order book, painted across time
A liquidity heatmap stacks every snapshot of the limit-order book side by side over time, so resting size shows up as bright horizontal bands you can watch build, hold, or evaporate.

A depth-of-market ladder shows you the book right now. A liquidity heatmap shows you the book through time — it takes each instant's resting limit orders, colours them by size, and scrolls them left as new snapshots paint on the right. Per Bookmap, "on the right side of the vertical timeline is the current order book; on the left is the position of the order book in the past." That time axis is the whole point: it lets you see which liquidity is sticky and which is fleeting, something a static ladder physically cannot show.

Brightness encodes size. "Brighter colors indicate higher concentrations of resting orders, while lighter colors represent thinner liquidity." A persistent bright line at one price is a large passive participant parked there and refusing to move — a wall. The map's background is the passive side (limit orders waiting); the trade bubbles printed on top are the aggressive side (market orders eating them). You are watching supply and demand negotiate in real time, with a memory.

This builds directly on Order Book Resiliency (Issue #42) — the heatmap is resiliency rendered visually: a band that reloads as fast as it gets hit looks like a stubborn glowing line; one that thins each time price touches it fades to nothing. It also makes Iceberg orders (Issue #47) legible — a hidden refill shows up as a band that keeps re-lighting after every print that should have cleared it.

If you only remember one thing A liquidity heatmap is not a forecast — it is a live map of where real orders sit. Price doesn't have to reach a band; it's just pulled toward where the fills are, and the trade is in how the band behaves on contact.
§02
Mechanics
How depth becomes heat — and why price chases it
Each price level's resting size is normalised against the heaviest size on screen and mapped to a colour; price gets pulled toward heavy bands because that's where large fills — and trapped stops — are waiting.

The rendering rule is simple. For every column of time and every price row, sum the resting limit size at that level, normalise it against the largest resting block currently in view, and map the ratio to a colour ramp. Big relative size → bright; thin → dark. As price moves, the active trades (market orders) paint as bubbles on top, so you can see aggression colliding with the passive wall underneath.

brightness(price, t) ∝ resting_size(price, t) / max_resting_size_in_view
magnet pull ← unfilled liquidity + clustered stops/leverage on the far side of price
read on contact → band holds = absorption/support · band pulls = spoof · band breaks on volume = real continuation
Brightness is relative to the current window, not absolute — a "bright" band only means large versus what else is on screen right now.

Why does price chase the bright stuff? Because a market exists to facilitate trade, and the heaviest resting liquidity is where the most trade can happen. Large players who need to fill size are drawn to where the opposing orders are; and on the far side of price sit clustered stops and leveraged positions that, when triggered, become forced market orders pointing straight at that liquidity. The result is the "magnet" behaviour: price probes toward dense bands, fills against them, and then either rejects (the band absorbed it) or accelerates through (the band was overwhelmed).

Crucially, there are two different objects both called "heatmap," and conflating them is the single most expensive error in this space:

DimensionLiquidity heatmap (Bookmap / ATAS)Liquidation heatmap (Coinglass / Hyblock)
ShowsActual resting limit orders in the bookEstimated price levels where leverage gets force-closed
SourceReal Level-2 order-book feedModelled from open interest + assumed leverage tiers
Truth valueReal orders (but can be cancelled/spoofed)Probability, not certainty — positions may close early
Time axisHistorical book, scrollingForward projection of where clusters sit
Best useExecution: support/resistance, absorption, targetsContext: where a cascade could accelerate

The liquidity heatmap is observed; the liquidation heatmap is inferred. Coinglass itself frames its levels as estimates — they "represent probability, not certainty," and "the actual liquidation amounts may be lower than displayed because some traders close positions manually before reaching their liquidation price." Both are useful. Only one is real orders.

§04
In Practice
Trade the contact, target the next band
The one rule: a bright band is a location, not a signal — you don't enter at the band, you enter on how the band behaves when price arrives.
1

Wait for the reaction, not the touch

A glowing wall is a place where something will happen, not proof it holds. Let price reach it and watch: aggressive prints absorbed with no progress = long the hold; price slicing through on rising volume = the wall failed, go with the break.

2

Behaviour separates real from fake

Real liquidity sits and gets eaten yet stays lit (absorption). Spoofed liquidity is the band that "appears well in advance, but vanishes right before price touches it." If a wall pulls as price approaches, it was never support — fade the head-fake.

3

Use the far band as your target

The opposite wall is where price wants to go to collect fills — "price often moves toward these areas to 'collect' that liquidity" and pauses or reverses on arrival. Park take-profit just before the heavy band, not inside it, where the queue eats you.

4

Stack confluence, never trade it naked

A band plus rising CVD into it, plus stretched funding on the trapped side, is a setup. A band alone is a coin flip. The heatmap tells you where; CVD and funding tell you who's offside and whether the magnet will actually pull.

Worked example — BID-WALL HOLD, longing the absorption
SetupBTC drifts down into a persistent bright bid band at $62,000 that's been lit for hours; funding is negative (shorts crowded), CVD ticking up as price falls (buyers absorbing).
TriggerPrice sweeps to $61,900, the band stays lit, sell prints get absorbed with no further progress, price reclaims $62,000.
EntryLong $62,050 on the reclaim
Stop$61,750 (below the swept low, where the wall is proven failed) → 300 risk

Timeframe fit: heatmaps are an execution tool — 1m–15m for the entry on contact, with the 1h–4h structure deciding which wall you trust. Bands matter most intraday; they decay as the book rebuilds.

Live Tape
The June 3–4 magnet that ate the longs
In the 3–4 June cascade, the cluster of long-liquidation levels under $62K behaved exactly like a magnet — price was dragged down to sweep them near $61,300 before snapping back.
● Live Tape · BINANCE:BTCUSDT.P · 4H · 14 June 2026

Liquidity below became the destination, not the floor

The cleanest recent illustration of magnet behaviour was the 3–4 June flush. Spot-ETF outflows and a corporate sale knocked sentiment, and BTC slid into a dense band of long-liquidation levels stacked beneath the low-$62K shelf. Rather than holding as "support," that liquidity was the target: price was pulled straight down to collect it, printing ~$61,300 before recovering toward ~$62,500.

The forced selling tells the story — ~$1.8B liquidated on 3 June alone (the largest single day since February 2026), with longs accounting for ~$1.35B, and roughly $3B over the two days. That's the leveraged side of the heatmap firing: each margin call was a forced market sell pointing straight at the cluster, accelerating the move until the stops were cleared. By 13 June BTC had stabilised in the low-$60Ks (~$63–64K), still some ~51% under the October-2025 high near $126,200; ETH sat near $1,679.

§07
Common Traps
Where the heatmap eats beginners
The biggest mistake: treating a bright band as a buy/sell button instead of a location whose meaning is decided by how it behaves on contact.
1
Reading it as a crystal ball

A band shows where orders are, not where price must go. Price can ignore liquidity entirely on a strong trend day. Use it to locate the fight, then let the tape decide who wins.

2
Front-running spoofed size

Crypto books are full of orders that exist only to be seen. If you buy a "huge bid" before price tests it, you're holding the bag when it gets pulled. Wait for the band to actually absorb prints before trusting it.

3
Trusting one exchange's book

A wall on a single venue is thin evidence; depth is fragmented across Binance, Bybit, OKX and the rest. Spoofs and real walls both look the same on one feed — confirm across venues or aggregate.

4
Assuming you see the whole book

Icebergs hide the bulk of their size, and many crypto venues throttle or delay L2 to retail. The brightest band you can see may be a fraction of what's actually resting — or a placeholder over a hidden refill.

5
Overhyped: the liquidation heatmap as prophecy

The Coinglass/Hyblock liquidation map is genuinely useful for context — but it is modelled, not measured. Levels are estimated from open interest and assumed leverage; many positions close manually first, so "the actual liquidation amounts may be lower than displayed." Treating its glowing clusters as guaranteed magnets — or worse, confusing it with a real order-book liquidity heatmap — is how traders get chopped front-running a level that was always a probability, never a promise.
