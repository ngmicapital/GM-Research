The Coup That Made Modern Iran

How a British oil company, $1 million in CIA cash, and one rogue operative overthrew a democracy — and why every US-Iran crisis since traces back to a single day in August 1953.

In August 1953, the CIA and MI6 overthrew Iran's elected Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh — for nationalising an oil company — and the authoritarian Shah they installed guaranteed the 1979 Islamic Revolution and every US-Iran confrontation since. The ceasefire you read about today is downstream of decisions made in a Tehran hotel room 73 years ago.

The One-Paragraph Version

In August 1953, the CIA and British MI6 overthrew Mohammad Mosaddegh — Iran's elected, secular, democratic prime minister — because he nationalised an oil company. The operation cost about $1 million, took 19 days, and installed a Shah whose brutal authoritarianism guaranteed the 1979 Islamic Revolution, the hostage crisis, decades of sanctions, and every confrontation since. The ceasefire you read about in this morning's briefing is downstream of decisions made by a Teddy Roosevelt's grandson in a Tehran hotel room 73 years ago.

Section 01

The Story

In 1951, Mohammad Mosaddegh was the most popular politician in Iran — a Swiss-educated, democratically elected Prime Minister who wore pyjamas to press conferences and wept openly in parliament to make a point. He was also, by any measure, a moderate. Anticommunist. Pro-West in principle. He simply believed that Iranian oil should belong to Iranians.

The problem was the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company (AIOC) — a British-controlled monopoly that extracted Iranian crude under terms so lopsided that Britain's government received more in taxes from the AIOC's profits than Iran did in royalties from its own oil. When Mosaddegh nationalized the AIOC in March 1951, the British government imposed a naval blockade, orchestrated an international boycott of Iranian oil, and went to the Americans with a pitch: this man is a communist, and if he stays in power the Soviets will take Iran.

The Americans knew it was mostly false. Mosaddegh had actually suppressed the Tudeh (communist) Party. The CIA's own analysis doubted the Soviet threat. But the Eisenhower administration, newly elected in 1953, had a simpler calculus: a friendly Shah with stable oil fields beats an unpredictable democracy any day. The plan, codenamed TPAJAX by the CIA and Operation Boot by MI6, was approved.

"It was a British idea. We kind of went along with it. The British were more enthusiastic than we were." — Kermit Roosevelt Jr., CIA operative, reflecting decades later
March 1951
Mosaddegh nationalises the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company. British naval blockade begins. London brings the case to the International Court of Justice (and loses).
April 1953
CIA Director Allen Dulles authorises $1 million for a coup. Kermit Roosevelt Jr. — grandson of Theodore Roosevelt — is dispatched to Tehran as ground operations chief.
August 15–16, 1953
First coup attempt fails. Mosaddegh had been warned by a sympathetic officer. Key conspirators are arrested. The Shah panics and flees to Rome. CIA headquarters cables: "Operations against Mossadegh should be discontinued."
August 19, 1953
Roosevelt ignores headquarters and pushes forward. CIA-funded mobs storm Tehran. The military switches sides. Mosaddegh surrenders. The Shah returns from Rome. The operation is over in hours.
1953–1979
Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi rules with SAVAK — his secret police — torturing dissidents. The US signs over 40% of Iran's oil fields to American companies as thanks.
February 1979
The Islamic Revolution. The Shah flees. Ayatollah Khomeini returns. The new government's founding mythology is built on the 1953 coup — proof that the West cannot be trusted.

Mosaddegh was arrested, tried by a military tribunal, and sentenced to death. The Shah commuted it to three years' solitary confinement, followed by house arrest until his death in 1967. He spent his final years in his village, tending roses.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The operation had four working parts — and each one became a template used in coups for the next 50 years.

1. Media disinformation. Roosevelt bought the Tehran press. CIA-funded journalists ran stories linking Mosaddegh to communism, to the Soviet Union, to hostility toward Islam. The disinformation worked because it exploited real anxieties: the Cold War was at its peak, and the Tudeh Party was real, even if Mosaddegh wasn't allied with it. The lesson: you don't need a lie to destabilize a government — you need a plausible fear.

2. Manufactured street chaos. Roosevelt used a tactic so counterintuitive it sounds like fiction: he paid mobs to attack religious leaders and symbols of the Shah while chanting pro-Mosaddegh slogans. The goal was to make Mosaddegh look dangerous and radical, alienating his own moderate base. Actual Mosaddegh supporters assumed he'd lost control. The tactic was a perfect confusion machine — genuine supporters turned against him because the fake ones were too violent.

3. Military defection by rank. The Shah had issued a firman — a royal decree — dismissing Mosaddegh. When the first coup failed, the decree was suppressed. On August 19, CIA-connected officers distributed the firman to unit commanders across Tehran, framing the coup as a legal constitutional act. Soldiers who might have hesitated over a coup would follow a lawful royal order.

4. Ignoring orders. The most important mechanism was Kermit Roosevelt's decision to proceed after headquarters cabled to stand down. The CIA's own after-action report noted that the operation's success depended entirely on one operative's personal judgment that headquarters was wrong. The lesson institutional planners drew: when you have people on the ground, don't micromanage. The lesson critics drew: covert operations are structurally unaccountable.

"If we could get rid of Mosaddegh, we should do it... We will be sorry for the rest of our lives if we do not." — CIA Director Allen Dulles, April 1953, quoted in declassified cable

The CIA formally acknowledged the operation's existence in 2013, 60 years after the fact, when declassified documents were released by the National Security Archive. The internal history called it "a success" — a judgment that depends entirely on how far forward you're willing to look.

Section 03

The Connections

The 1953 coup didn't cause the 1979 revolution by itself. But it's the thread that runs through everything.

The Islamic Republic's founding mythology. Khomeini's government made the 1953 coup central to its ideology — and still does. The argument is simple and powerful: Iran tried democracy, the West destroyed it, so Western-style liberalism is a trap. Every Iranian student knows the word "coup" in the context of Mosaddegh before they know much else about their country's political history. When Iranian leaders call the US "the Great Satan," they are not speaking abstractly.

The hostage crisis (1979–1981). When Iranians seized the US embassy and held 52 Americans for 444 days, they found something unexpected inside: a CIA station with files confirming the US role in 1953. The coup wasn't just ideologically useful anymore — it was documented. The hostage-takers called the embassy "the den of spies."

The nuclear program. Iran's nuclear program began under the Shah — with American support and technology transfers. After 1979, the Islamic Republic kept the program and the US declared it a proliferation threat. The same technology the US helped install became the justification for the sanctions regime that followed.

Today's ceasefire. The US-Iran ceasefire agreed to on April 7, 2026 follows the same structural logic as every negotiation since 1979: the US negotiates from a position of wanting Iran to act "normally" as a state actor, and Iran negotiates from a position of profound institutional distrust of American intentions that has a specific origin date. The gap between those frames is 1953.

The AIOC becomes BP. The Anglo-Iranian Oil Company rebranded to British Petroleum in 1954 — one year after the coup — and eventually became BP. The company whose nationalisation triggered the operation is still operating. Its history page does not mention Mosaddegh.

The Counterfactual: What if Mosaddegh Had Survived?

Iran in 1953 had a functioning constitutional monarchy, a secular civil society, and a democratically elected government. Had the coup failed, the Islamic Revolution becomes much less likely. What Middle East follows? Historians genuinely disagree — which makes it one of history's more tractable counterfactuals.

⚖️

The First CIA Regime Change — and Its Template

TPAJAX was the first time the CIA overthrew an elected government. It was followed by Guatemala (1954), Congo (1960), Chile (1973), and others. Each drew on lessons from 1953. The playbook — disinformation, paid proxies, manufactured chaos, military co-option — became standard.
