Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

This week's strongest convergence is that stronger tools do not erase human agency; they raise the premium on it. Cate Hall's idea of agency as seeing and acting on hidden degrees of freedom, Cedric Chin's argument that expertise is sensemaking under uncertainty, and Ethan Mollick's shift from co-intelligence to co-existence all point to the same operational rule: when execution gets cheaper, the scarce skill becomes choosing the frame, setting the constraints, and intervening at the right moment.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Event Note · Cedric Chin / Cate Hall · Cate Hall Q&A on Agency · 23-24 Jun 2026

Agency starts with noticing the door before you try to kick the wall down.

Cedric Chin's introduction to Cate Hall's agency framework lands because it is not motivational fluff. Hall defines agency as the capacity to both see and act on the degrees of freedom life offers, which means the bottleneck is often perception before courage. Most people do not need more effort first; they need a better map of what is actually movable.

That changes how to read stalled projects. The problem is often not laziness or lack of talent, but a hidden assumption about what is fixed: who can be contacted, which process can be bypassed, which version can be shipped, which conversation can be reopened. High-agency people are not always the strongest operators in the room. They are often just the first to notice that the room has more exits than it appears to.

“the capacity to both see and act on all of the degrees of freedom that life offers”Cate Hall, quoted by Cedric Chin, Commoncog

Once tools get stronger, the practical edge shifts upstream: the win is not “doing more” but seeing more options early enough to choose a better game.

Essay · Cedric Chin · Sensemaking as the Heart of Expertise · 12-15 Jun 2026

Expertise is a better sensemaking loop, not merely a faster execution engine.

Chin's deeper claim is that expertise compounds through sensemaking: the ability to fit noisy evidence into a workable frame, update that frame when reality changes, and learn from trial and error without paying the full cost of each mistake. That is why real operators look uncannily calm in ambiguous situations. They are not seeing less chaos; they are holding a better internal model of it.

This matters far beyond business. In markets, in management, and now in AI-assisted work, the person with the best sensemaking loop will often look slower at the start and much more decisive by the end. They spend less time reacting to every signal because they know which signals deserve a frame update and which are just noise.

Agency without sensemaking becomes impulsive. Sensemaking without agency becomes elegant passivity. The durable advantage is the combination.

Newsletter · Ethan Mollick · Co-Existence and the End of Co-Intelligence · 4 Jun 2026

As AI becomes more autonomous, human value migrates from output to judgment and contract-setting.

Mollick argues that the old model of co-intelligence, where the human stayed at the center of a back-and-forth chatbot exchange, is giving way to something harsher and more interesting. Autonomous systems now complete stretches of work on their own. That makes the human role less about typing the next sentence and more about deciding what the system is for, where it can act, and what counts as a good result.

His framing is useful because it avoids both hype and denial. AI is clearly capable enough to absorb more execution, but imperfect enough that its operator has to set the boundaries, read the failure modes, and step in at the right checkpoints. The future worker is not just an end-user. They are a governor of semi-autonomous systems.

When execution cheapens, the premium shifts to framing, thresholds, and veto power. Judgment becomes infrastructure.

Newsletter · Ethan Mollick · What it feels like to work with Mythos · 9 Jun 2026

Capability shocks feel delightful and unnerving because they collapse the gap between asking and happening.

Mollick's most useful description of next-generation models is emotional, not benchmark-driven: it is delightful because you ask and it happens, unnerving because you ask and it happens. That is the correct operator instinct. The new risk is not simply that models hallucinate; it is that they can now make enough real progress to tempt you into skipping supervision.

In practical terms, the better the tool, the more dangerous lazy delegation becomes. Competent systems increase the cost of sloppy intent. If your brief is fuzzy, your review is late, or your stop condition is weak, the machine can run a long way in the wrong direction before you notice.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · Agency + Sensemaking

Run a weekly hidden-doors review before you escalate effort.

Before you push harder on a stuck project, do a fast review aimed at degrees of freedom rather than output volume. The question is not “how do I work longer?” It is “what am I treating as fixed that might actually be movable?”

  • Name the false constraint. Write down the one assumption that makes the project feel boxed in.
  • List three alternate doors. New contact, narrower scope, changed sequencing, or different distribution all count.
  • Choose one low-status move. Send the awkward email, ask the naive question, or ship the smaller draft.
  • Review what changed. Agency improves when you notice that reality often yields after one concrete probe.

Effort is expensive. Reframing is cheap. Run the cheaper lever first.

Practice · Working With AI

Separate map-making from button-pressing when you delegate to machines.

The cleanest way to use stronger AI is to keep humans responsible for the map and let the machine handle as much of the route as it safely can. That means your best leverage is in problem framing, acceptance criteria, and review checkpoints, not in micromanaging every intermediate step.

  • Write the win condition first. Define what success must include and what failure must never include.
  • Specify stop-losses. Name the triggers that should force review before the system continues.
  • Audit the first long run. Early autonomy needs more frequent checkpoints than mature workflows.
  • Capture the good path. When a run works, save the framing and constraints, not just the final output.

The goal is not constant supervision. It is intelligently placed supervision.

04 On the Horizon
Work

Hiring will tilt toward framing ability, not just raw production speed.

As more execution gets automated, the employees who stand out will be the ones who define problems cleanly, detect edge cases early, and rescue drifting workflows before they become expensive.

AI

The premium layer in agent products will be governance, not just cleverness.

Expect the best systems to compete on checkpoint design, memory quality, auditability, and controllable autonomy. Raw capability will matter, but operator trust will decide production usage.

Finance

In markets, better tools will widen the gap between fast actors and clear thinkers.

Automation can compress research and execution loops, but it also amplifies bad framing. The traders who benefit most will be the ones with explicit invalidation logic and disciplined review, not the ones who simply push faster.

Culture

Low-status experimentation will get more valuable as polished output gets cheaper.

When everyone can produce competent work quickly, the uncommon move becomes trying odd doors early, tolerating visible roughness, and learning in public before the playbook is obvious.
