Alpha — Stop Clusters & Magnet Zones

Tuesday, 17 November 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue 7 11 min read
TL;DR

Stop clusters are the bands of stop-loss and breakout orders that rest beyond obvious swing highs and lows, and they matter because every touched stop turns into a market order, which makes them fuel that price tends to revisit. The practical rule is to map pools on the higher timeframes, then trade the return back inside the range after a sweep rather than the touch itself. BTC's weekend drop to about $62,000 followed by a failed push back to $65,000 is a clean recent case, and it also shows why sources disagree on the exact level.

§01
What It Is
Where the resting stops sit, and why price goes back for them
A stop cluster is a band of resting stop-loss and breakout orders, usually just beyond an obvious swing high or low, that price tends to visit because those orders are latent liquidity.

A stop cluster is a band of price where many stop-loss and breakout orders rest together, usually just beyond an obvious swing high or swing low. The stops are not passive. Once one is touched it becomes a market order, so the cluster is latent liquidity sitting in the book, waiting for the moment someone needs size filled against it. My position is that the cluster matters less as a line on the chart than as fuel the market returns to, and the sweep is the moment that fuel gets burned.

Two definitions get blurred and it is worth separating them. ICT-style writing calls the resting stops and pending breakout orders a liquidity zone, and treats the event of those orders being taken as the sweep. Crypto perpetuals add a second layer: liquidation levels where the exchange forcibly closes leveraged positions. The two often overlap on the chart, but they are not the same thing, because stops are chosen by traders and liquidations are forced. [Sources differ] on whether a pool should be drawn as a precise tick or as a band. ICT treats the exact level as the target, while most futures guides treat it as a zone.

BSL / SSLBuy-side liquidity sits above highs; sell-side liquidity sits below lows.

Remember If you only remember one thing: a stop cluster only becomes tradeable once price has actually taken it and closed back inside, so the sweep and the return are the signal and the line on the chart is just the map.
§02
Mechanics
From a visible swing to a taken pool
Each stop that gets touched fires as a market order, so a sweep through the pool supplies the counterparty a large seller or buyer needs and then price either returns inside or runs.

The mechanic runs in three steps, and the signal only appears at the third. Each step has a different job, and most losses come from acting on step one.

  1. Locate the pool. Mark swing highs and lows, equal highs and equal lows, and the prior day and prior week extremes. Where several of these sit within a few ticks of each other, the cluster is heavier, because more stops were placed around the same obvious price.
  2. Watch the take. A decisive print through the level moves the resting stops into the market as market orders. The take is fast, often a single wick or a short burst, and it is where the counterparty is supplied.
  3. Read the return. The sweep is only meaningful if price closes back inside the prior range. A close that holds outside the level tells you the take was a real breakout, not a sweep.
Cluster weight (working heuristic) = touches at the level × timeframe weight
This is not a published formula. Count how many times the level was tested, then give daily and weekly levels more weight than 5-minute levels. Use it to rank pools, not to forecast them.

Wyckoff's spring and upthrust describe the same event from inside a trading range, and the SMC mapping of them is a sell-side or buy-side sweep. This builds on the Sweep Orders issue from 14 November (Issue #6 in the archive), which covered the single market order that eats several levels. Today is the layer above that: where the resting orders were in the first place.

Read the diagram as a map of two different kinds of resting size. The buy-side pool sits above the highs and is filled by market buys; the sell-side pool sits below the lows and is filled by market sells. The liquidation band is a separate layer in crypto because it is forced, so its fills can run further and faster than a stop cluster would. The right-hand sweep is the one that matters, because the wick alone does not tell you whether the pool was taken for a return or a breakout.

§04
In Practice
WAIT FOR THE RETURN, NOT THE TOUCH
Wait for the close back inside the level after the sweep, and place your stop outside the sweep extreme rather than inside the pool.
1

Map the pools on the higher timeframe first

Mark the prior day and prior week extremes, plus any equal highs or lows that have formed over the last two weeks. A pool that has already been swept and not rebuilt should come off the chart.

2

Do not act on the touch

A print through the pool is the event, but it is not the entry. Wait for the first candle that closes with a clear rejection and a close back inside the prior range.

3

Stop outside the sweep, size from the stop

Place the stop beyond the sweep extreme, not inside the pool, and let that distance set the position size. Target the opposing pool, which gives you a defined reward rather than an open-ended one.

A weekend sweep below $62,000, a rebound to $64,000, and a failed push at $65,000.

The most recent clear instance runs from the weekend of 14 to 15 November. Bitcoin traded as low as about $62,000 while exchanges closed out roughly $2 billion of leveraged positions across crypto in a single 24-hour window. Sources give slightly different weekend lows, with one citing $61,946 to $61,956 and others rounding to about $62,000, and the retrieved sources do not carry exact timestamps for the print, so treat the time stamps as unknown. By Monday the price had stabilised above $64,000, pushed to about $65,400 within 30 minutes, then gave back nearly all of it. §07
Common Traps
WHERE MAGNET MAPS GO WRONG
Treating every swing as a magnet and then calling every wick a stop hunt, which makes the map unfalsifiable and the stop-placement advice useless.
1
Drawing a line at every swing

If every swing high is a magnet, nothing is a magnet, and the map becomes noise. Rank pools by touches and timeframe, and keep only the ones that have not been swept yet.

2
Trading the touch instead of the return

A wick through the pool feels like the signal, but it is only the event. Wait for the candle that closes back inside the range before you act.

3
Putting the stop inside the pool

Stops inside the cluster get taken in the sweep you were trying to trade. Put the stop beyond the sweep extreme and let the distance set your size.

4
Overhyped: the institutional-intent story

The mechanics are real, because stops become market orders when touched. The claim that institutions engineer moves to fill against them is an [Analyst Opinion] that none of the sources here support with order-level evidence. You observe where stops are likely to sit, not who placed them or why, and a narrative that calls every wick a hunt cannot be wrong.
