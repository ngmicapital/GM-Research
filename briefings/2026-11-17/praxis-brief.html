Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

Your spending baseline is set by the tribe you compare yourself to, not by what you earn, and three Morgan Housel pieces converge on that point from different angles. The essay Little Rules About Big Things treats wealth as the gap between ego and income and warns that people underestimate how much their tribe shapes their thinking. Long-Term Money reframes a parent's hard work as a goal that lets the next generation live in a way the earlier generation reads as spoiled, and a Morningstar Long View interview describes frugality outlasting a rise in income. Mark Manson's 2 November issue, You're Not as Different as You Think, points the same way, though only its title and date were verified for this brief. The practical move is to name your reference group and test large purchases against it before your next raise resets the baseline.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Essay · Morgan Housel · “Little Rules About Big Things” · Collaborative Fund · undated

Wealth Is the Gap Between Your Ego and Your Income, and Your Tribe Decides How Wide That Gap Stays

Housel’s short list of rules opens with a claim that reads like a joke until you test it: the only route to wealth runs through a gap between what you want to be seen as and what you actually earn. The essay’s first working rule is about ego, not arithmetic. A higher salary does not close that gap by itself, because the expectations attached to the salary tend to rise with it.

The second rule carries the mechanism. Housel writes that everyone belongs to a tribe and underestimates how much that tribe shapes their thinking. In practice the tribe becomes the reference point for what counts as a normal house, a normal holiday, or a normal school for the kids. Once that reference point moves up, spending follows it, and the gap closes from the expenditure side while income is still rising.

“The only way to build wealth is to have a gap between your ego and your income.��

Morgan Housel, “Little Rules About Big Things”, Collaborative Fund

Income sets the ceiling on what you can spend. The tribe sets the floor you feel you cannot go below without looking poorer, and that floor rises faster than most people’s pay.

Essay + Interview · Morgan Housel · “Long-Term Money” and The Long View · Collaborative Fund and Morningstar

Frugality Outlasts Income Because the Reference Point Lags Behind the Paycheque

In “Long-Term Money”, Housel describes a man who grew up with immigrant parents who worked in low-wage jobs, and who now sees his own daughter buying books that he once borrowed from the library. Housel’s reply is that the parents would probably have called this the goal. The grind of one generation is meant to buy a standard of living that the next generation treats as ordinary, and that ordinariness looks spoiled only from the vantage point of the earlier generation’s scarcity.

The Morningstar Long View conversation tells the same story from the other side. Housel admits that as a teenager he judged his own parents for living frugally after their incomes had grown substantially, and wanted a bigger house and a nicer car. He only understood the savings rate after his father retired and stopped earning. The frugal habit persisted long after the income that justified it had arrived, because the habit was anchored to a past reference point rather than to the new salary.

Both pieces describe a lag. The reference point is set in the years of scarcity and does not update when the money arrives, so the spending either stays too low or drifts upward with the people around you rather than with your actual goals.

Essay · Morgan Housel · “Very Bad Advice” · Collaborative Fund · 24 Jul 2025

Fear of Missing Out Is the Costly Habit, and Refusing It Is the Skill Most Investors Lack

Housel’s line that having no fear of missing out might be the most important investing skill sits inside the same tribe logic. FOMO is the feeling that your reference group is getting something you are not, and it is the emotional engine that turns a rising tribe into an expensive purchase. The essay on very bad advice makes the parallel point about competition: a rival can probably innovate and execute as well as you, so any talent you feel proud of should be tempered by the knowledge that others want to win just as badly.

The same essay adds a sharper observation about ego. Housel’s rule that it is easy to mistake “I’m good at this” for “others are bad at this” explains why people overrate their skills and overspend to match a version of themselves that the skill seems to justify. The fear and the self-flattery come from the same source: a comparison to a group you chose without noticing.

Manson’s 2 November issue, “You’re Not as Different as You Think”, approaches the same ground from the self-improvement side. Only its title and date were verified for this brief, so read it as a pointer to the same territory rather than as evidence for the claim.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · drawn from Housel’s reference-point essays

Name Your Reference Group Before You Set Your Budget

The ideas above share one practical move. Your reference group is the default benchmark for every spending decision, so the first task is to make it visible. Once it is written down, you can judge each purchase against the group you chose rather than the group that happened to be in your feed this week.

  • List the three people you benchmark against. Write their names or roles, then note two things you assume they spend on that you do not actually value.
  • Run the ego-gap check. Compare your spending growth over the past twelve months with your income growth. If spending rose faster, the gap is closing and the tribe is setting your floor.
  • Apply a 72-hour rule to FOMO purchases. Any purchase triggered by a peer’s post, trip or upgrade waits three days before you decide, and you record what prompted it.
  • Test every “I’m good at this” claim. Ask who you are comparing yourself to and whether that group is actually weaker, or whether you have simply chosen an easy comparison.

A budget built on a reference group you have not named will keep drifting toward whoever you last compared yourself with.

Practice · drawn from the Long View interview and “Long-Term Money”

Set Your Spending Baseline Now, Before the Next Raise Resets It

The lag described in the interview and the essay means the time to set a spending ceiling is before the money arrives. A raise is the moment the reference point is most likely to move, so decide in advance where the extra income will go.

  • Record your current monthly spend as one number. This is the baseline the next raise must not quietly inflate.
  • Send a fixed share of any raise to savings first. Choose the share before the raise lands, not afterwards.
  • Write down what each frugal habit protects. Name the time, optionality or security it buys, so the habit reads as a goal rather than a deprivation.
  • Review the baseline each quarter. Ask whether the people you compare with have moved, and whether your spending moved with them.
03 Tools & Resources
📝
Primary Source

“Little Rules About Big Things” — Morgan Housel

The short essay that holds the ego-income gap and the tribe-influence rules discussed above. It is compact enough to reread slowly, and each rule is worth testing against your own last three purchases. Available at collabfund.com/blog/little-rules-about-big-things/.

🎙️
Podcast / Newsletter

The Long View: “Don’t Let Your Expectations Grow Faster Than Your Income”

An interview with Morgan Housel in which he discusses financial contradictions and the habit of frugality outlasting a rise in income. The personal stories about his parents make the reference-point lag concrete. Available on Morningstar’s financial advisors pages.

✉️
Primary Source

“Long-Term Money” — Morgan Housel

A short Collaborative Fund essay on the goal of parents who work hard so that their children can live a life that looks spoiled by earlier standards. It is the clearest statement in this issue of why a standard of living is inherited rather than chosen. Available at collabfund.substack.com/p/long-term-money.

📘
Book

The Psychology of Money — Morgan Housel

Housel’s book-length treatment of how behaviour, rather than knowledge, drives financial outcomes. The reference-point and tribe ideas in this issue recur throughout it, so it is the natural next read for anyone who wants the full argument rather than the short essays.

🔍
Framework

The ego-income gap as a working test

A simple framework rather than a published source: compare spending growth with income growth each year and treat a closing gap as a warning. It turns Housel’s aphorism into a number you can check, which is the reason it is worth keeping after you close this issue.

04 On the Horizon
Finance

Liquidity events will reset the reference points of a whole cohort

Mollick notes that Anthropic’s IPO is likely to happen soon, which means a visible set of paper fortunes is about to enter the tribes of people working near AI. The lesson from Housel’s essays is that a reference group revalued overnight changes what ordinary people think is normal, so the ego-income gap for that cohort may widen quickly.

Mind

Social feeds turn the tribe into a stage

Housel’s description of social media as a place people go to perform rather than to communicate suggests that the reference group is increasingly a performance audience. If the tribe is a performance, FOMO becomes a constant background input, and the 72-hour rule becomes a more important habit rather than a one-off discipline.

Work

Competitors multiply when the tools are shared

Housel’s warning that rivals can probably innovate as well as you applies with more force when AI tools are available to everyone. The skill you feel proud of is easier to copy, so the ego-income check on your own skill claims matters more as the comparison group widens beyond your immediate peers.
