Driving Thesis

The strongest finding this cycle comes from §02: a systematic review and meta-analysis posted as a medRxiv preprint on 17 October 2026 pooled 16 trials and 380 participants, and found that training a muscle once, twice or three times a week made no clear difference to hypertrophy once weekly sets were equated. The pooled effect was g = -0.06 (95% CI -0.24 to 0.13), and the one-versus-three contrast was small and non-significant at g = -0.23 (95% CI -0.57 to 0.10). The authors rate the certainty as low to very low, and most trials measured muscle thickness rather than whole-muscle size, so this is a reason to plan weekly volume first, not a licence to ignore frequency.

Two other items from §01 and §03 add context without changing the programming picture. A new observational analysis reported that poorer sleep is associated with faster ageing in several organs across 247,697 adults, which argues for reading multi-marker biological-age panels by component rather than as one number. A new clinical trial report, whose primary paper we could not retrieve, found a ketogenic diet reduced liver fat more than Mediterranean or low-fat diets even when weight loss was similar. Treat that last claim as provisional until the trial design is confirmed.

Section 01
Longevity & Performance Science

The longevity news this cycle is mostly about measurement, not intervention. Arclight’s November survey states that no intervention has yet been shown to extend human lifespan, while the mouse rapamycin effect of roughly 10–20% has been seen repeatedly through the NIA programme; animal and human results are moving at very different speeds. For a practitioner tracking biomarkers, the practical question shifts from which compound to take toward which signal to trust. Two new observational studies point the same way: if ageing differs by organ, a single whole-body clock will hide the part that matters.

Poor sleep is linked to faster ageing in several organs across 247,697 adults
Obs · Cohort

A GeroScience paper published 24 November 2026 reports that poorer sleep tracks with accelerated organ-level ageing, measured across several organ systems in a very large adult sample. The organ-by-organ framing matters: an aggregate biological-age score can average out a signal that sits in one system. The primary abstract was not retrieved, so the effect sizes and adjustment set are unverified here, and the association is observational. Mechanistically, the study is consistent with sleep loss acting on inflammation and metabolic regulation, but that link is an inference, not something this paper tested directly.

Source: GeroScience, 24 Nov 2026 (reported via Longevity Progress). Digest page
Breakfast timing is reported to track with lifespan in a large cohort
Obs · Cohort

A press report from 21 November describes a large study linking the time of day people usually eat breakfast to how long they live. We could not retrieve the primary paper, so sample size, follow-up and the adjustment for sleep timing and body composition are unknown. Treat this as a signal to watch rather than a protocol: eating-clock associations are heavily confounded by chronotype and shift patterns, and no interventional data was reported in the coverage we could access.

Source: ScienceAlert via The Morning, 21 Nov 2026. Topic page
⚡ Implication: Keep the biomarker panel organ-aware where you can: ask for component results rather than a single biological-age number, and log sleep timing alongside them. Do not change meal timing on the strength of an unverified press report; add it to the watchlist until the primary paper is confirmed.
Section 02
Training & Performance

⚡ Update: this cycle’s training item is a follow-up to the 20 November volume story. That item tested a sudden jump in weekly volume; this one asks whether splitting a fixed weekly volume across more sessions changes the result. It is a new question with a new evidence base, not a repeat.

⚡ Update: Hypertrophy is driven by weekly sets per muscle; session frequency adds little at equal volume
Preprint · SR/MA

Boutagy and McKean (medRxiv, posted 17 October 2026, not peer-reviewed) pooled 16 volume-equated trials with 380 participants, comparing one, two or three sessions per muscle per week at matched weekly sets. The pooled effect was g = -0.06 (95% CI -0.24 to 0.13). The one-versus-three contrast leaned toward higher frequency at g = -0.23 (95% CI -0.57 to 0.10), but the interval includes zero. Restricting to direct measures of muscle size gave g = -0.07, and duration and per-session volume did not moderate the effect. The authors rate certainty as low to very low, and most trials measured muscle thickness, which does not track whole-muscle growth reliably. Mechanistically, the proposed reading is that frequency mainly changes how a fixed volume is distributed over the week rather than adding a separate growth stimulus. That is a hypothesis the data are not strong enough to confirm.

Source: medRxiv preprint doi 10.64898/2026.10.07.26365015 (Boutagy & McKean, Univ. of the Sunshine Coast). Full text
A peer-reviewed overview of 137 reviews agrees: volume matters more than frequency for size
Meta · Overview of reviews

The ACSM Position Stand (Med Sci Sports Exerc 2026;58:851–872) synthesised 137 systematic reviews covering more than 30,000 participants. Resistance training clearly improved muscle size compared with no exercise. Load and weekly frequency affected strength gains but not hypertrophy, whereas the number of sets influenced both strength and hypertrophy. The position stand still recommends a default of two or three sessions per muscle per week as a programming convention, which is a scheduling choice rather than a proven growth advantage.

Source: Currier et al., Med Sci Sports Exerc 2026. Record
Integrate now
  • Set a weekly hard-set target per muscle first, then distribute it across sessions.
  • Use frequency as a scheduling lever when it helps adherence or keeps individual sessions manageable.
Caveats
  • Frequency comparisons here are low to very low certainty; a small effect favouring higher frequency is not excluded.
  • Effort (proximity to failure) was inconsistently reported across the trials, so the matched-volume comparison is imperfect.
⚡ Implication: For a PPL split, the evidence supports keeping your weekly sets per muscle as the main variable to progress. Do not restructure the week for hypertrophy alone; if a muscle is currently hit twice a week at the same total volume, the preprint gives no reason to expect a meaningful gain from moving it to three sessions.
Section 03
Supplements & Nutrition
A ketogenic diet reduced liver fat more than Mediterranean or low-fat diets, at similar weight loss
Trial · design unverified

A ScienceDaily report on 20 November 2026 describes a new clinical trial in which the ketogenic arm reduced liver fat more than the Mediterranean and low-fat arms, even though participants lost similar amounts of weight. This suggests the liver-fat effect is not simply a weight-loss effect. We could not confirm randomisation, sample size, the liver-fat measure (for example MRI-PDFF versus ultrasound) or the follow-up length, so this should be read as a provisional, press-level finding. The likely pathway is reduced hepatic carbohydrate and de novo lipogenesis under low carbohydrate intake, but that is a mechanistic inference, not something the report measured.

Source: ScienceDaily, Nutrition News, 20 Nov 2026 (primary paper not retrieved).
Higher diet-quality scores track with modestly lower mortality in probable fatty liver disease
Obs · UK Biobank cohort

A UK Biobank analysis in adults with probable metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease links higher food-based diet scores to modestly lower all-cause mortality. Diet components linked to the score included processed meat and refined grains, and the authors used E-values to test how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be. The effect is modest and observational. For a lifter whose liver enzymes or fat markers are borderline, the more useful clinical step is to ask a GP about a fibrosis score such as FIB-4, since fibrosis rather than steatosis appears to carry the prognostic weight.

Source: Frontiers in Nutrition 2026, DOI 10.3389/fnut.2026.2016904 (via Scienmag, 26 Nov 2026).
⚡ Implication: If you are considering a low-carbohydrate phase for liver-fat reasons, the new trial is encouraging but not yet decisive. Keep the diet-quality baseline you already track, and if you test, ask for a fibrosis-aware liver panel rather than fat-only imaging.
