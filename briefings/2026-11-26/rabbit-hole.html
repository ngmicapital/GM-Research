Why Hot Water Can Freeze First, and Why Nobody Agrees Why

The Mpemba effect has been reported for over two millennia, and physicists still disagree about its cause.

Hot water can sometimes freeze before cold water placed in the same freezer, a claim that sounds like a parlour trick until you learn that careful labs have reproduced it and then failed to reproduce it. The effect is named after Erasto Mpemba, a Tanzanian student whose ice-cream observation in the 1960s revived a idea that goes back to Aristotle, and the explanations still compete: evaporation, convection, dissolved gases, and supercooling.

TL;DR

Put two identical containers in a freezer, one with water at 100 °C and one at 35 °C, and the hotter one can, under some conditions, turn to ice first. This is the Mpemba effect. It has been written about since Aristotle and was pushed into the modern conversation by a schoolboy's ice-cream observation, but more than fifty years of experiments have not produced a single agreed mechanism. Some careful groups find it, others find nothing at all, and the disagreement tells you as much about how physics handles messy, sensitive systems as it does about water.

Section 01

The Story

The strange claim is almost embarrassingly simple: a hotter sample of water can reach ice before a colder one, even though it has further to fall. Intuition says this cannot happen, and for most of history that intuition was enough to dismiss the reports. Aristotle recorded the observation, and writers after him repeated it, but it stayed a curiosity until a Tanzanian student named Erasto Mpemba noticed in the 1960s that his hot ice-cream mix set faster than the cooled version when both went into the freezer. The mix was sugared milk, not pure water, and that detail turns out to matter a great deal.

Mpemba took the question to a physics teacher, Denis Osborne, and in 1969 the two published their result in Physics Education under the title “Cool?”. Their experiment used identical volumes in identical containers, with the hotter sample starting near boiling and the cooler one near body temperature. The hotter sample reached freezing first in their runs, and the paper's modest tone invited the rest of the world to test it. Each test that followed seemed to produce its own version of the result, and that is where the trouble started.

not a genuine physical effect and is a scientific fallacy
— Burridge & Linden, Scientific Reports, 2016

By 2016 the controversy had hardened into a dispute about definitions. Harry Burridge and Paul Linden at Cambridge argued that the popular phrasing, “hot water does not cool more quickly than cold”, was too vague to test, and that in their own well-controlled runs the effect disappeared once the setup was tightened. A reply by Katz, posted to arXiv the following year, insisted that the original claim was about freezing rather than cooling and that the effect deserved quantitative study. Both sides were looking at the same freezer and seeing different questions.

Section 02

The Mechanism

Start with what happens to a cooling tub of water. Heat leaves through the walls and the surface, and the rate depends on the temperature difference between the water and its surroundings. A hot sample has a bigger difference to start with, so it should lose heat faster at first and fall behind later. The Mpemba effect asks whether something else in the hot sample can overturn that ordering. Four candidates dominate the literature: evaporation, convection, dissolved gases, and supercooling, and each one has a plausible route to a head start.

Evaporation is the most intuitive. Hot water loses molecules from its surface faster, and that vapour carries energy away, which cools the remaining liquid and also shrinks its mass, so there is less water left to freeze. The trouble is that the mass lost in many experiments is small, and the effect sometimes appears with small temperature differences where evaporation barely differs. Convection offers a second route: the warm sample sets up stronger circulation currents, which carry heat to the surface, and the water density anomaly near 4 °C can shape how a column of water freezes from the top down. Both mechanisms are real, but neither is sufficient on its own.

The supercooling problem

Pure water can stay liquid below 0 °C and freeze only when a nucleation site forms. In one careful comparison, James Brownridge found that the hot vial froze first only when the cold vial supercooled further, while both vials cooled at the same rate. Supercooling is random, so it can make a single trial look like an effect and a repeat look like nothing.

Dissolved gases and dissolved solids add another layer. Warm water holds less nitrogen and oxygen than cold water, and heating also precipitates calcium and magnesium out of hard water as carbonate. Jeng's 2006 calculation suggested that the drop in hardness can change the freezing point more than the loss of dissolved air, perhaps by roughly an order of magnitude. Mpemba's own milk mixture was full of solutes, so his observation may have been driven by chemistry that a distilled-water experiment would never trigger. That is an unusually neat reason why the effect looks so inconsistent across labs.

The 2023 work from an experimental group in the Emerging Investigators series is a useful caution. It tested several liquids and found that the Mpemba pattern showed up in some of them, but the authors concluded that the exact explanation remained unclear. Their own data pointed to supercooling as a necessary ingredient in some samples without accounting for every observation. The lesson is methodological as much as physical: the result depends on uncontrolled details like the way samples couple to the freezer's air, and small differences in lids, containers, and thermometer placement can flip a result.

Section 03

The Connections

The definition problem. The Mpemba debate is a lesson in how a folk claim turns into a scientific one. Once “hot water freezes faster” became a slogan, each lab tested a slightly different version of it, and the failures and successes were hard to compare. Burridge and Linden's central complaint was that a phrase which can be reinterpreted after every experiment is not testable at all. Economists and epidemiologists know this pattern well: a broad claim survives every rebuttal by shifting its definition, and only a precise, pre-registered version can settle anything.

Why rival explanations keep surviving. Evaporation, convection, dissolved gases, and supercooling are each supported by some experiment and contradicted by another, which is exactly what you would expect if the effect is a sum of small contributions whose balance changes with the setup. A single mechanism would need to explain why the effect appears in some freezers and not others, and that has been the stumbling block for every proposed theory. Tracing the effect to one cause tends to be less productive than asking which conditions tip the balance.

The irony of the origin. The most famous version of the effect was never a controlled experiment on pure water. It was a student making dessert with sugared milk, noticing something his teacher had not expected, and asking a question that outlived both of them. Aristotle's version was an observation with no measurement at all. The effect has survived for two thousand years precisely because it sits at the boundary between domestic experience and laboratory control, where the everyday practice of cooking has the advantage of messiness and the lab has the advantage of rigour.

The live dimension. The name has travelled far beyond kitchens. Over the past decade physicists have found Mpemba-like behaviour in granular gases, spin glasses, colloids, and quantum systems, where a system started further from equilibrium can relax faster than one started closer to it. The 2026 review by Teza, Bechhoefer, Lasanta, Raz and Vucelja catalogues these relatives in detail. For a trader or a model builder, the transferable idea is a structural one: a starting point far from the eventual equilibrium can sometimes reach it sooner, but only when the dynamics have the right shape. That is an analogy, not a forecast, and the Mpemba history is a reminder that such analogies need the same careful controls as the freezer experiment.
