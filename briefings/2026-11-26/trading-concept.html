Alpha — Absorption

Thursday, 26 November 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue 62 11 min read
TL;DR

Absorption is what happens when aggressive market orders hit a price level and price refuses to follow, because passive limit size keeps refilling and takes every contract. It matters because it is the earliest visible sign that one side is losing a fight at a level, before price has confirmed anything. Practical takeaway: trade it only at an established level, wait for delta to flip and the aggressor to run dry, and treat a passive wall that breaks as your stop.

§01
What It Is
Effort with no result, at a price
Absorption is aggressive market orders being soaked up by passive limit orders, so price stalls at a level despite heavy volume.

Absorption is the cleanest order-flow signal for one reason: it is effort with no result, visible at a single price. When aggressive market orders keep hitting a level and price does not travel, a passive participant with resting limit orders is taking every contract on the other side. That is a statement about who is winning the fight at that level, and it is more useful than any single bar's colour.

The definition has three parts that must all be present. First, aggressive flow, meaning market orders lifting the ask or hitting the bid in size. Second, a passive wall that refreshes as it is consumed. Third, price that stalls or refuses to move through the level. Miss the refresh and you have a level being run over. Miss the stall and you have ordinary flow. This builds on the Bid/Ask Imbalance issue from 25 November (Issue #61): imbalance tells you which side is leaning, absorption tells you whether that lean is winning at a level.

AggressorThe side crossing the spread with market orders. It pays for immediacy.
Passive sideResting limit size that absorbs the aggressor and refills after each fill.
RefillNew size appearing at the same price as the old size is consumed. This is the tell.
ExhaustionThe aggressor running out of size, which is what turns absorption into a reversal.
Remember Absorption is aggression that failed. The attacker spent size, the defender kept refilling, and price stayed put, so read the level, not the print.
§02
Mechanics
From the print to the reversal trigger
Large resting orders refill as aggressors hit them, and the signal appears when delta is one-sided but price does not follow.

The mechanic works in three beats. Aggressive orders arrive at a level and fill against resting size. The resting size is not depleted, because it is replenished as fills occur or it was much larger to begin with. Price fails to take out the level, delta stays one-sided, and then the aggressive flow fades. Once it fades, the passive side is left holding inventory it wants to sell back at a better price, which is why the reversal often follows.

Two variants matter. Bullish, or selling, absorption happens at a low: aggressive sellers pour into the bid and passive buyers take every contract. Bearish, or buying, absorption happens at a high: aggressive buyers lift the offer and passive sellers refill it. Absorption also has to be separated from exhaustion, which looks similar on the surface but has a different cause.

Working read = aggressive volume at level ÷ net price progress through level
A descriptive ratio, not a standard indicator. High numerator with near-zero progress means absorption. Use it to rank candidate levels, never as a standalone signal.

The trigger is not the absorption itself. You wait for delta to flip or flatten, price to tick through the absorption level in the reversal direction, and the opposite side to start lifting or hitting. The stop sits beyond the level, because if the passive wall breaks, the thesis is dead.

§04
In Practice
Three rules for trading the wall
Never trade absorption on its own; wait for the aggressor to exhaust and price to reverse, and only at a level that matters.
1

Trade only at a level that already matters

Absorption in the middle of a range is noise, because nothing structural is being defended. Take it at a prior low, a high-volume node, VWAP or a value edge. If you cannot name the level before the print, skip it.

2

Wait for the aggressor to run dry

The absorption candle is not the entry. Enter on the first sign that the aggressive side has stopped, meaning delta flattens or flips and price ticks back through the level. Entering on the absorption itself means catching a knife that may still have size behind it.

3

Define the invalidation as the wall, not a percentage

Your stop belongs just beyond the absorption level, because a break of that level means the passive side has been consumed. A fixed percentage stop ignores the one thing the setup is built on. Size the position so that stop still fits your risk.

§07
Common Traps
Where the label gets overused
Calling every long wick or big print absorption, when the level, the refill and the delta flip were never confirmed.
1
Calling every big print absorption

A large print or a long wick is not absorption unless price stalls at a level while the other side keeps trading into it. Without a stall and a refill, you are looking at a fast move. Fix it by requiring all three conditions before you write the word.

2
Entering on the absorption candle

The absorption bar shows the defender winning so far, not that the fight is over. Wait for the aggressor to exhaust, delta to flatten or flip, and price to reclaim the level. This is the single most common way to lose on the setup.

3
Treating a static wall as a refilling one

A big bid that does not get hit is not absorption, because nothing is being absorbed. A wall that is hit and then refreshed is the signal. Check that size returns after each fill, or leave the level alone.

4
Honest callout: the label is overused

Absorption is Wyckoff's effort-versus-result law with footprint tooling attached, and many vendors sell it as a proprietary edge. The idea is useful, but you cannot see who is behind a resting bid, and the cleanest screenshots are selected after the fact. Treat it as a hypothesis to test at your levels, not as a standalone signal.
