The lake that exhaled a village in its sleep

A calm crater lake in Cameroon, a gas held in the dark, and the pipe that now bleeds it away

In August 1986 a calm crater lake in Cameroon killed about 1,700 people without a flame, a flood of lava or a visible explosion. The mechanism is a gas dissolved under roughly 208 metres of water, released when a small disturbance let the deep water rise and the pressure fall, and the fix turned out to be a pipe.

TL;DR

On the night of 21 August 1986, the villages around Lake Nyos in northwest Cameroon died in their sleep, and the lake gave almost no warning. The killer was carbon dioxide, stored for years in the lake's deep water and released in one runaway event. Understanding why the gas sat quietly for so long, what tipped it, and why it flowed down the valleys like water explains a hazard that had no precedent in the scientific record, and it leads straight into carbon storage and tail risk.

Section 01

The Story

On the night of 21 August 1986, the villages around Lake Nyos in the northwest of Cameroon went to sleep beside a lake that looked like any other. Hours later the people in the valleys below were dead, and the lake had offered almost nothing to see, hear or smell. There was no flame, no lava and no ash column. Witnesses recalled a rumble and, in some accounts, a sulphur smell. The first Science report on the disaster, written by George Kling and colleagues, put the toll at at least 1,700. The figure most often quoted today is 1,746, alongside thousands of cattle and countless birds and insects.

The gas did not stay in the lake. Carbon dioxide is about half again as heavy as air, so the cloud hugged the ground and poured downhill, tracking the valleys for many kilometres. Nyos, the settlement nearest the shore, was almost wiped out, with all but six of its residents lost. Further villages a dozen or more kilometres away were also hit, and the deaths were concentrated wherever the ground dipped and the cloud could pool. People who were awake and outdoors had a better chance than those asleep in low houses.

The sudden, catastrophic release of gas from Lake Nyos on 21 August 1986 caused the deaths of at least 1700 people in the northwest area of Cameroon, West Africa.
— George W. Kling et al., Science, 1987

Nobody had planned for this kind of event, and the warning had already passed unnoticed. Two years earlier, in August 1984, Lake Monoun, about 100 km away, had released a lethal gas burst that killed 37 people. Monoun was treated as a local oddity rather than as a template for a sister lake that could behave the same way. When Nyos went, scientists had to reconstruct the event from water samples, medical evidence and the shape of the dead landscape, because the cause had vanished into the air within hours.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The key is that the lake is layered, and the layers do not mix. Lake Nyos sits in an old volcanic crater, and its surface water turns over with the seasons while its bottom water does not. The deep layer is separated from the air above it, so the gas that accumulates there has no routine way out. Kling's team showed that the deepest water had been building up dissolved carbon dioxide over a long period, and that the concentration at the bottom was rising over time rather than being flushed away.

The lake in numbers

Depth of water over the trapped gas: about 208 m. The gas is held in solution, not in bubbles. Downhill reach of the cloud: roughly 25 km around the lake in some accounts. Released gas: estimates in the literature run from about 100,000–300,000 tonnes in widely repeated figures to about 1.6 million tonnes in other analyses, and the spread reflects how hard it is to measure a gas cloud after the fact. Lake Monoun, 1984: 37 dead.

Where does the gas come from? The team's conclusion was that the carbon dioxide was magmatic, rising from beneath the lake, but that there was no direct volcanic eruption involved. Gas dissolves more readily at higher pressure, which is why a sealed bottle of soda fizzes when opened. At the bottom of Nyos, the water column above the gas acts like that sealed cap. At depth, the water is cold and dense enough to keep a large quantity of carbon dioxide in solution, and the pressure stays high as long as nothing disturbs the layers above.

The trigger is the part that turns a slow reservoir into a sudden release. The leading explanation is a rockfall or landslide into the lake, which pushed a pocket of deep, gas-rich water upward. As that water rose, the pressure on it dropped, and dissolved carbon dioxide came out of solution as bubbles. Those bubbles made the rising column lighter still, which carried it higher and released more gas. This is a self-reinforcing siphon: each stage of ascent sets off the next. The surface bulged and a wave surged onto the shoreline, although estimates of its height vary widely between sources.

Why does so little gas kill so many people? Carbon dioxide does not poison in the way cyanide does. It displaces oxygen, and once it makes up more than about one-tenth of the air, it causes rapid loss of consciousness. Lower doses also distort the senses, and many survivors reported smelling sulphur that was not there. That detail misled early observers into thinking a volcanic blast had occurred, when the gas itself was responsible. The disaster was a physics problem with a biological end point, and it finished quickly.

Section 03

The Connections

Not a volcano, and not quite a bomb. The volcanic explanation was tempting, because the region is volcanic, witnesses heard a bang and smelled sulphur. But the Kling team found no sign of direct volcanic activity. The deep water was not unusually warm, the bottom sediments were undisturbed, and there was very little sulphur in the water, unlike after a normal eruption. The competing stories failed because they treated the event as a single bang. The more accurate term is a limnic eruption, a release in which gas rather than magma or rock does the work.

The same physics in an ordinary glass. The reason Nyos matters beyond Cameroon is that the physics is ordinary. Solubility rises with pressure, and gas that is held under a cap can be released by a sudden loss of that cap. That is the same logic engineers use for geological carbon storage, where carbon dioxide is pumped underground and must stay there for centuries. The Sleipner project off Norway, which has injected carbon dioxide under the seabed since the mid-1990s, is monitored for leaks with the same question in mind: what happens when the pressure changes?

Lake Kivu, and the lakes nobody has named yet. After Nyos, geoscientists checked other African lakes. Lake Kivu, on the Rwanda and Democratic Republic of Congo border, holds much more gas than Nyos does, although at lower concentrations, and geological evidence shows it has erupted in the past. The lesson is that hazard depends on the reservoir below the surface, not on how the water looks. Kivu is also being tapped for methane to generate power, so the same deep water is both a resource and a risk, which is a tension the Nyos story makes unavoidable.

The modern parallel: calm surfaces and tail risk. A calm lake, like a quiet year in a backtest or a leveraged position that looks stable, can hide a large store of pressure that grows where nothing is visible. The trigger can be small, and the release can be nonlinear, so the damage arrives all at once. The fix at Nyos was not a better prediction. A French team led by the physicist Michel Halbwachs spent years installing degassing pipes that slowly bleed the gas out of the deep water, so the pressure is released in controlled stages. That approach is closer to managed deleveraging than to forecasting, and it is worth remembering whenever a risk model reports that nothing is happening.
