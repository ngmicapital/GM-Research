Treasury Bars U.S. Banks From Moving Funds for MEXC Global Over $16.7bn in Illicit Flows

"[paraphrased] FinCEN designated MEXC Global a financial institution of primary money-laundering concern, citing Russia-linked illicit finance." FinCEN, Section 9714 order — Federal Register, 10 Nov 2026 (paraphrased)

On 6 November FinCEN issued an order under Section 9714 of the Combating Russian Money Laundering Act. It bars covered U.S. financial institutions from transmitting funds, including convertible virtual currency, to or from MEXC Global. FinCEN found the exchange facilitated roughly $16.7 billion in transactions linked to illicit activity between May 2021 and May 2026, spanning sanctions evasion involving Russia and Iran, North Korea-benefiting transfers and cybercrime.

The order takes effect 30 days after Federal Register publication. Measured from the 10 November publication date, the cut-off lands on 10 December 2026. It reaches MEXC through the U.S. banks and intermediaries that carry its flows, not through a direct penalty against the exchange.

📎 Historical Precedent: FinCEN’s January 2023 Section 9714 order against crypto exchange Bitzlato used the same transmittal-ban mechanism.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Lowenstein Sandler’s weekly Crypto Brief reports the order as a transmittal ban, so the exposure sits with counterparties that have U.S. banking nexus rather than with MEXC alone. The Brief’s read: the trigger is the transmission itself, so screening should start now and not wait for the 10 December effective date. Banks and payment processors that wait will be the ones caught mid-flow.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Section 9714 orders are blunt. A designated venue can migrate to new entities or new rails, and the order’s bite depends on U.S. banks over-complying rather than on the designation itself. The Brief’s read: the real test is whether MEXC restructures its U.S.-facing access within the 30-day window, not whether volume disappears.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Offshore venues with dollar on-ramps that depend on U.S. correspondent banks carry the most direct exposure, and compliance vendors gain demand for counterparty screening. The Brief’s read: regulated U.S. venues gain relative share of USD flows, while banks absorb the monitoring cost of the reach-through.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish COIN — U.S.-regulated venues gain share as USD flows move away from designated offshore exchanges.

🔴 Bearish Offshore exchange tokens and liquidity pools tied to MEXC order books — loss of USD on-ramps and rising delisting risk.

⚪ Neutral BK — custody and clearing exposure is limited, though screening costs rise.

📋 What To Do — Before 10 December
TraderRemove MEXC from any routing that touches a U.S. bank rail and reprice USD-funded MEXC liquidity before the cut-off.
CounselIdentify which U.S.-nexus counterparties would be covered institutions and review their wind-down terms now.
ComplianceAdd MEXC Global to transaction-monitoring blocklists and record the Section 9714 typologies in the risk assessment.
FounderIf your flows touch MEXC and a U.S. correspondent, get your processor’s exposure confirmed in writing this month.

OCC Finalizes GENIUS Act Stablecoin Rule, Effective 18 January 2027

"[paraphrased] The final rule keeps most of the February 2026 proposal’s framework but changes the reserve, redemption and capital requirements." OCC final rule, as summarized by Mayer Brown — 4 Nov 2026 (paraphrased)

On 21 October the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency issued a final rule implementing the GENIUS Act for payment stablecoin activity under its jurisdiction. The rule adds a new 12 C.F.R. Part 15. According to Mayer Brown, it keeps the February 2026 proposal’s architecture while changing reserve, redemption and capital terms in ways that matter to both U.S. and non-U.S. issuers. It takes effect on 18 January 2027.

The rule is one piece of a multi-agency build-out. The Federal Reserve, FDIC and NCUA have each proposed rules for the issuers they supervise, so issuers will face staggered regimes depending on their charter. The foreign-issuer registration route for coins circulating to U.S. persons remains a planning variable.

📎 Historical Precedent: OCC Interpretive Letter 1174 (January 2021) first confirmed that national banks could hold stablecoin reserves, the activity this rule now regulates expressly.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Mayer Brown’s analysis frames the reserve, redemption and capital changes as the points to plan around, since the framework itself is largely unchanged. The Brief’s read: OCC-supervised issuers move first with a fixed 18 January date, while state-qualified issuers face a fragmented 2027 until their own regulators finish. Winners are bank-subsidiary and federally chartered issuers.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The Brief’s read: a regulator setting capital and reserve terms before its peers finish invites charter shopping toward whichever supervisor offers the lightest terms. That undercuts the uniform framework the statute was built around, and the Fed, FDIC and NCUA rules will show whether the OCC terms become the benchmark or the outlier.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Bank-affiliated issuers and trust-charter entities gain a clear federal pathway, while non-bank issuers face higher capital and reserve costs. The 21 October rule also reframes the multi-issuer question that Circle’s share move in the 1 July issue raised. The Brief’s read: expect reserve demand to concentrate in short-dated Treasuries where the final text permits.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish CRCL — a defined federal pathway supports U.S.-regulated USD issuance.

🔴 Bearish Non-bank stablecoin issuers with thin capital buffers — higher capital and reserve costs under the final terms.

⚪ Neutral BK — trust and custody demand is steady, with no direct change to its position.

📋 What To Do — Before 18 January 2027
TraderRe-run stablecoin reserve-asset exposure against the revised reserve terms before the effective date.
CounselCompare the final OCC text with your issuer’s pathway and flag any reserve or redemption clause that conflicts with it.
ComplianceBuild a dated readiness checklist covering reserve attestations and redemption procedures.
FounderDecide now which regulator will supervise any planned stablecoin, since charter choice drives the capital position.

SEC Backs CFTC in Court Fight Over Single-Stock Perpetual Futures

"[paraphrased] Security futures remain subject to joint SEC and CFTC oversight, so classifying the products as security futures does not take them outside SEC jurisdiction." SEC amicus brief — filed 9 Nov 2026 (paraphrased)

On 9 November the SEC filed an amicus brief supporting the CFTC in litigation over perpetual futures tied to individual U.S. stocks. The plaintiff, the Capital Markets Alliance Association, argues these contracts should be regulated as security-based swaps rather than security futures. The SEC’s response is that the security-futures label keeps the products under joint SEC and CFTC oversight, so the classification does not remove them from its reach.

On 3 November a federal district court denied the plaintiff’s request for a temporary restraining order, finding insufficient evidence of irreparable harm. The court nonetheless found the plaintiff likely to succeed on its claim that the CFTC exceeded its statutory authority. The SEC’s review of the exchanges’ related rule changes is still pending, and the SEC declined to take a position on abrogating them.

📎 Historical Precedent: The Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000 created the security-futures category, and Dodd-Frank’s 2010 swap definitions created the security-based swap line this case now tests.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Lowenstein Sandler’s summary of the filings shows the SEC stepping in to defend the CFTC’s jurisdiction while avoiding the abrogation question. The Brief’s read: the classification decides which regulator’s rulebook governs U.S. stock-linked perpetuals, so the label is the case. The pending exchange rule review remains a second lever that could change the outcome without a merits ruling.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The Brief’s read: the plaintiff’s strongest point is that the district court already found a likely overreach by the CFTC, so an SEC brief in its favour does not settle the merits. A win for the CFTC on jurisdiction could still leave the products exposed to the exchange rule review.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Derivatives venues that list stock-linked perpetuals carry the litigation risk, and crypto-native platforms offering U.S. equity-linked products face the sharpest overhang. This is another CFTC jurisdictional front, following the agency’s July suit against Kentucky over prediction markets covered in the 2 July issue. The Brief’s read: if the security-based swap classification prevails, retail-access platforms face the tighter eligibility rules.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish CME — a CFTC-supervised venue with an established futures channel gains if the security-futures route holds.

🔴 Bearish Crypto-native perpetual venues with U.S. stock-linked listings — litigation and exchange-review overhang.

⚪ Neutral NDAQ — no direct position change while the SEC review of exchange rule filings is pending.

📋 What To Do — Before the Next Ruling
TraderMark stock-linked perpetual positions to a scenario where the security-futures classification is later reversed.
CounselTrack the pending SEC review of the exchanges’ rule changes as a separate route to abrogation.
ComplianceDocument which regulator’s rules your stock-linked products are being offered under until the classification is settled.
FounderAvoid launching new U.S. stock-linked perpetual products until the court resolves the authority question.

FinCEN Withdraws 2020 Travel-Rule Threshold Proposal as Banks Press for Digital-Asset AML Gaps

"[paraphrased] Any future change to the threshold would be subject to an additional opportunity for public comment." FinCEN and Federal Reserve Board withdrawal notice — Federal Register, 9 Nov 2026 (paraphrased)

On 6 November FinCEN and the Federal Reserve Board withdrew an October 2020 proposal. It would have cut the cross-border funds-transfer recordkeeping and Travel Rule threshold from $3,000 to $250, and would have clarified how the rule applies to convertible virtual currency and digital assets with legal tender status. The agencies cited the more than five years since issuance, developments in virtual currency markets and the need for further public input.

The $3,000 threshold stays in place, and existing Travel Rule obligations for qualifying crypto transactions are unaffected. Separately, the Bank Policy Institute wrote to Treasury on 6 November, arguing banks lack visibility into self-hosted wallets, mixers, decentralized exchanges and cross-chain bridges, and asking that DeFi operators and digital asset service providers face Bank Secrecy Act obligations.

📎 Historical Precedent: FinCEN’s May 2019 guidance on convertible virtual currency applied the Travel Rule to crypto transfers, the framework this withdrawal leaves intact.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Lowenstein Sandler reads the withdrawal as leaving the existing framework unchanged, with no new recordkeeping burden imposed. The Brief’s read: the near-term compliance cost of transfers falls, but the BPI letter signals that banks will push the next round toward DeFi and self-hosted wallet visibility, so the gap is moving rather than closing.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Crypto transfer and travel-rule vendors keep a stable $3,000 baseline, while bank correspondents keep their monitoring cost. The Brief’s read: banks are positioning themselves as the backstop for DeFi and wallet visibility, which would shift compliance spend toward bank-side monitoring tools.
