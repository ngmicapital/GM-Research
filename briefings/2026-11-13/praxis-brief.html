Long-term choices keep failing for one reason: the person who pays for them feels like a stranger, and that distance is measurable and can be narrowed. Hal Hershfield's group has quantified future self-continuity in a nationally representative sample, and it varies with age and education. Morgan Housel argues that comfort does not pass on the frugality and grit that hardship once forced into people, so the discipline has to be chosen deliberately. A 2025 study of AI-generated future selves found that one imagined future steers decisions, while several futures help people weigh trade-offs. The convergent move for this week is to make the future person concrete and to hold more than one future in view before deciding.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Journal · Hershfield, Brimhall & Kerbel · “Exploring the distribution and correlates of future self-continuity” · 2025

The person who pays for your long-term choices feels like a stranger, and that gap can be measured

Hershfield's group defines future self-continuity as the degree to which a person's identity overlaps with the person they expect to become. Their study in Judgment and Decision Making looked at a large, nationally representative sample and reported an average score of 68. The scores rose with age and with education, and the authors found no gender difference. The paper also points to earlier correlational and experimental work showing that higher overlap makes people more willing to accept present-day sacrifices.

The popular gloss is that the brain treats the future self like a stranger. The defensible claim is narrower and more useful: continuity is a variable, not a fixed trait. It moves with age, schooling, and how vividly the future person is rendered, which means it can be worked on.

Long-term money and long-term health fail for the same reason: the person who benefits has no face yet, so the present self keeps spending their share.

Newsletter · Morgan Housel · “Long-Term Money” · Apr 2026

Comfort does not transmit the discipline that hardship once forced into people

Housel opens with Adam Smith's account of Scottish mothers who had lost most of their children, then sets it against a modern grocery store and pharmacy that would astonish a visitor from that time. The visitor's verdict, he suggests, would be that today's people are spoiled rather than admirable. He then describes a man who felt ashamed that, as a college-educated office worker, he had never had to suffer the way his parents had, and who wondered whether his own children would learn frugality and grit from a comparatively easy life.

The essay's question is whether a comfortable environment can pass on the habits that made the comfort possible. Frugality and grit are learned through contact with scarcity, and that contact disappears one generation at a time. The argument is not nostalgia. The disposition has to be rebuilt on purpose, because circumstances no longer supply it.

Hardship used to be the default teacher. Now the teacher has to be chosen, and choosing it is itself a long-term decision that the present self will resist.

Research · Poonsiriwong, Hershfield et al. · UCLA Anderson Review · 2025

One imagined future steers the choice, so show several and let the trade-offs surface

A 2025 study by researchers at MIT, Stanford, UCLA Anderson, and collaborators used AI-generated future selves. The authors report that presenting a single future self can subtly steer decisions, while showing multiple futures helps people weigh trade-offs more thoughtfully. They also warn that the same design could be used to manipulate, for example by programming a future self to promote particular investments.

A separate randomised study of 92 people compared text, voice, and avatar versions of an AI future self with a neutral control. It reported reduced anxiety and higher future self-continuity after a brief interaction. The single study is small, but together with the Hershfield measurements it extends the research from a survey score to a tool people can use on demand.

Housel's comfortable heirs and Hershfield's stranger describe the same gap. The fix is shared too: make the beneficiary specific, then make the options plural so each cost is visible before the choice is made.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · drawn from Hershfield and the UCLA Anderson study

Write a dated letter from the person who will live with this week's trade-off

The research suggests the future person needs to be specific and the options need to be plural. A short letter forces both. Use it for one decision that pays off over years, such as a savings rate, a training block, or a contract you are about to sign.

  • Name the future person. Give them an age, a city, and one concrete constraint, so they are not a generic “future you”.
  • Write two futures. Describe the outcome of each option in a short paragraph, because a single imagined future steers the choice more than a comparison does.
  • Let the letter answer back. Write the reply from the future person, including what they would have wanted you to refuse.
  • Check it in four weeks. Reread the letter and mark which future your actions have actually moved toward.

The letter is cheap; its value comes from making the stranger specific enough to be owed something.

Practice · drawn from Housel's “Long-Term Money”

Schedule one chosen hardship each week so the discipline does not die with the hardship

Housel's point is that the habits of frugality and grit were supplied by circumstance and are not automatically inherited. If the environment no longer forces them, the reader has to create a small, recurring source of friction on purpose.

  • Name the lesson you want to keep. Write one sentence, such as “I can do without things I want”, so the purpose is clear.
  • Pick a cost you choose. Cook at home for a week, walk instead of riding, or skip a convenience you would normally buy.
  • Make it recurring, not heroic. A modest weekly hardship beats a dramatic one-off that you abandon by Thursday.
  • Tell someone who would notice. Naming a witness raises the cost of quietly skipping it.
03 Tools & Resources

Primary Source

Hershfield, Brimhall & Kerbel, Judgment and Decision Making (2025)

The paper that measured future self-continuity across a large, nationally representative sample and reported the average score and its correlates. Read it for the method and the caveats before repeating any headline number. DOI 10.1017/jdm.2024.23.

📚
Book

Hal Hershfield, Your Future Self: How to Make Tomorrow Better Today (2023)

A popular synthesis of the research programme behind this issue, written for general readers. It is the fastest way to see how the findings translate into saving, health, and everyday choices.

Book

Morgan Housel, The Psychology of Money (Harriman House, 2020)

Short chapters on how behaviour and emotion shape financial decisions. It supplies the background for Housel's argument that character, not arithmetic, drives long-term outcomes.

Housel's running essays, including “Long-Term Money” and “Optimism sounds like a sales pitch”. Worth the reader's time for the habit of testing ideas against lived experience rather than against a spreadsheet.

A readable account of the 2025 multi-institution study on AI-generated future selves. It is the place to read the authors' own caveat about manipulation, which matters for anyone considering these tools.

04 On the Horizon
AI

Future-self tools will need disclosure rules before they become mainstream

If one imagined future steers a decision, then whoever writes the future has real influence. The authors' own warning about programmed investment nudges points toward disclosure requirements for commercial future-self products, which is where this thesis meets regulation.

Finance

Long-horizon saving products will compete on how vividly they show the future self

The 68 average continuity score and its rise with age suggest that younger savers are the least connected to their later selves. Products that render a specific retirement self, and show several scenarios, are the natural next step for a thesis about making the beneficiary concrete.

Culture

The intergenerational comfort gap becomes a parenting and housing question

Housel's argument that young people locked out of their own homes are less likely to marry, have children, or feel invested in their community treats a missing future as a social problem. The same mechanism that weakens personal saving can weaken the shared commitments that long-term planning depends on.
