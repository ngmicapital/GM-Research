Alpha — Footprint Charts

Friday, 13 November 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue 7 11 min read
TL;DR

A footprint chart opens each candle and prints the bid volume against the ask volume at every price the bar traded through, so you see who was aggressive at each level instead of only the open, high, low and close. The practical takeaway: look for stacked imbalances and the point of control inside a bar, and only trust a single lopsided row once price confirms it. It builds on CVD from the 1 July Kyle’s Lambda issue, and the 12–13 November BTC flush is a live test of why the inside of the bar matters more than the wick.

§01
What It Is
The candle opened up to show who paid up at each price
A footprint chart breaks each candle into one row per traded price, showing the volume that hit the bid against the volume that lifted the ask.

A footprint chart is a candlestick with the inside exposed. Each bar is broken into rows, one per price it traded through, and every row carries two numbers: volume that executed at the bid (aggressive sellers hitting resting buyers) and volume that executed at the ask (aggressive buyers lifting resting sellers). My position: the candle tells you where price went, while the footprint tells you whether the move was pushed through with size or simply stepped into thin liquidity. That distinction is why it earns a place on a Wyckoff desk, even though it is only the trade tape arranged by price.

Three derived numbers sit on top of the raw cells. Delta is ask volume minus bid volume at a row, or summed across the bar. The point of control (POC) is the row with the highest total volume, which marks where the auction did most of its business inside that bar. Imbalances flag rows where one side outweighs the other by a set ratio, and they are compared diagonally: the ask at one price against the bid one tick below it, because that is the pairing an aggressive market buy actually consumes.

The footprint is the same data as CVD and volume profile, cut at a finer grain. CVD sums delta across time, a volume profile sums volume across price over a session, and the footprint keeps both dimensions inside a single bar. It is a microscope rather than a new signal. It builds on CVD from the 1 July Kyle’s Lambda issue: if you have already worked through why signed volume matters, this page is about where inside the bar that signed volume happened.

§02
Mechanics
From tagged prints to imbalance stacks and the POC
Each print is tagged as buyer- or seller-initiated, binned by price, and read as imbalances, stacks and a point of control inside the bar.

The mechanic runs on the trade tape, not the resting book. Each print is tagged by the venue as buyer-initiated (it lifted the offer) or seller-initiated (it hit the bid). The chart then bins those prints by price inside the bar. The signal lives in how aggression is distributed across rows, and the bar totals alone hide that.

1. Tag every print. The venue marks the aggressor side. Keep one feed for the whole read, because aggressor tagging and volume differ between venues and cross-venue totals will not reconcile.

2. Bin by price. Each tick the bar traded through gets a row holding its bid total and ask total.

3. Compare diagonally. An aggressive buyer lifting the offer at one row is consuming liquidity that sits one tick above the bid that was being hit below it. So an imbalance is the ask at one row set against the bid one tick lower.

4. Find the POC and the stacks. Three or more consecutive rows where one side wins by a wide ratio are a stack, and a stack shows sustained initiative. A single lopsided row is noise until price confirms it on the next bar.

5. Read the close against the delta. A bar that closes near its high on negative delta, or near its low on positive delta, is a divergence worth flagging.

Cell delta = Askp − Bidp
Bar delta = Σp (Askp − Bidp)
POC = argmaxp (Askp + Bidp)
Diagonal imbalance = Askp ÷ Bidp−1 tick, flagged at 3:1 or more across a stack
Bid and ask are traded volume by aggressor side at each row inside one bar. The 3:1 threshold is a common convention, not a standard. Calibrate it per market and timeframe.

§04
In Practice
STACKS, POC AND ABSORPTION AT THE EXTREMES
Trust stacked imbalances that price confirms on the next bar, never a single lopsided cell.
1

Read the stack, not the cell

Require three or more consecutive rows with a clear ratio on the same side before you call initiative. One lopsided row is often a single large print, while a stack shows sustained aggression across the bar.

2

Ask whether price leaves the POC

Price that moves away from the POC through thin rows is leaving liquidity behind. Price that keeps returning to a POC and holding there is accepting a new fair value, which changes how you read the next push.

3

Test absorption at the extremes

Heavy ask volume at a session low, with price failing to extend lower on the following bar, is the candidate absorption read. Wait for that confirmation, because the same heavy row in a cascade can be the start of the next leg.

§07
Common Traps
ONE ROW IS NOT A SIGNAL
Treating forced selling as absorption: heavy bid volume in a liquidation cascade is aggression, not defence.
1
Trading a single lopsided row

One cell with a 4:1 ratio is often one large print. Require a stack of rows and price confirmation on the next bar before you act on it.

2
Calling forced selling absorption

In a liquidation cascade the bid side fills with market sells, so heavy bid volume is aggression, not defence. Absorption needs price to stop extending while volume keeps arriving.

3
Mixing feeds across venues

Aggressor tags and volumes differ between exchanges, so a footprint from one venue will not match another. Pick one feed, name it, and compare only within it.

4
Tuning thresholds to hindsight

A 3:1 imbalance rule chosen after the fact will always look right on the chart you tuned it to. Set the threshold before the session and log whether it worked.

!
Overhyped: the footprint as a secret tool

The bid and ask split is an inference from exchange aggressor flags, not a direct view of intent, and it is built from the same tape that CVD already summarises. For most swing decisions a CVD line beside a clean candle read carries most of the same weight. The footprint earns its place when you need to see where inside one bar the fight happened.
