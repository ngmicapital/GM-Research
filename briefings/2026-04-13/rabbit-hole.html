The Man Who Poisoned the World Twice

13 April 2026 · Monday

Thomas Midgley Jr. invented both leaded gasoline and Freon — two products that poisoned the atmosphere at planetary scale — and was awarded every major chemistry honour while the damage accumulated unseen. The mechanism was not ignorance but suppression: his own employers funded the research that proved the danger, then required scientists to submit their findings for corporate approval before publication.

TL;DR

Between 1921 and 1930, a single mechanical engineer at General Motors invented two products that would go on to poison the global atmosphere in different but complementary ways. Tetraethyl lead — added to petrol to stop engine knock — deposited neurotoxic lead into every urban environment on Earth for seventy-five years, quietly reducing IQ and amplifying violent crime across entire generations. Freon, his "safe" refrigerant, drifted silently into the stratosphere and punched a hole in the ozone layer. Thomas Midgley Jr. died in 1944, strangled by a rope-and-pulley device he had built to lift himself out of bed, before either catastrophe was fully understood. He had already received the highest honours American chemistry could bestow. The story of how the damage was known, suppressed, and finally admitted is as disturbing as the damage itself.

Section 01

The Story: A Mechanical Engineer Remakes the Atmosphere

Thomas Midgley Jr. was not a chemist by training. He graduated from Cornell in 1911 with a degree in mechanical engineering and joined General Motors in 1916 as a junior researcher under the company's chief inventor, Charles Kettering. His first major assignment was to solve "engine knock" — the pinging rattle in petrol engines caused by fuel detonating too early in the cylinder. It was an unglamorous problem, but the automotive industry was expanding at a staggering rate and knock limited how far you could push an engine.

Midgley worked through hundreds of compounds over four years. His team found that ethanol — grain alcohol — was an effective antiknock agent. It was cheap, renewable, and non-toxic. It could not be patented. So they kept looking. On 9 December 1921, a small quantity of tetraethyl lead (TEL) was tested in a single-cylinder engine. The knock vanished. Lead had been a recognised poison since antiquity, but Midgley and Kettering pressed forward. In 1923, GM and Standard Oil of New Jersey formed the Ethyl Gasoline Corporation — the brand name deliberately chosen to avoid printing the word "lead" anywhere near the product.

"He had more impact on the atmosphere than any other single organism in earth's history." — J.R. McNeill, Something New Under the Sun: An Environmental History of the Twentieth-Century World (W.W. Norton, 2000)

By 1924, Midgley himself was already suffering from lead poisoning — documented in his own letters as early as January of that year. He took a month off work due to lead-fume illness. That October, workers at Standard Oil's TEL plant at the Bayway refinery in New Jersey began going mad. Of 49 men assigned to the building, 32 were hospitalised and 5 died, hallucinating and convulsing before the end. The plant became known as the Looney Gas Building. Standard Oil told The New York Times the workers "probably went insane because they worked too hard." At a press conference the same month, Midgley poured TEL over his hands and inhaled its vapours for sixty seconds in front of reporters, announcing he could do it every day "without getting any health problems whatsoever." He could not.

Nine years later, in 1930, Midgley did it again. Searching for a safe alternative to refrigerants like ammonia and sulphur dioxide (which genuinely had killed people), he developed dichlorodifluoromethane — Freon. At the annual meeting of the American Chemical Society, he inhaled a lungful of the colourless gas and used it to blow out a candle, demonstrating simultaneously that it was neither toxic nor flammable. The demonstration was a sensation. Within a decade, Freon was in every refrigerator, air conditioner, and aerosol can in America.

Between 1923 and 1941, Midgley was awarded every significant prize American chemistry offered: the Nichols Medal, the Perkin Medal, the Willard Gibbs Medal, and finally the Priestley Medal — the ACS's highest honour — in 1941. He served as president of the American Chemical Society and was elected to the National Academy of Sciences. When he contracted polio in 1940 and was left paralysed below the waist, he engineered a system of ropes and pulleys to lift himself from bed unaided. On 2 November 1944, he was found dead, entangled in the device. The coroner ruled it a suicide. He was 55. The ozone hole would not be discovered for another 41 years. The US would not ban leaded petrol for another 52.

Section 02

The Mechanism: How Two Invisible Poisons Work at Scale

Midgley's two inventions damaged the world through different mechanisms, but both shared a common feature: the damage was invisible, cumulative, and time-delayed — perfectly suited to corporate denial.

Lead operates neurotoxically on the developing brain. Tetraethyl lead is fat-soluble, meaning it crosses the blood-brain barrier with ease. In children under six, whose brains are still forming neural architecture, even low-level lead exposure disrupts the development of the prefrontal cortex — the region governing impulse control, executive function, and long-range planning. There is no known safe level. The lead from automobile exhaust settled onto soil and dust in cities worldwide, where children inhaled and ingested it constantly. It did not produce symptoms obvious enough to connect easily to a cause — just a quiet, statistical dimming.

1924 — BAYWAY PLANT DEATHS
Five workers die at Standard Oil's TEL facility in New Jersey. Thirty-two more are hospitalised with hallucinations and convulsions. Standard Oil blames overwork. New Jersey temporarily shuts the plant. The federal government ultimately rules in the industry's favour.
1925 — THE SUPPRESSED HEARINGS
The US Surgeon General convenes a public conference. Harvard's Alice Hamilton testifies: "There are thousands of things better than lead to put in gasoline." Yale's Yandell Henderson warns that 30 tons of lead per year would fall on New York's Fifth Avenue alone. A planned second day of hearings — specifically addressing non-lead alternatives — is cancelled. The resulting report finds "no good reason" to ban TEL. The Bureau of Mines research had required Ethyl Corporation's approval before any finding could be published.
1965 — CLAIR PATTERSON SOUNDS THE ALARM
Caltech geochemist Clair Patterson, while trying to date the age of the Earth using uranium-lead ratios, discovers his lab samples are contaminated with orders of magnitude more lead than natural deposits can explain. His landmark paper "Contaminated and Natural Lead Environments of Man" demonstrates that average Americans have blood lead levels far above any natural baseline — the atmospheric fallout of leaded exhaust. The lead industry cuts off his research funding and has him removed from a federal advisory panel.
1974 — THE OZONE WARNING
Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland publish in Nature: CFC molecules are stable enough at low altitude to drift into the stratosphere, where UV radiation breaks them apart, releasing chlorine atoms. Each chlorine atom can destroy tens of thousands of ozone molecules in a catalytic chain reaction. DuPont's board chair calls it "a science fiction tale… utter nonsense."
1985 — THE HOLE IS FOUND
British Antarctic Survey scientists Joe Farman, Brian Gardiner, and Jon Shanklin publish satellite measurements showing a massive seasonal depletion of stratospheric ozone over Antarctica — a hole as large as North America. By 1984, ozone above their monitoring station had fallen to roughly two-thirds of earlier levels. The finding triggers the Montreal Protocol (1987), the first international treaty to achieve near-universal ratification.
2021 — THE LAST TANK OF LEADED PETROL
Algeria exhausts its final stockpile of leaded gasoline. On 30 August 2021, the UN Environment Programme officially declares the worldwide end of leaded petrol use — a hundred years after Midgley's discovery. The UN estimates the phase-out saves $2.44 trillion per year and prevents over 1.2 million premature deaths annually.

The crime connection is perhaps the most counterintuitive finding. Economists Rick Nevin (2007) and Jessica Wolpaw Reyes (2007) independently found that childhood lead exposure — operating on a roughly 23-year lag between exposure and peak criminal-age behaviour — accounted for the majority of the surge in violent crime in the 1960s–70s and its dramatic fall in the 1990s. Nevin's cross-national analysis found gasoline lead use explained roughly 90% of the variation in US violent crime from 1964 to 1998. Remove the lead; a generation later, the crime rate falls. The mechanism is the prefrontal cortex: impaired impulse control at scale becomes a public-safety crisis.

Section 03

The Connections: What the Story Is Actually About

Midgley is often presented as a cautionary tale about unintended consequences — a well-meaning inventor who couldn't have known. The archive tells a different story.

The ethanol road not taken. Midgley's own team knew ethanol was an effective antiknock agent before they settled on lead. Kettering and Midgley publicly claimed in 1925 — at the Surgeon General's conference and to the press — that no alternatives to TEL existed. The claim was false, and they knew it. The choice of lead over ethanol was entirely about intellectual property: ethanol was too cheap to produce, could not be patented, and offered no competitive moat. The company that controlled the antiknock patent controlled the gasoline market. Hundreds of millions of people paid the cognitive price for that calculation.

The suppression machine. The Ethyl Corporation required the US Bureau of Mines to submit all research findings for corporate approval before publication. This was not unusual for the era — it was standard practice. What made it unusual was the scale: the Bureau was effectively the only institution conducting early TEL safety research, and its findings were filtered through the company being regulated. When Clair Patterson later proved the atmospheric contamination beyond doubt, the industry had him removed from government advisory panels. The mechanism for suppressing dangerous knowledge was not conspiracy — it was institutional routine.

"The lead industry's response to Patterson was not to dispute his science. It was to remove him from the rooms where science was translated into policy." — Summary of findings documented in Caltech Magazine and PNAS correspondence

The double performance. Both of Midgley's famous demonstrations — pouring TEL on his hands for reporters in October 1924, inhaling Freon before the ACS in 1930 — were acts of theatre performed by a man who knew more about the dangers than his audience. By 1924 he had already suffered documented lead poisoning and taken a month off work. The performances were not ignorance. They were reassurance manufactured for the public record, by someone with every reason to know better.

The poetic end. Midgley's death by his own mechanical device is irresistible as metaphor — a man whose life's work entangled the world in slow, invisible, mechanical harm, found strangled in his own ropes. But the more disturbing symmetry is temporal: he died celebrated, before a single award committee had reason to reconsider. The lead did its damage over the next fifty years. The ozone hole opened over the next forty. The institutions that honoured him never got the chance to feel the discomfort of revising their judgement. That discomfort belongs to us.

Further Down the Hole

🔬

Clair Patterson's War on Lead

Patterson's forty-year campaign against the lead industry is one of the great stories of scientific courage. His 1965 paper was followed by decades of harassment, defunding, and institutional exclusion — before his work finally drove the 1973 EPA phase-out. The 2014 documentary From the Big Bang to the Big Apple covers it in detail.

🌍

The Crime-Lead Hypothesis

Rick Nevin's 2007 paper in Environmental Research and Jessica Wolpaw Reyes's concurrent Amherst study independently found that leaded gasoline phase-outs predicted violent crime declines with a 20–23 year lag across nine countries. Kevin Drum's 2013 Mother Jones longform piece "America's Real Criminal Element: Lead" popularised the finding for general audiences.

🧊

Rowland, Molina, and the Ozone Fight

F. Sherwood Rowland and Mario Molina spent two decades being dismissed by industry before winning the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1995. Their story mirrors Patterson's: correct science, immediate corporate counter-attack, eventual vindication. Rowland's line: "What's the use of having developed a science well enough to make predictions if, in the end, all we're willing to do is stand around and wait for them to come true?"

⛽

Why Ethanol Lost in 1921

The suppression of ethanol as an antiknock agent in 1921 had consequences far beyond public health — it locked the global car fleet into petroleum dependency at the precise moment it might have pivoted. William Kovarik's research at Radford University documents the full history of the deliberate burial of the ethanol alternative.

🏭

The Montreal Protocol as Template

The 1987 Montreal Protocol, which phased out CFCs under near-universal ratification, is frequently cited as the most successful international environmental agreement ever negotiated — a template for what climate agreements could look like. The Science Museum's account of the ozone hole story traces how the British Antarctic Survey data moved from scientific paper to treaty in just two years.
