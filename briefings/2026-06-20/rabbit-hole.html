The Brothers Who Almost Owned All the Silver

How Nelson Bunker Hunt and his brother accumulated a third of the world's tradable silver — and what happened when the exchange changed the rules mid-game

Two Texas oilmen once accumulated a third of the world's tradable silver supply and nearly broke the US financial system on their way down. The Hunt brothers drove silver from $2 an ounce in 1973 to nearly $50 in January 1980 — then a single exchange rule change triggered a collapse so violent that a consortium of banks had to loan them $1.1 billion just to prevent broker failures from cascading into a wider crisis.

TL;DR

In the late 1970s, two sons of Texas oil tycoon H.L. Hunt quietly accumulated nearly 200 million ounces of silver — close to a third of the world's entire non-governmental supply — using a combination of physical bullion, futures contracts, and borrowed Saudi money. They drove the price from under $2 per ounce to nearly $50. When the COMEX futures exchange declared "liquidation only" trading in January 1980, cutting off new buyers, the price collapsed 50% in a single day in March, the Hunts owed over a billion dollars they didn't have, and a consortium of banks including Citibank and Bank of America had to step in with an emergency $1.1 billion loan to stop their broker from going under and taking parts of the financial system with it. The episode set the template for every "too big to fail" market rescue that followed — and remains the largest attempt to corner a commodity market in modern history.

Section 01

The Richest Men in America Start Buying Silver

In 1973, silver sold for less than $2 an ounce, and most serious investors wouldn't touch it. It was an industrial commodity — used for photography film, electrical contacts, silverware — not a financial instrument. But Nelson Bunker Hunt, the eldest surviving son of Texas oil legend H.L. Hunt, had a different view. He believed the US dollar was being systematically debased, that inflation was structurally embedded in the Nixon-era monetary system, and that hard assets — particularly silver — were deeply undervalued against the real supply of money. He started buying. Quietly. Through multiple brokers, so no single entity could see the full position.

When H.L. Hunt died in 1974, releasing a fortune estimated at $5 billion to his heirs, Bunker and his brother William Herbert Hunt accelerated. They were not subtle men — Bunker was famous for saying, "A billion dollars isn't what it used to be" — but they were methodical. Rather than simply buying futures contracts and rolling them forward as most speculators did, the Hunts did something unusual: they took physical delivery of the silver. Actual bars, warehoused in Switzerland and Dallas, chartered in their own name. This was not speculation in the conventional sense. It was accumulation with intent.

By 1979, the picture had grown considerably stranger. The Hunts had brought in Saudi partners — including Ali bin Mussalem and Mohammed Aboud al-Amoudi, associates of banker Khalid bin Mahfouz — and in July 1979 formally established the International Metals Investment Company (IMIC) in Bermuda to pool their purchasing power. The Saudi interest was partly strategic: there was talk, never confirmed, of lobbying to have silver recognised as a reserve asset alongside gold within the Saudi Arabian Monetary Authority. Whether or not that was real, the practical effect was the same — a well-capitalised consortium was buying every silver contract in sight and taking delivery of the metal.

A billion dollars isn't what it used to be.
— Nelson Bunker Hunt, attributed widely in press accounts of the 1980 crisis

By late 1979 the Hunt-Saudi consortium had accumulated an estimated 195 million troy ounces of silver — physical metal plus futures positions — close to one-third of the entire world's non-governmental supply. The silver price, which had started 1979 at around $6 per ounce, was in full vertical ascent. Between January 1979 and January 1980 it rose 485%, from $6.54 to over $38. At its peak on January 18, 1980, spot silver hit $49.45 per troy ounce on COMEX — and intraday futures briefly touched $50.35. Every photographer buying film, every dentist buying amalgam, every silversmith in the world was suddenly paying 25 times what they had paid seven years earlier. American households were melting down their silverware and selling it for cash.

The Family Fortune

H.L. Hunt — father of Bunker, Herbert, and Lamar — was considered one of the wealthiest Americans of the 20th century, with an oil fortune estimated at $5 billion at his death in 1974. His sons inherited not just money but an orientation toward hard assets and a profound distrust of paper money that dated to H.L.'s own experience of Depression-era bank failures.

Section 02

How You Corner a Market — and Why It Always Ends the Same Way

A commodity corner is one of the oldest financial manoeuvres in existence, and its logic is brutally simple. Futures markets exist so that producers (miners, farmers) can lock in prices in advance and so that consumers (manufacturers, refiners) can hedge their cost of inputs. In between, speculators provide liquidity. A corner works by acquiring so much of a commodity — or so many of the futures contracts requiring delivery of it — that anyone who has sold short a futures contract finds themselves unable to source the physical material to deliver. They have to come to you. You name the price.

The Hunt mechanism had a specific two-stage structure. First, they used futures contracts on margin — putting up only 10-20% of the contract value — to control vastly more silver than they could afford outright. Second, and critically, they demanded physical delivery on those contracts rather than rolling them forward. This was legally permitted but unusual, and it served a purpose: silver that was sitting in a Dallas vault or a Swiss depository couldn't be delivered by a short seller in New York. The physical market and the paper market were being separated. Every time the Hunts took delivery, they reduced the float of metal available to close out short positions.

The leverage made everything fragile. The Hunts used their accumulating silver holdings as collateral to borrow more money, which they used to buy more silver contracts. Their broker, Bache Halsey Stuart Shields, lent them money against silver bullion — then used the same silver as collateral to borrow from banks. A single chain of collateral ran from the COMEX trading floor through a broker to a network of US banks. When the price moved, the whole chain moved with it. The Hunts were, by 1980, not just large speculators but a systemic node in the US credit system.

The speculative frenzy in futures brought the country perilously close to financial chaos.
— Paul Volcker, Federal Reserve Chairman, testifying before the Senate Banking Committee, May 1980

The exchange's response came in two steps. On January 7, 1980, COMEX adopted "Silver Rule 7," cutting speculative position limits from 5,000 contracts to 2,000 and requiring any positions above that to be liquidated by February 18. Then on January 21, 1980 — the day silver peaked — COMEX went further: it declared "liquidation only" trading. You could sell your silver contracts. You could not buy new ones. The rule exempted "bona fide" commercial hedgers but targeted pure speculators. The effect was surgical and devastating: it eliminated the demand side of the market at a stroke. There were sellers. There were no buyers. The price had nowhere to go but down.

The Conflict of Interest

The Wall Street Journal later reported that members of the COMEX board held their own substantial short positions in silver futures at the time they voted on Silver Rule 7 and the "liquidation only" restriction. The rule that crashed the market benefited the people who wrote it. The Hunts, whatever else could be said about their scheme, were not wrong to feel that the game was being played with two sets of rules.

By March 1980, silver had fallen from its January peak to around $21. Then came the margin calls. On March 27, 1980 — now known as Silver Thursday — the Hunts failed to meet a margin call of approximately $100 million. The news broke into the market. Silver opened at $15.80 and closed at $10.80 — a 50% collapse in a single session. What had been $7 billion in paper assets had become a debt of over $1.7 billion. Bache, their broker, was hours from insolvency, having used the same collateral that was now worthless to support its own borrowings from banks. The Federal Reserve, the Treasury, and four congressional committees mobilised simultaneously.

Section 03

What the Silver Corner Actually Broke

The first bailout of the "too big to fail" era. To prevent Bache from failing — and to stop its failure from pulling down the banks that had lent against the same silver collateral — a consortium led by Citibank and Bank of America provided the Hunt brothers with a $1.1 billion emergency credit line, collateralised by their Texas oil fields, real estate, artwork, and a stable of thoroughbred racehorses. This is the template that would be rerun in 1998 with Long-Term Capital Management and in 2008 with Bear Stearns: a private position large enough to threaten public stability, rescued not because the position was sound but because unwinding it disorderly would hurt everyone else. The Fed's condition on the Hunts' loan was simple — they were barred from further commodity speculation. It was the price of the lifeline.

The Peru footnote. The most pointed legal consequence came not from the US government but from an unexpected plaintiff: Minpeco S.A., the mineral marketing company of the Peruvian government. Peru had sold silver forward — hedging its mining output — and watched those positions collapse as the Hunts drove the price up, then down. In August 1988, a federal civil jury found Nelson Bunker Hunt, William Herbert Hunt, and Lamar Hunt guilty of conspiring to corner the silver market and ordered them to pay damages that ultimately totalled over $130 million to Minpeco. The Peruvian government, a silver producer, had lost more than $150 million in the process. The Hunts filed for personal bankruptcy in September 1988, within days of the verdict, to avoid posting bond on appeal.

The CFTC Report

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission's 1985 investigation into the silver crisis ran to 716 pages. The SEC issued its own staff report in October 1982. Together they documented how a single large position — held across multiple legal entities and brokers — could escape regulatory detection until it was large enough to be unwindable. The structural fix was tighter position-limit reporting. The market lesson, slower to register: leverage against an illiquid underlying asset is always more dangerous than it looks.

The modern echo: GameStop and silver, 2021. In January 2021, as Reddit's r/WallStreetBets was driving GameStop to absurd heights, a faction within the community proposed a follow-up act: corner the silver market. The logic was identical to the Hunts' — accumulate enough to squeeze the shorts. Within days, the iShares Silver Trust ETF saw record inflows and spot silver climbed to an eight-year high above $30. It stalled there. The lesson the Reddit crowd learned, and the Hunts learned before them, is that silver is far larger and more distributed than a single stock. The global silver market has multiple sources — mines, recycling, industrial stockpiles — that can feed supply to a rising price over time. A squeeze can move the price, but holding the corner requires holding it indefinitely against the full weight of the world's secondary supply. The Hunts came closer than anyone since. They still failed.

The inflation hedge that became its own inflation. The Hunts' original motivation was not irrational. The 1970s saw genuine dollar debasement, genuine commodity inflation, genuine monetary instability. Silver was in fact undervalued in 1973 against the supply of money being printed. Their error was not in the thesis but in the mechanism: they tried to force a reckoning rather than benefit from one. The price they drove to $50 was not discovered by a market — it was manufactured by a corner. When the corner broke, the price collapsed not to its fair value but far below it, because every stop-loss and margin call triggered by the unwind hit simultaneously. The Hunts were right about inflation. They destroyed themselves trying to profit from being right.

The rule-change question that never goes away. The Hunts maintained for the rest of their lives that Silver Rule 7 and the "liquidation only" restriction were illegal interference — that the COMEX changed the rules mid-game when its own members were losing. Legal scholars have never fully settled this. The CFTC has broad emergency authority to impose position limits, and the Hunt position was genuinely destabilising. But the documented conflict of interest — board members with short positions voting on restrictions that benefited those positions — is real. Modern commodity market regulation, including CFTC position limit rules updated in 2013, still grapples with the same tension: how do you distinguish a large speculative position from a legitimate inflation hedge, and who gets to draw that line?

Further Down the Hole

📖
Beyond Greed — Stephen Fay (1982)

The definitive contemporary account of the Hunt brothers' silver scheme, written shortly after Silver Thursday. Fay had access to principals and documents that later investigators didn't. Still the clearest narrative of how the corner was constructed.

📄
The SEC Silver Crisis Report (1982)

The SEC staff report on the 1980 silver crisis, published in October 1982 and available via the SEC Historical Society. A rare case of a regulatory autopsy written close enough to the events to be genuinely revealing about how regulators saw the crisis unfold in real time.

📉
Commodity Corners in History

The Hunts weren't the first: Jay Gould's attempted gold corner in 1869 ("Black Friday") and the 1907 copper corner by F. Augustus Heinze both ended in crashes and contagion. Each one refined the regulatory response — but never eliminated the incentive to try again.

⚖️
Minpeco S.A. v. Hunt — The Case Files

Available via Justia (686 F. Supp. 420). The Peruvian government's civil lawsuit produced some of the most detailed documentation of the Hunts' trading structure, including the IMIC vehicle and the Saudi partnership. The verdict — $130M+ in damages — is remarkable for involving a foreign state as plaintiff against US commodity traders.

🔄
Long-Term Capital Management (1998)

The next time a private position was large enough to threaten systemic stability, the Fed again organised a private-sector bailout rather than a public one. LTCM's failure is often treated as a separate story, but the rescue architecture — consortium of banks, Fed as convener, trading restrictions as condition — was drawn directly from the Hunt playbook.
