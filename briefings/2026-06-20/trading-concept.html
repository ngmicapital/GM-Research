Alpha — Speed of Tape

Saturday · 20 June 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #55 11 MIN READ
TL;DR

Speed of tape is the velocity of the print stream — how many trades fire per second, not where price is heading; it tells you the urgency behind a move before the candle confirms it. Acceleration of prints marks the start of impulsive legs and breakouts; a sudden slowdown after a run is the tape running out of fuel — momentum exhaustion. Read pace first, direction second.

§01
What It Is
The velocity of the print stream
Speed of tape is how many trades print per second — the market's pulse rate, measuring urgency rather than direction.

Most traders read the tape for what prints — green or red, big or small. The edge is in how fast it prints. Speed of tape — tape pace, tape velocity — is the rate at which executions hit the time & sales feed: prints per second (or contracts per second) in a rolling window. It is the market's pulse rate. A flat chart and a racing tape are two different markets, and only one of them is about to move.

Direction tells you who is winning right now. Speed tells you how badly they want it. Fifty prints a second crossing the offer is a different animal from five — same green candle, completely different conviction. When pace surges, aggressors are lifting liquidity faster than it reloads and price is about to travel. When pace dies, the auction has gone to sleep and the prior move is unsupported.

This builds directly on Time & Sales (Alpha, 19 May 2026) — the print-by-print receipt of the tape. Speed of tape is that receipt with a stopwatch on it. It is also the microstructure root of Wyckoff's Effort vs Result (Alpha, 11 Jun 2026): pace is raw effort, and a fast tape that buys no new ground is the cleanest exhaustion tell on the screen.

Remember If you only remember one thing: pace is conviction. A fast tape says the move has fuel; a tape that suddenly goes quiet after a run says the fuel is gone — read the speed before you read the candle.
§02
Mechanics
Prints per second, and what moves the needle
Count executions in a short rolling window, then divide by the recent baseline — speed is meaningful only relative to its own average.

Mechanically, tape speed is just a counter over a clock. Build it in four moves:

1. Pick the unit. Prints (trades), contracts/coins (volume), or order events. Most desks watch prints/sec for pure pace and volume/sec when size matters. A modern Speed-of-Tape indicator lets you choose between volume, orders, or trades over a fixed interval — confirmed in Deepcharts' own documentation.

2. Pick the window. A rolling 1s, 5s or 10s bucket. Short windows are twitchy and instant; longer windows are smoother but laggier. Match the window to your hold time.

3. Normalise to a baseline. Raw prints/sec is noise. The signal is the ratio to the recent average (e.g. the trailing 10-minute mean). "Forty prints a second" means nothing until you know whether the baseline is eight or eighty.

4. Read the ratio. ~1× is normal. A push to 3–5× is acceleration — a leg is starting. A spike well above 5× that then collapses below 1× is the classic climax-then-exhaustion sequence.

TapeSpeed(t) = ( prints in window [t−w, t] ) / w  →  prints per second
Pace Ratio = TapeSpeed(t) / SMA( TapeSpeed , 10 min )
Read:  Ratio ≳ 3 → acceleration  ·  Ratio ≫ 5 then < 1 → climax + exhaustion
Swap "prints" for "contracts" to weight by size (volume velocity). The ratio, not the raw count, is the tradeable object.

Two flavours of the same gauge, and they answer different questions:

§03
Visual
Acceleration into a leg, deceleration into exhaustion
One leg, two pace regimes: prints accelerate as price breaks out, then thin to a trickle as the move tops — the tape decelerating while price stalls is the exhaustion signature.
Pace leads price. The prints/sec bars surge as aggressors lift the offer into the breakout, peak at the climax, then collapse — while price merely stalls. The tape going quiet after a run, not a red candle, is the first exhaustion tell.
§04
In Practice
Trade the pace, not just the price
The one rule: a level breaks on a fast tape and holds on a slow one. Trade the pace into the level, not the touch of it.
1

Pace-confirm breakouts

A breakout on a dull tape is a trap; a breakout on an accelerating tape has fuel. Wait for prints/sec to expand through the level before paying up. No expansion, no trade — the move can't sustain itself.

2

Fade the silence, not the high

Don't short the climax bar — short the quiet after it. When a fast one-sided tape suddenly drops below baseline while price stalls, the aggressors are spent. That deceleration is your trigger; the wick alone is not.

3

Pace + delta = conviction

Speed without direction is just noise. Pair tape speed with CVD / delta (Alpha, 19 + 29 May 2026): fast tape + rising delta = real demand; fast tape + flat delta = absorption — someone large is quietly fading the crowd.

4

Respect the dull tape

In a dull tape, mean-reversion beats momentum — fade the range edges and cut size. Most false breakouts happen precisely because pace never confirmed. The tape tells you which game is on; play that one.

Worked example — speed-confirmed long (BTC, 19–20 Jun, illustrative)
SetupPrice coiled $62.3k–$62.6k for hours on a dull tape (pace ≈ 1× baseline).
Trigger~23:00 Syd: prints/activity expanded as the $62.6k offer was lifted — pace confirmation through the range high.
Entry$62,750 on the expansion (not the first green candle).
Stop$62,300 — below the pre-breakout base. Risk = $450 (1R).
Target 1$63,200 (the stall/leg-high zone) → +$450 ≈ 1R; trail the remainder.
Exit logicPace collapsed below baseline into 00:00 Syd while price stalled $63.1k–$63.2k — deceleration = book it, don't chase.
Outcome~1R at the deceleration; the dead tape that followed validated the exit (no continuation).

Levels are real (CoinGecko); the prints/sec read is described qualitatively — exact pace needs a tape tool (Bookmap / Velo / Coinglass).

Timeframe fit: pure execution tool — 1s–1m tape for the trigger, 5m–15m for structure, 1h+ for bias. Speed of tape is useless as a swing signal; it lives in the seconds-to-minutes window.

Voices
From the 1910 ticker to the order-flow desk
Across a century, the same idea: the tape's pace is the market thinking out loud.

"The tape is like a moving picture film. Every minute of the day it is demonstrating whether supply or demand is the greater."

Richard D. Wyckoff (as "Rollo Tape") · Studies in Tape Reading · 1910

"I have been told that I am a good reader of the tape. But reading the tape like an expert did not save me."

§07
Common Traps
Where speed-readers blow up
The biggest mistake: treating a fast tape as a reason to chase, instead of asking who is getting filled.
1
Chasing the climax

A 5×+ pace spike feels like "get in now." It is usually the last buyers panicking in — the climax sits closer to the end of the move than the start. Buy the pace expansion, never the pace explosion.

2
Pace without context (delta-blind)

Fast tape alone is direction-agnostic. A furious bid-side tape that price won't follow is absorption — large passive size eating the aggressors. Always pair speed with delta/CVD or you'll buy the exact top a market maker is selling.

3
Window-shopping the noise

Too short a window (1s) makes every flurry look like a signal; too long (60s) and you miss the move. Pick a window that fits your hold time and normalise to baseline — raw prints/sec is meaningless without "compared to what."

4
Mistaking dull for safe

A dull tape isn't low-risk — it is low-information. Range chop is where overtrading and death-by-a-thousand-cuts happens. Less tape activity should mean less of your activity, not a hunt for phantom signals.

5
"Speed of tape" is not a holy grail

Vendors sell prints/sec indicators as edge-in-a-box. They aren't. Pace is context, not a system — Livermore himself said reading the tape "like an expert did not save me." In thin crypto books a single whale or a spoof-and-cancel can fake a pace burst. Treat tape speed as one input, sized and stop-protected like any other.
