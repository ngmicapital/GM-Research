Alpha — Liquidity Voids

Fri · 19 Jun 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #54 11 min read
TL;DR

A liquidity void is a price band where the order book is nearly empty — so a single aggressive order can travel many ticks with almost no resistance, printing a vertical move on tiny volume. They open when traders yank resting orders ahead of risk (data prints, level breaks, volatility spikes), and price almost always revisits the vacated zone once liquidity rebuilds. Practical takeaway: treat voids as caution zones, not levels — don't chase the flush through a blank book; fade the exhaustion once bids or offers re-liquify at the new extreme.

§01
What It Is
Definition & first principles
A liquidity void — an "air pocket" — is a stretch of price with almost no resting limit orders, so flow rips through it fast and the chart prints a vertical, low-volume move.

Forget the chart for a second and look at the book. Price doesn't move because of candles; it moves because incoming market orders consume resting limit orders, level by level. Where each price tick holds plenty of resting size, an aggressor gets absorbed and price grinds. Where the next several ticks are nearly empty, that same aggressor walks the book — one order clears many levels at once — and price covers ground in seconds on barely any traded volume. That empty stretch is the void.

Bookmap's order-flow desk frames it cleanly: an air pocket forms when "there's suddenly very little or no visible buying or selling interest" at certain levels, so "even small trades can trigger huge price moves" because "there aren't enough limit orders to slow things down." The key inversion most beginners miss: a void is the absence of liquidity, not a surge of conviction. Price can scream higher or lower with nobody actually pressing size — there's simply no one on the other side.

This builds directly on the resting-depth picture from Liquidity Heatmaps (Issue, 14 Jun) and Order-Book Resiliency (Issue, 5 Jun) — a heatmap shows you the walls; a void is the dark gap between them, and resiliency is how fast that gap refills after price tears through it.

§02
Mechanics
How a void moves price
Displacement per unit of flow is inversely proportional to depth — as resting size per tick collapses toward zero, the same order size moves price exponentially further.

Reduce it to one relationship. The distance price travels is the aggressor's size divided by how much resting depth sits in its path:

ΔP ≈ Q / D
Q = aggressor (market-order) size · D = resting depth per tick in the path. Hold Q fixed and let D → 0 (a void): ΔP blows up. A tiny order in an empty book moves price like a whale would in a deep one.

This is just Kyle's Lambda (Issue, 25 May) told locally: λ is the price impact per unit of order flow, and a void is a local spike in λ — a patch of the book where impact per contract is abnormally high. Same physics, zoomed in.

How voids open — the reflexive loop. Liquidity has an inverse relationship with volatility. As price accelerates, market makers and traders pull resting orders to avoid being run over, which thins the book, which makes the next order move price further, which scares off more liquidity. Bookmap's volatility-day breakdown gives the life cycle in four stages:

(1) Liquidity removal — passive orders vanish ahead of the move. (2) Spread expansion — bid/ask gaps widen as makers step back. (3) Stop cascades — a key level breaks, clustered stops fire and feed the move. (4) Re-liquification — after the flush, larger players rebuild size near the new extreme. That fourth stage is why price "typically revisits areas it moved through quickly": the vacated zone becomes structural support/resistance once orders refill it.

§04
In Practice
Trading the air pocket
The one rule: never trade inside the void — trade its edges. Entries belong at the walls that bound it, not in the blank middle where slippage and reversal risk both peak.
1

Trade the edges, not the middle

A void has no level to lean on. Position at the walls that bound it — the shelf it leaves or the magnet it points at — and treat everything in between as untradeable transit space.

2

Fade the flush, don't chase it

The vertical candle is the worst possible entry: max slippage, max reversal risk. Wait for re-liquification — bids or offers visibly reloading at the new extreme — then position for the revisit back into the vacated zone.

3

Map the magnet

A void points at the next pool of liquidity — a resting wall or a stop / liquidation cluster. That pool is your target, not a round number. Price wants to reach where the orders actually are.

4

Size to the spread, not the chart

When depth thins, spreads blow out. Base stops on liquidity zones rather than fixed-tick distances, and cut size so one air-pocket tick can't silently double the risk you signed up for.

§07
Common Traps
Where traders blow up
The single biggest mistake: treating the void as a level to trade against, when it's the one place on the chart with nothing to trade against.
1
Resting limits inside the void

Putting a bid (or offer) in the middle of a blank zone expecting a bounce. There's no liquidity there to defend it — price accelerates through, and you're filled at the worst tick right before the reversal happens at the wall, not in the gap.

2
Chasing the breakout candle

The vertical move looks like momentum to join. It's actually the moment of maximum slippage and maximum reversal risk — you're paying up into a vacuum that re-liquifies and snaps back. Enter on the reload, not the flush.

3
Reading "low OI" as "safe book"

Low open interest means less liquidation fuel, but it says nothing about resting depth. The two are different axes. A market can be low-leverage and hollow at once — which is precisely the setup that air-pockets toward a stop cluster.

4
Honest flag: "liquidity void" as ICT chart-gap jargon

In real microstructure, a void is observable live on the DOM / heatmap — a blank patch you can see before price gets there. The ICT/SMC repurposing of "liquidity void" usually means a 3-candle chart gap (essentially a fair-value gap) drawn after the fact and dressed up as an institutional secret. The live-book version is causal and tradeable; the retro-fitted rectangle is a description of what already happened. Use the book, not the post-hoc box.
