SEC Votes to Scrap Reg NMS Rule 611 — a Landmark Unlock for Tokenized US Stocks

"After two decades of Rule 611, it is high time that the Commission review its unintended consequences that have hindered — rather than enhanced — the long-term growth of our markets." Paul Atkins, Chairman, US Securities and Exchange Commission — SEC Press Release 2026-54, 11 June 2026

The SEC voted on 11 June to propose rescinding Rule 611 (the Order Protection Rule) and Rule 610(e) of Regulation NMS — the bedrock of US equity market structure since 2005. Rule 611 forces every order to execute at the best displayed price across all venues; Rule 610(e) bars venues from locking or crossing each other's quotes. Both are mechanically impossible for an automated market maker to satisfy, which is precisely why tokenized US equities have been unable to trade on decentralized venues. Strike the rules and that structural barrier disappears.

The Commission would fall back on a principles-based "best execution" regime under FINRA Rule 5310, applied at the broker level rather than the order level — a framework compatible with AMM and on-chain settlement. TD Cowen's Washington Research Group expects a final vote no earlier than Q1 2027 after the standard comment-and-reproposal cycle; Benchmark analysts have called it the single most consequential US crypto rule of the year.

The timing is not coincidental. It lands as Citigroup opens a blockchain venue for tokenized private-company shares and on-chain RWAs (ex-stablecoins) hit roughly $31.8bn. The SEC is clearing the rails before the cargo arrives.

📎 Historical Precedent: Reg NMS Rule 611 has anchored US equity microstructure since 2005; the last serious attempt to revisit fee/quote mechanics — the SEC's 2018 Transaction Fee Pilot — was vacated by the DC Circuit in 2020 (NYSE v. SEC), a reminder that market-structure deregulation invites litigation from incumbents.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

This is a market-structure rewrite wearing a tokenization costume. Rescinding the Order Protection Rule reshapes routing economics for every US equity — exchanges, wholesalers and ATSs included — and the tokenized-equity unlock is the headline beneficiary, not the only one. SIFMA has already issued a guarded statement, signalling that the sell-side will fight to preserve best-execution certainty. The Brief's read: the deregulatory intent is unambiguous under Atkins, but a 60-day comment window plus likely re-proposal means the on-chain unlock is a 2027 event, not a 2026 one — anyone trading the headline is trading a proposal, not a rule.

🔄 Contrarian Take

SIFMA and institutional execution desks argue Rule 611 protects retail investors from inferior fills and that scrapping it trades two decades of price-protection for an unproven AMM thesis. The Brief's read: the counter is real on equities, but order-protection was designed for a lit, fragmented exchange world — it was never engineered for continuous on-chain liquidity, and clinging to it simply exports tokenized-equity volume offshore to venues already running today.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Direct beneficiaries: tokenization rails and DeFi venues — Coinbase (COIN), Robinhood (HOOD), Kraken, Ondo, and the chains hosting tokenized equities (Solana, Ethereum). Citigroup's just-launched tokenized private-share platform validates the institutional pull. Losers: incumbent exchange order-protection moats — NYSE (ICE), Nasdaq (NDAQ) and wholesalers like Citadel Securities and Virtu (VIRT), whose routing advantages partly rest on Rule 611. The Brief's read: watch whether ICE/NDAQ lobby through SIFMA to narrow the rescission to a carve-out rather than a full repeal.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish COIN, HOOD, ONDO, SOL — tokenized-equity addressable market expands materially if AMMs can list US stocks onshore.

🔴 Bearish ICE, NDAQ, VIRT — order-protection-dependent routing moats erode; wholesaler economics face structural review.

⚪ Neutral Broad equities/SPX — net microstructure effect is contested and won't bind before 2027; no near-term index impact.

California's Digital Financial Assets Law Goes Live 1 July — a BitLicense-Style Cliff With $100k-a-Day Penalties

"Any person who engages in digital financial asset business activity … must submit a complete DFAL license application by July 1, 2026, to continue serving California residents." California DFPL guidance, per Goodwin client alert — "The California DFAL License Application Is Open", April 2026

On 1 July 2026, California's Digital Financial Assets Law (DFAL) takes effect: anyone engaged in "digital financial asset business activity" with a California resident — custody, exchange, issuance, stablecoin activity, or crypto kiosks — must hold a DFPI licence or have a complete application on file. Applications opened through the NMLS portal on 9 March 2026. The law is explicitly modelled on New York's BitLicense and was delayed a year (from July 2025) by AB 1934 to give the DFPI time to stand up the regime.

The teeth are sharp. Unlicensed activity carries civil penalties of up to $100,000 per day; licensee violations run to $20,000 per day or per incident. The DFPI also wields cease-and-desist, licence suspension, restitution and injunctive powers — and it is already using them, having extracted a $300,000 settlement from Coinme and a suspended $9.9m penalty against a kiosk operator in 2025. Applicants must show $100,000 in initial tangible net worth, an independent BSA/AML review, and a NIST-CSF-2.0-benchmarked security program.

California is the largest US state economy and the third major July-1 cliff this fortnight, alongside the EU's MiCA transition expiry and Australia's AFSL application deadline.

📎 Historical Precedent: New York's BitLicense (2015) is the direct template — a state-level conduct licence that drove an exodus of smaller operators ("the great Bitcoin exodus") while anchoring compliant incumbents; expect a similar California shake-out of marginal players.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The decisive nuance, per the Goodwin and Venable alerts, is the safe harbour: file a complete application by 1 July and you can keep operating while it is pending — miss it and you are exposed to $100k/day from 2 July. DFAL also stacks on top of, not in place of, California's Money Transmission Act, so single-licence thinking is a trap. The Brief's read: with federal market-structure law (CLARITY) still stuck in the Senate, states are filling the vacuum — and a California regime this punitive becomes a de facto national floor, because almost no serious platform can geofence out California residents.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Some founders argue federal preemption is coming — if CLARITY or a federal market-structure regime passes, state conduct licences like DFAL could be partially displaced, making July-1 compliance spend wasteful. The Brief's read: betting on Congress to bail you out before a hard state deadline 12 days away is not a compliance strategy; even maximalist federal bills preserve significant state consumer-protection authority.

🏗️ Industry Implications

National platforms (Coinbase, Kraken, Gemini, Circle) have the compliance infrastructure to clear DFAL; the squeeze lands on smaller exchanges, kiosk networks and DeFi front-ends that have treated US state licensing as optional. NMLS-based filing favours firms already multi-state-licensed for money transmission. The Brief's read: expect a wave of "we no longer serve California" notices from marginal operators in late June — and DFPI enforcement headlines in Q3 targeting those who neither applied nor exited.

🟢 Bullish COIN, compliant multi-state incumbents — regulatory moat widens as marginal competitors exit California.

🔴 Bearish Sub-scale exchanges, crypto-kiosk operators, unlicensed DeFi front-ends — $100k/day exposure or forced California exit.

⚪ Neutral USDC/RLUSD issuers — stablecoin activity is in scope but well-capitalised issuers absorb licensing cost without strategic impact.

Washington Takes the AI Federalism Fight to Court — the DOJ's Litigation Task Force Moves to Pre-Empt State Law

AI companies should "be free to innovate without cumbersome regulation." Pam Bondi, US Attorney General — DOJ internal memo establishing the AI Litigation Task Force, reported by CBS News, January 2026

With Congress having twice failed to pass a federal AI moratorium, the executive branch is litigating instead. The DOJ's AI Litigation Task Force — stood up in January 2026 under AG Pam Bondi's memo — is now actively in federal court, having intervened alongside xAI to challenge Colorado's AI Act on three theories: that state AI laws are illegal, unconstitutionally regulate interstate commerce (Commerce Clause), and are preempted by federal policy.

The litigation track runs in parallel with the December 2025 executive order's administrative levers: a Commerce Department evaluation cataloguing "onerous" state AI laws, an FCC proceeding to weigh a federal disclosure standard that would preempt conflicting state rules, and referrals to the Task Force. The next targets are obvious — New York's RAISE Act and California's SB 53 (Transparency in Frontier AI), both effective 1 January 2027, both imposing safety-disclosure and incident-reporting duties on frontier developers above revenue thresholds.

The contrast with Brussels is stark: as the EU centralises AI oversight through its (now-delayed) Act, the US is fragmenting — Washington fighting its own states in court.

📎 Historical Precedent: The Senate stripped a federal AI moratorium from the FY2025 reconciliation bill by a 99–1 vote, and again left it out of the FY2026 NDAA — Congress has repeatedly refused preemption, which is exactly why the fight has migrated to executive action and the courts.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per BakerHostetler's "federal–state AI showdown" analysis, the Task Force's Commerce Clause and dormant-preemption theories are untested as applied to AI transparency mandates, and an executive order "does not have the force of law" (Manatt) — agencies must still act, and impacted states will litigate back. The Brief's read: frontier developers cannot assume the courts will rescue them from the RAISE Act or SB 53 before 1 January 2027 — build for compliance with both, and treat the litigation as upside, not a plan.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The administration and xAI argue a 50-state patchwork is itself unconstitutional under the dormant Commerce Clause and will throttle US AI competitiveness against China. The Brief's read: the patchwork burden is genuine, but the cleaner fix is federal legislation, not selective DOJ litigation — and courts have historically been reluctant to find implied preemption absent a federal statutory scheme, which Congress has pointedly declined to enact.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Frontier labs above the revenue thresholds — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta, xAI — face a binary: comply with NY/CA disclosure-and-incident-reporting regimes, or bet on a DOJ courtroom win. Compliance vendors and AI-governance counsel are the structural winners regardless of outcome. The Brief's read: xAI's dual role — litigant and regulated party — makes it the bellwether; watch the Colorado docket for the first ruling on whether AI transparency mandates survive a Commerce Clause challenge.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish AI-governance/compliance software and law firms — demand rises whether state laws survive or federal preemption forces a new standard.

🔴 Bearish Frontier-model deployers facing dual-regime uncertainty — legal overhang and divergent state obligations raise the cost of US deployment.

⚪ Neutral Big-cap AI infra (NVDA, MSFT, GOOGL) — governance fight is a compliance-cost question, not a demand-destruction one.

Singapore's MAS Adds Bybit to Its Investor Alert List — a Warning Shot at the World's #2 Exchange

The Investor Alert List flags entities that "may be or may have been wrongly perceived as being licensed or authorised by MAS." Monetary Authority of Singapore — Investor Alert List description, entry added 17 June 2026

On 17 June 2026, the Monetary Authority of Singapore added Bybit Fintech Limited — the world's second-largest crypto exchange by volume — to its Investor Alert List. The List is a public warning tool, not a ban or scam designation: it flags entities that may be wrongly perceived as MAS-licensed. MAS gave no specific reason, but the effect is to confirm publicly that Bybit is not authorised to serve Singapore users, where the jurisdiction already sits among Bybit's "service-restricted" countries.

Bybit joins Binance (listed in 2021) on the roster. The timing cuts against Bybit's recent compliance momentum — it was removed from Malaysia's alert list in April 2026 after regulatory engagement — and underscores MAS's continued perimeter discipline even as it courts licensed, reserve-backed stablecoin issuers.

📎 Historical Precedent: MAS placed Binance on the same Investor Alert List in September 2021 and ordered it to stop serving Singapore residents — the listing presaged Binance's regional retreat, the template Bybit will want to avoid.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

An alert-list entry is a low-cost, high-signal MAS tool: no enforcement process, no fine, but a public flag that complicates banking relationships and institutional onboarding. Coming days before the EU's MiCA cliff — and on the heels of Binance's reported Greek MiCA rejection — it fits a global pattern of regulators tightening the perimeter around offshore exchanges that lean on "service-restricted" geofencing. The Brief's read: for Bybit this is reputational, not yet existential, but it raises the cost of any future Singapore licensing bid.

🏗️ Industry Implications

The listing advantages MAS-licensed players (Coinbase Singapore, Independent Reserve, StraitsX) at the margin in institutional and banking access, while reinforcing that geofencing alone no longer buys regulatory peace in APAC. The Brief's read: watch whether other APAC regulators echo the flag — coordinated alert-listing is becoming the soft-enforcement complement to hard licensing cliffs.

Weekly Rollup

Week Ending 19 June 2026

This week confirmed the central tension of US crypto-and-AI policy in 2026: a federal government deregulating hard while the states pull the other way. The SEC's vote to scrap Reg NMS Rule 611 — the Order Protection Rule that has defined US equity microstructure since 2005 — is the most consequential US securities-rule proposal of the year, engineered to clear tokenized equities onto on-chain venues. Yet on the same horizon, California's DFAL imposes a BitLicense-style state regime with $100k-a-day penalties, and the DOJ's AI Litigation Task Force is in court trying to pre-empt the very state AI laws (RAISE Act, SB 53) that states keep passing.

The connective thread is the 1 July cliff: California's DFAL deadline, the EU's MiCA transition expiry and Australia's AFSL lodgement deadline all converge in the same fortnight, while Singapore's MAS flags Bybit and the EU squeezes offshore exchanges. Licensing perimeters are hardening worldwide even as Washington loosens at the federal centre — the arbitrage is now between jurisdictions and between federal and state, not between regulated and unregulated.

Single Most Important Development

The SEC's proposed rescission of Reg NMS Rule 611/610(e) (11 June). It rewires the plumbing of US equity markets and removes the one structural barrier keeping tokenized US stocks off DeFi rails — a 2027 unlock that institutions are already building toward.
