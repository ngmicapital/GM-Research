Praxis · Intelligence Briefing · 11 June 2026

Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

As AI agents shift from answering questions to executing multi-step work, the scarce human skill flips from doing to judging: Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index finds 67% of AI's measured impact comes from how organisations redesign work, not individual usage, while METR clocks frontier task-horizons now doubling every ~4 months. The operator's edge is Mollick's two-iteration delegation rule, calibrated verification, and Cedric Chin's case-method route to the tacit judgment agents can't supply.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
AI & Work · Ethan Mollick · "Real AI Agents and Real Work" · One Useful Thing · Sep 2025

The Bottleneck Moved From Capability to Judgment — and Most Workflows Haven't Noticed

Mollick marks the threshold where AI stopped being a chatbot you prompt and became an agent that plans, executes multi-step tasks, and uses tools (code, search, analysis) with minimal supervision. Newer "thinking" models self-correct across long sequences, so the human is no longer course-correcting at every step. In his testing, Claude Sonnet 4.5 reproduced a sophisticated economics paper end-to-end — translating statistical code across languages autonomously. Human experts still narrowly beat the agent on 4–7 hour tasks, but they won on formatting, not accuracy.

The catch is what he calls the abundance problem: capability without judgment becomes a busywork multiplier. A single memo prompted the model to generate 17 unnecessary PowerPoint decks. The hard question is no longer "can the AI do this?" but "is this work worth doing at all?"

When execution becomes nearly free, the binding constraint becomes taste — knowing what deserves to be built. The agent can produce; only the operator can decide whether the output is signal or expensive noise.

Reporting · Microsoft Work Trend Index · "Agents, Human Agency & the Opportunity" · May 2026

The 67/32 Finding: Most of AI's Payoff Lives in How You Redesign Work, Not in the Model

Microsoft's 2026 index puts a number on something operators feel but rarely quantify: roughly 67% of AI's measured impact traces to organisational factors — redesigned workflows, quality standards, repeatable agent patterns — versus about 32% from individual usage. Active agents inside Microsoft 365 grew ~15× year over year; 66% of AI users say they now spend more time on high-value work, and 58% report producing work that was impossible a year earlier.

But the report's sharpest insight is a behavioural trap it calls the transformation paradox: 65% of workers fear falling behind without AI, yet 45% say it feels safer to keep their current goals, and only 13% are actually rewarded for redesign experiments with uncertain outcomes. The incentive structure quietly punishes the exact bets the technology rewards.

The model is not the moat; the redesign is. Two operators with identical tools diverge entirely on whether their organisation makes it safe to rebuild the process — or merely bolts an agent onto the old one.

Strategy · PwC · "No More Pyramids" / AI Agent Survey (300 execs) · 2026

The Org Chart Is Becoming an Hourglass — and It Threatens the Pipeline That Makes Judgment

PwC's data (79% of firms already adopting agents; 66% of adopters reporting measurable productivity value; 88% raising AI budgets) leads to a structural prediction. Knowledge work bends toward an hourglass: swelling junior and senior tiers, a lean orchestrating middle. Front-line work bends toward a diamond: agents absorb entry-level tasks while mid-level "orchestrators" expand to manage them. One experienced engineer, PwC notes, can now run teams of agents across every stage of a job.

The buried risk is a generational one. If agents eat the entry-level rungs, you also remove the apprenticeship that manufactures future experts. The very judgment that makes orchestration valuable is forged in the low-level reps we're now automating away.

Decision Science · Cedric Chin · "Our First Accelerated Expertise Course" · Commoncog · Feb 2026

If Judgment Is the Scarce Asset, It Can Be Built Faster Than the 10,000-Hour Myth Implies

Chin's answer to the apprenticeship problem: the slow part of expertise isn't procedure — it's perception, the trained ability to see a situation the way an expert sees it. Drawing on Naturalistic Decision Making, he argues you can target mental-model construction directly instead of waiting for thousands of repetitions to deposit it as a by-product.

Two mechanisms do the work. Cognitive Flexibility Theory uses case-based learning across many angles (his "Calibration Case Method") to build flexible representations; Cognitive Transformation Theory uses process feedback to reshape causal mental models and dismantle wrong ones. Tactical decision games exploit a useful quirk — the brain doesn't sharply distinguish a vividly imagined scenario from a lived one — so reps can be simulated, not just survived.

The skill agents can't hand you is the one worth compounding: adaptive judgment in novel situations. Chin's claim is that you don't have to wait a decade for it — you can engineer the cases that build it.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · The Delegation Protocol (after Mollick & the WTI redesign finding)

Run Agents Like a Manager, Not a User: The Two-Iteration Rule and the Rubric-First Habit

The default failure mode is treating an agent like a faster search box — prompt, accept, move on. The operator's move is to delegate deliberately and verify against a standard you set before you see the output, so the agent's confidence can't anchor your judgment.

  • Write the rubric first. Before the agent runs, note what a great answer must contain and how you'll falsify it. Mollick's experts lost to AI mainly on formatting — so judge substance against a pre-committed bar, not polish.
  • Cap corrections at two passes. Assign the task, give corrective feedback, re-run — once or twice. If quality plateaus, take it over. OpenAI's own figures behind this loop: roughly 40% faster completion and 60% cheaper, but only when you stop iterating on time.
  • Watch for the busywork multiplier. If the agent is generating volume (decks, drafts, variants) faster than you can evaluate it, you've automated production without automating discernment. Cut scope, don't admire output.
  • Redesign the task, not just the tool. The WTI's 67/32 split says the gain is in reshaping the workflow around intent and review — not bolting an agent onto yesterday's process and hoping.
Practice · Building the Judgment Agents Can't (after Chin & calibration research)

Train Perception, Not Just Procedure: A Weekly Loop to Compound the Scarce Skill

If orchestration is the new mid-career skill and judgment is what it runs on, the highest-leverage personal investment is accelerating your own expertise — deliberately, not by accident. A lightweight weekly loop:

  • Keep a calibration log. Before a decision (a trade, a hire, an agent's output you're about to ship), write your probability it's right. Score it later. Over weeks this turns vague confidence into a measurable, trainable instrument — the same edge that separates forecasters from pundits.
  • Use process feedback, not outcome feedback. Chin's point: a good result from a bad process teaches you nothing. Review why a call was right or wrong, not just whether it paid — this preserves your ability to sensemake in the next, different situation.
  • Run tactical decision games. Once a week, simulate a hard call you might face and reason it through to a decision before reality forces it. The brain banks imagined reps; you don't have to lose real money to practise.
  • Collect cases, not rules. Build a personal library of vivid, annotated examples across angles. Flexible expertise comes from many cases seen many ways — not from a tidy checklist that breaks on the first novel situation.

Verification is the orchestrator's core act, and verification is just calibration under another name. The person who can reliably tell a good agent output from a plausible-but-wrong one owns the part of the job that doesn't get automated.

04 On the Horizon
AI

Month-Long Autonomous Tasks Are a 2027 Question, Not a 2030 One

If METR's accelerated ~4-month doubling holds, agents reach month-long task horizons around 2027 — though the confidence intervals are wide and saturation effects are real. The practical implication for operators: the unit of work you delegate keeps getting larger, so the muscle to scope, decompose, and verify large delegations is the one to build now.

Work

The Apprenticeship Gap Becomes Visible

The hourglass and diamond both thin out entry-level work — the exact roles where judgment is traditionally forged. Expect the sharper firms to start treating "expertise manufacturing" as a deliberate design problem (rotations, case libraries, simulated reps) rather than assuming senior talent will keep appearing from a hollowed-out base.

Mind

Calibration Goes From Trader's Edge to General Literacy

When your job is verifying agent output, probabilistic, calibrated thinking stops being a niche skill for forecasters and traders and becomes table-stakes for knowledge work. The behavioural-finance toolkit — base rates, pre-mortems, separating process from outcome — generalises cleanly to "is this AI answer actually right?"

Accountability

The "Agent Boss" Liability Question Is Coming

86% of AI users in Microsoft's index say they "stay responsible for the thinking" — a comfortable claim until an agent's output causes real harm. Watch for governance, audit-trail, and liability frameworks that try to pin accountability for autonomous, multi-step agent work onto a named human orchestrator. The norms are being written now, mostly inside firms.
