Rabbit Hole · 23 April 2026
Economics · History

The most famous financial bubble in history — Dutch tulip mania — never actually crashed the Dutch economy, ruined few people, and was largely invented in a moralising 1841 Victorian bestseller that most modern economists still cite without checking. The real story is stranger: a credit market in flowers that never existed, settled by handshakes in taverns, unwound by a government compromise nobody enforced, remembered as the archetype of financial madness by generations of investors who had the wrong century, the wrong country, and the wrong facts.

TL;DR

Every finance textbook teaches the 1637 Dutch tulip mania as the archetypal financial bubble: ordinary people mortgaging their homes for flower bulbs, fortunes vanishing overnight, a national economy brought to its knees. Almost none of that is true. The accepted version descends almost entirely from a single 1841 Scottish bestseller — Charles Mackay's Extraordinary Popular Delusions — which relied on a German scholar who relied in turn on moralistic pamphlets written by Dutch Calvinists with an axe to grind. When historian Anne Goldgar actually went into the Amsterdam archives in the 2000s, she found fewer than 40 people who paid the famously extortionate prices. The Dutch economy shows no measurable slowdown in 1637. No one drowned themselves in a canal. The "crash" was a failed auction in Haarlem on 3 February 1637, and its effects were absorbed by a government compromise most merchants simply ignored. The bubble everyone cites was a morality play, not an economic event — and three centuries of investors have been learning the wrong lesson from it ever since.

Section 01

The Story: A Scottish Journalist and the Flowers He Never Saw

In 1841, a 27-year-old Scottish journalist named Charles Mackay published a three-volume work with an irresistible premise: a history of all the times large groups of people had gone collectively insane. Memoirs of Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds covered everything from the Crusades to alchemy to witch-hunts. Its most famous chapter was short — about twenty pages — and concerned a speculative frenzy two centuries earlier over flower bulbs in the Dutch Republic. Mackay described peasants trading farms for single tulips, merchants ruined overnight, and a panic so total it shook the foundations of one of Europe's richest economies.

The book was a sensation. It has never been out of print. It was carried in a famous 1930s anecdote by the investor Bernard Baruch, who credited it with helping him sidestep the 1929 crash. Financier Ron Chernow described Mackay's work as a perennial favourite on Wall Street. Every subsequent account of bubbles in popular economics — from J.K. Galbraith's Short History of Financial Euphoria to thousands of breathless finance blog posts about Bitcoin and dot-com stocks — retells Mackay's tulip story as established fact. It became the foundational parable of financial irrationality: the thing investors are supposed to remember so they don't repeat it.

There was one problem. Mackay had not done any original research. His account was a near-verbatim paraphrase of an earlier German scholar, Johann Beckmann, writing in the late 18th century. Beckmann, in turn, had not looked at notarial records, court filings, or merchant correspondence either. His sources were a small number of Dutch pamphlets printed in 1637 and 1638 — satirical dialogues with names like Samenspraeck tusschen Waermondt ende Gaergoedt ("A Dialogue Between True-Mouth and Greedy-Goods"). These pamphlets were not news reports. They were moralising Calvinist polemics, written to condemn speculation as a sin against the Protestant work ethic. Their descriptions of drowned merchants and ruined widows were rhetorical devices, not journalism.

So the story most of the financial world believes about the birth of financial irrationality was written by a Victorian who copied a German who copied a handful of angry Dutch moralists. It is hearsay through three languages and two centuries, and it has been repeated as empirical history for 180 years.

"Mackay plagiarized most of his descriptions from Beckmann, with a little literary embellishment. Beckmann in turn relied on pamphlets motivated by a moralistic attack against speculation."

— Peter Garber, Professor of Economics, Brown University, in Famous First Bubbles (2000)

The first serious dismantling of the tulip mania myth came from economists, not historians. In a series of papers in the late 1980s and his 2000 book Famous First Bubbles, Brown University economist Peter Garber examined the surviving price data and concluded that most of what Mackay claimed was either exaggerated or fabricated. But Garber's critique was still built on secondary sources. The person who finally went and looked at the actual evidence was a historian at King's College London named Anne Goldgar. She spent the better part of a decade reading court records, notarial archives, and merchant letters from seventeenth-century Amsterdam and Haarlem. Her 2007 book Tulipmania: Money, Honor, and Knowledge in the Dutch Golden Age is the definitive account, and it demolished almost every popular claim about the episode.

Section 02

The Mechanism: Windhandel, a Failed Auction, and a Government Shrug

To understand what actually happened, you have to understand the strange financial instrument tulip bulbs had become by 1636. Tulips were real — prized, exotic, and expensive — but almost no trading happened in actual flowers. The bulbs were underground for most of the year, invisible and unverifiable. What traders exchanged were notarised contracts promising delivery of a specified bulb at lifting-time in June, weeks after the winter trading season ended. The contracts were settled in taverns and guild-halls, paid for in small deposits, and changed hands multiple times before the bulb was ever lifted. The Dutch called this windhandel — literally "wind trade" — a market in promises rather than possessions. It was, in effect, an informal futures market operating without clearing houses, margin calls, or centralised price discovery.

The notorious prices — the 10,000-guilder Semper Augustus, the bulbs worth a canal house — were almost always contract prices, not transaction prices. Nobody paid 10,000 guilders in cash. Traders wrote IOUs for absurd sums on the bet that they could flip the paper before settlement. It was the 17th-century equivalent of options chains settled through gentlemen's agreements: the notional value of the contract was nothing like the actual capital flowing through the trade. Goldgar found that most buyers at the top of the market put down deposits of just a few percent. The rest was promises.

What Goldgar Actually Found in the Archives

Scale. Only 37 individuals across the entire Dutch Republic can be documented paying more than 300 guilders for a tulip bulb — roughly a skilled craftsman's annual wage. This is not a mass mania. It is a small, elite speculation community.

Participants. Nearly all buyers were already prosperous — merchants, physicians, apothecaries, skilled artisans. The trope of peasants mortgaging farms is absent from the records. Peasants had no access to the notarised contracts that made the market work.

Suicides and ruin. Goldgar found no documented case of a tulip-related suicide. She found a small number of bankruptcies but nothing approaching the scale Mackay described. Most disputes ended in refused payments and damaged reputations, not ruin.

Economic impact. Dutch GDP, trade volumes, tax revenues, and shipping manifests show no detectable downturn in 1637 or 1638. The Dutch Golden Age continued uninterrupted for another four decades.

The famous "crash" itself was not a crash in any modern sense. On 3 February 1637, a routine bulb auction in Haarlem was held during an outbreak of bubonic plague that had thinned the city's merchant population. Sellers offered common bulbs at their usual winter prices. No one bid. The auction was abandoned. Word spread to other towns that the buyers had not appeared, and within days the market in contracts stopped functioning — not because prices had collapsed, but because no one knew what prices were. The contracts were unenforceable without willing counterparties. Trading in windhandel simply froze.

What followed was less a crash than a bureaucratic mess. Across the Dutch Republic, thousands of contracts had been signed for June delivery at prices nobody wanted to honour. The buyers refused to pay. The sellers refused to accept reduced payment. Dutch courts, overwhelmed with suits, asked the provincial government of Holland for guidance. After months of paralysis, the government issued a compromise in April 1637: contracts signed before November 1636 were fully enforceable, contracts signed later could be settled for 10% of the agreed price. In practice, most traders ignored the ruling and negotiated privately, often walking away from contracts entirely because the amounts involved were small and the social cost of litigating was high. By the summer the issue had largely dissolved. The tulip trade itself continued. Bulbs were cultivated, sold, and written about for decades afterwards, as if nothing remarkable had happened — because, in economic terms, nothing remarkable had.

"Tulipmania did not cause serious economic problems for Holland, let alone the Dutch Republic as a whole, and it did not even seriously damage the tulip trade."

— Anne Goldgar, King's College London, Tulipmania (2007)
Section 03

The Connections: Why a False Story Became the Truest Thing in Finance

If the tulip mania was mostly invented, why did it become the most durable parable in financial history? The answer says something uncomfortable about what economics is actually for. The textbook story is useful not because it is accurate but because it flatters a particular theory of markets: the theory that bubbles are caused by ordinary people catching collective madness, that speculation is a moral failing, and that "the crowd" is the thing to be feared. This framing absolves institutions, regulators, and professional investors of responsibility. If manias are driven by peasants trading cows for flowers, then 1929 and 2008 and the dot-com crash become episodes of mass psychology rather than failures of credit creation, regulatory capture, or leveraged finance. Mackay's tulip is a comforting villain.

The actual Dutch episode tells a different and less flattering story: the top of the market was an elite phenomenon, driven not by mass hysteria but by a small group of wealthy participants using sophisticated credit instruments to speculate amongst themselves. The "crash" was a collapse of contract enforceability rather than a loss of real money. The event's aftermath was absorbed quietly because the losses were small, concentrated, and social rather than systemic. Strip away the 19th-century moralism and what remains is a recognisable modern story — not of peasants and flowers but of professionals writing paper they could not cover, a plague-driven liquidity event, and a regulator issuing guidance that most participants ignored. That version is useful but less fun. So it has never caught on.

The tulip mania story has a curious afterlife. It is invoked constantly during market euphorias — dot-coms, housing, crypto, NFTs — as a warning from history. And there is a warning in it, but not the one everyone thinks. The real lesson of the Dutch windhandel episode is that opaque credit markets without central counterparties can freeze suddenly when confidence fails, that legal systems struggle to untangle contracts written on implicit assumptions about future liquidity, and that the social fallout from broken promises can exceed the direct financial fallout. These are contemporary lessons. They apply to repo markets, to OTC derivatives, to shadow banking. Instead, the episode has been stripped of its specifics and repurposed as a vague fable about greed.

Mackay himself, ironically, provides the final twist. During the British Railway Mania of 1845–46 — a genuine, economically significant speculative bubble that wrecked thousands of middle-class savers and caused a real financial crisis — Mackay was one of its most enthusiastic cheerleaders. Writing as a journalist for the Glasgow Argus, he dismissed concerns about speculative excess and encouraged readers to buy in. When the Railway Mania collapsed, Mackay did not add a chapter to his book. Later editions of Extraordinary Popular Delusions still carried his confident account of Dutch tulips — the delusion he could identify — while omitting the one he had helped cause.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

The Real Cause of the "Crash": A Mosaic Virus

The most valuable tulips — including Semper Augustus — had their distinctive flame-patterned petals caused by a pathogen now known as tulip breaking virus. The virus weakens the bulb with each generation, making broken tulips progressively harder to propagate. This scarcity drove early prices, but also meant the most prized stocks were literally dying. By the 1640s, new horticultural techniques had reduced the rarity premium. The thing collectors thought was beauty was actually disease, and the asset class was biologically unsustainable from the start.

Windhandel: The Futures Market Before Futures Markets

Dutch tulip contracts of the 1630s were sophisticated financial instruments: standardised descriptions by variety and weight, notarised signatures, deposit structures, and secondary markets where contracts changed hands repeatedly before settlement. They pre-dated the formal futures markets of Osaka and Chicago by decades. Economists studying them have argued that the Dutch were not primitive speculators but inventors of a credit instrument whose risks — counterparty default, liquidity freeze, legal unenforceability — are still the main risks in OTC derivatives today.

Railway Mania: The Bubble Mackay Missed

In 1845–46, British investors poured roughly £170 million into railway shares — equivalent to about 40% of UK GDP at the time. When the bubble collapsed, middle-class savers were wiped out, banks failed, and the economy entered a genuine recession. This was a real mania with real systemic consequences. Charles Mackay, author of the canonical book on financial delusion, actively promoted railway shares in his journalism throughout the mania and never acknowledged his role. His silence is its own case study in how bubbles get remembered.

Anne Goldgar and the Amsterdam Archives

Goldgar's work is a model of slow-burning archival revisionism. She spent years reading court records, notary ledgers, personal correspondence, and Haarlem guild documents to reconstruct who actually traded tulips, for how much, and what happened to them. Her finding — that tulip mania was largely a literary fiction — has been accepted by specialist historians for nearly two decades but has barely penetrated popular economics, where Mackay's version remains dominant. The gap illustrates how slowly empirical history propagates against a good story.

The Calvinist Pamphlets That Made the Legend

The primary sources underlying Mackay's account were satirical dialogues published in Dutch cities in 1637–38 by Calvinist authors horrified by what they saw as the moral decay of trade. Titles translate to things like "A Dialogue Between True-Mouth and Greedy-Goods" — a genre closer to morality play than journalism. These pamphlets were never intended as historical records; they were sermons in dialogue form. That they became the factual basis for the most famous bubble in economic history is a lesson in how easily polemical literature can be mistaken for evidence.
