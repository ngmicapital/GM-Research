Alpha — Liquidations

Thursday · 23 April 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #1 11-min read
TL;DR

Liquidations are forced position closures triggered when a leveraged trader's margin balance falls below the maintenance threshold; they're the involuntary selling (or buying) engine behind every violent crypto candle. The practical edge: treat the liquidation heatmap as a gravity map — price tends to run toward clustered stops, so place yours outside the cluster, never inside it.

§01
What It Is
Plain-English definition · why perps are different
A liquidation is what happens when the exchange pulls the plug on a leveraged trade because the collateral can no longer cover the open loss — it's not a suggestion, it's automatic and final.

Every leveraged position in crypto perps has two prices that matter: the one you entered at, and the one at which the exchange will involuntarily close you out. That second price is your liquidation price — the point where your posted margin has been fully consumed by unrealised losses and the exchange must close the position to protect itself from your going negative.

Unlike a stop-loss, which you set yourself and can cancel, a liquidation is a pure function of collateral math. The exchange's risk engine watches the mark price (an aggregate, manipulation-resistant reference — not the last trade on one venue) against your maintenance margin (the minimum equity required to keep the position open). The moment mark crosses your liq price, the engine fires a market order to close you, takes its fees, and whatever's left goes back to you. Often that's zero.

The reason liquidations dominate crypto tape in a way they don't dominate equities: perps let retail run 10–100× leverage, 24/7, with no circuit breakers, on instruments that sometimes move 5% in an hour. When a cluster of 20× longs all share a similar liq price, crossing it doesn't just close one trader — it fires a fresh wave of market sells into the book, which moves price further, which liquidates the next cluster. That's the cascade.

§02
Mechanics
The math · isolated vs cross · what actually triggers the engine
Liquidation fires the moment your margin balance divided by maintenance margin requirement hits 100% — and that ratio is eroded by price moves, funding drags, and fees, in that order.

The exchange's risk engine runs one check, continuously: Margin Balance ÷ Maintenance Margin Requirement. When that ratio hits 100%, you're liquidated. Your margin balance is wallet collateral plus unrealised PnL minus accrued funding and fees. Maintenance margin is set by tier — bigger positions require a higher %.

Long liquidation price (isolated): Liq Price = Entry × (1 − Initial Margin Ratio + Maintenance Margin Ratio) Short liquidation price (isolated): Liq Price = Entry × (1 + Initial Margin Ratio − Maintenance Margin Ratio) Leverage → cushion heuristic: Cushion to liquidation ≈ 1 / Leverage − Maintenance Margin %
Formulas ignore fees and funding. Real liq prices are ~0.1–0.5% tighter than the naive calc. Cross-margin accounts use the entire wallet as collateral, so the effective liq price moves as other positions' PnL changes.
Margin modeCollateral at riskWhen to use
IsolatedOnly the margin allocated to that positionDirectional trades where you want a hard ring-fence; size the risk at entry, walk away
CrossEntire wallet balance (and PnL of other positions)Market-neutral or hedged books where one leg's unrealised gains cushion the other leg; never for single-ticker retail punts

What erodes your cushion besides price: funding rate debits (on a 50% annualised funding, you bleed ~0.14%/day against you on the wrong side), fees (round-trip ~0.1%), and — on cross — losses from unrelated positions. In a trending market with extreme funding, a leveraged position can be liquidated without price moving adversely simply from funding drag over time.

§04
In Practice
How this actually lives in a P&L · four real uses
The one rule: price hunts clustered stops, so put yours where the herd isn't — and size so a 2× ATR move can't touch liq.
1

Read the heatmap as a gravity map, not a forecast

On Coinglass, bright yellow clusters are concentrated liq exposure, not predictions of where price will go. But price gravitates to them — magnet zones exist because exchanges and MMs profit from filling those orders. Use them to anticipate where moves extend, not whether.

2

Place stops outside the visible cluster

If the heatmap shows dense longs at $82.1K and you're long BTC, a stop at $82.05K is the worst possible location — you'll be the first hit and the first refilled. Either stop above the cluster (tighter risk, cleaner invalidation) or well below (survive the sweep, wider R).

3

Fade the flush — but only when OI drops hard

A liquidation cascade that vaporises Open Interest (e.g. −15% in an hour) is a real cleanse and often marks a short-term bottom/top. A cascade that doesn't drop OI means new shorts piled in — more downside likely. Always check OI delta alongside the liq print.

4

Size to survive 2× ATR, then ignore leverage

Forget the leverage number on the ticker. Pick a stop based on invalidation (structure/ATR), pick a risk-per-trade (say 1R = 0.5% account), and solve for position size. Your effective leverage is whatever that math produces — it could be 3× or 17×, doesn't matter. What matters is that a 2× ATR move against you is ≤1R, not liquidation.

Worked example · Long BTC at $87,400

Timeframe fit: Liquidation heatmap reads best on 1H–4H for execution timing; 12H–1D for regime/cluster positioning. Ignore 1m/5m heatmap noise — too shallow to matter.

§07
Common Traps
Where most traders lose unnecessary money on this
The most expensive misconception: "low leverage is automatically safe" — funding drag and cross-margin entanglement kill accounts that never see a 1% adverse move.
1
Confusing liquidation price with stop-loss

A stop-loss is discretionary, cancellable, and typically placed at a structural invalidation. A liquidation is automatic and determined by margin math. If your stop is past your liq price, you don't have a stop — you have a ceremony. Always verify liq sits well beyond your stop.

2
Running cross-margin on a directional punt

Cross is for market-neutral books or delta-hedged plays. On a single-ticker leveraged bet, cross exposes your entire wallet to one bad candle. The "bigger cushion" isn't a feature — it's a longer fuse on a bigger bomb.

3
Ignoring funding drag on the liq price

At 50%+ annualised funding (normal in euphoric tops/bottoms), a leveraged position bleeds ~0.14%/day against it. Hold a 20× long through a week of extreme funding and your effective cushion shrinks by ~1% with zero price movement. Recalculate liq daily in extreme funding regimes.

4
Treating the heatmap as predictive

It's reactive. The heatmap tells you where concentrated stops currently sit, not that price will go there. It's a probability shader on top of your thesis, not a substitute for one. Heatmap-only trading is a faster route to becoming the liquidity than reading no data at all.

5
Anti-hype: "liquidation hunting" as an edge is mostly overfitted

A lot of the "liquidity grab / stop hunt" lore on crypto Twitter is just after-the-fact rationalisation of normal swing-point behaviour. Real liquidity sweeps exist at obvious prior highs/lows with visible cluster density; most "the whales ran the stops" claims are noise. Be honest about the signal-to-noise ratio before you build a system around it.
