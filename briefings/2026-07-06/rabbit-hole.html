The animal with 16 kinds of eyes sees colour worse than you do

Mantis shrimp have 12–16 types of colour photoreceptors. Humans have 3. Yet behavioural tests show the shrimp lose at colour discrimination — and the reason tells you something fundamental about what a brain is actually for.

Mantis shrimp have up to 16 types of colour-sensing cells versus the human eye's 3 — yet they fail basic colour-discrimination tests that any child could pass. Their brains skip the comparison step entirely, using their eyes as a spectral barcode scanner instead: fast lookup, no mixing, no shading. It is a completely different computational strategy, and it works.

TL;DR

The mantis shrimp's compound eye contains up to sixteen types of photoreceptors, spanning deep ultraviolet to far red — a range of 300 to 720 nanometres. Humans manage the entire visible spectrum with three. For decades researchers assumed the shrimp must therefore perceive some extraordinary richness of colour that we cannot imagine. A 2014 Science paper ran the behavioural tests and found the opposite: mantis shrimp cannot reliably distinguish two shades separated by fewer than 25 nm, a task humans accomplish effortlessly. They do not blend signals across channels. Instead each receptor fires independently; the brain reads which one lit up brightest and calls it done — a spectral barcode read in milliseconds. More channels, less colour mixing, faster answer.

Section 01

The World's Most Celebrated Eyes

The mantis shrimp is a marine crustacean — Odontodactylus scyllarus is the most studied species — that punches prey with a club-like appendage fast enough to cavitate water, producing a shockwave that stuns or kills independently of the physical blow. It has been described in popular science as having the most extraordinary vision in the animal kingdom, and the photoreceptor count is usually the headline: up to sixteen types of colour-sensitive cells in the midband of its compound eye, compared with three in any healthy human retina. Coverage typically stops there, implying a corresponding richness of perceived colour — a world of hues invisible to us.

The anatomical discovery itself is real and remarkable. In 1989, Thomas Cronin and Justin Marshall published two papers — one in Nature, one in the Journal of Comparative Physiology A — demonstrating that the stomatopod retina contained at least ten, and in some species twelve or more, spectrally distinct photoreceptor classes. The four colour-processing rows of the midband sample a sweep from deep ultraviolet (around 300 nm) to far red (around 720 nm), each row tuned to a narrow band. No other animal had been found with anything close. The logical inference seemed obvious: more receptors, more colour.

The results suggest that stomatopods use a color identification system based on temporal signaling combined with scanning eye movements — a previously unknown form of color vision.
— Thoen, How, Chiou & Marshall, Science, 2014

The inference turned out to be wrong. Twenty-five years after Cronin and Marshall's anatomy papers, Hanne Thoen and colleagues — including Marshall himself — published a behavioural study in Science (343:411–413, 2014) that tested the shrimps directly. They trained Haptosquilla trispinosa with food rewards to choose a target wavelength of light over an alternative, then gradually closed the gap between the two wavelengths to find the discrimination threshold. The result: mantis shrimp could not reliably distinguish wavelengths separated by fewer than roughly 25 nanometres. A normally-sighted human, using only three receptor types compared with twelve, can tell apart wavelengths separated by as little as 1 nanometre in the most sensitive part of the spectrum.

Section 02

The Mechanism: Barcode Reader vs Mixing Desk

Understanding why requires knowing how human colour vision actually works. The human retina has three cone types — short (S, peaking around 420 nm), medium (M, around 530 nm), and long (L, around 560 nm) — whose sensitivity curves overlap substantially. A red-orange light at 610 nm stimulates L cones strongly and M cones moderately; a yellow-green at 560 nm stimulates both about equally. The visual cortex and the retinal ganglion cells downstream compare the ratio of signals across channels — the opponent process — and it is this comparison that produces fine colour discrimination. The brain is running arithmetic on the differences, not just logging which receptor fired.

The opponent process

Human colour discrimination works because the three cone types have overlapping spectral sensitivities. A single wavelength stimulates all three to different degrees. The brain compares the ratios (L vs M, S vs L+M). This subtraction amplifies small wavelength differences into large neural signals — the reason a human can resolve 1 nm near 495 nm even though no single receptor type changes sensitivity that sharply across 1 nm.

The mantis shrimp's photoreceptors are narrowly tuned — each covers only about 12–25 nm of spectrum and the curves do not substantially overlap. There is no meaningful ratio to compute across adjacent channels; they fire almost independently. What the brain appears to do instead is read the output of each channel as a separate binary signal: did this receptor hit a threshold or not? The pattern across all twelve channels constitutes a spectral fingerprint — a barcode — for a given colour. The shrimp identifies the colour by recognising the pattern, not by computing the differences between channels. Rapid scanning movements of the compound eye sweep targets across the midband rows in sequence, generating that temporal barcode in roughly the time a human opponent-process computation takes.

The evolutionary logic is almost certainly about speed, not richness. Mantis shrimps are ambush predators: the interval between detecting prey and striking is measured in milliseconds. On a coral reef, the palette of relevant biological objects — other crustaceans, fish, the UV-fluorescent patches on conspecifics used for intraspecies signalling — is large but finite. Recognising a hard-coded spectral fingerprint for each category of object is faster than computing a continuous colour space and then pattern-matching against it. The shrimp trades the ability to discriminate infinitely many subtle shades for the ability to recognise a specific palette almost instantly.

Section 03

The Connections

Recognition vs discrimination. The distinction the mantis shrimp embodies — between recognising a category and discriminating fine differences within a continuum — maps onto a debate in cognitive science about how human expert perception works. Chess grandmasters do not compute ahead more moves than novices on average; they pattern-match board positions against a library of roughly 50,000 stored configurations built over years of study, shortcutting slower deliberative search. The shrimp's visual system may be doing something structurally similar: its 12 channels constitute a high-dimensional lookup table, not a measuring instrument.

The same trick in a different lab. Astronomers face a version of the mantis shrimp's design problem: the observable universe emits light across a vast spectral range, but ground-based telescopes observe through discrete narrowband filters. A 2025 arXiv paper (Merz et al., 2501.09112) named its photometric redshift estimation model Mantis Shrimp explicitly because it fuses UV, optical and infrared band images across surveys — GALEX, PanSTARRS, UnWISE — the same way the stomatopod midband fuses narrow spectral slices. The model's inputs are not compared as ratios but presented as independent parallel channels to a neural network, which then pattern-matches to a redshift. The name was not whimsy.

Engineering the eye. Viktor Gruev's group at Washington University in St. Louis built a silicon imaging chip that stacks vertically-separated photodiodes — each layer sensitive to a different wavelength — combined with metallic nanowires for polarisation detection, reproducing the mantis shrimp architecture in hardware. The device costs under $100 to fabricate and produces both spectral and polarisation images on a single sensor without moving parts or filter wheels. The medical target is early cancer detection: tumour tissue depolarises reflected light differently from healthy tissue, a signal invisible to standard cameras. The mantis shrimp's design was not copied because it sees more colour; it was copied because it is a compact, parallel, low-latency multispectral sensor.

What complexity is actually for. The mantis shrimp story is a corrective to a persistent intuition: that more sensors mean more perception. Perception is always the product of the entire system — receptor, neural processing, and the decision the animal needs to make. The shrimp's twelve channels are not twelve times the colour experience; they are twelve parallel binary inputs to a hardwired lookup table optimised for speed and specificity rather than for continuous discrimination. It is a reminder that the appropriate measure of a sensory system is not its input dimensionality but its fit to the problem. Modern machine-learning architectures revisit this tradeoff constantly — whether a narrowly-tuned specialist network or a broad general model is better depends entirely on the cost structure of the task, not on the number of parameters.

The original discovery paper

Cronin & Marshall's 1989 Nature paper 'A retina with at least ten spectral types of photoreceptors in a mantis shrimp' is short and readable — the anatomical revelation that started a 25-year misunderstanding.

The behavioural reversal

Thoen, How, Chiou & Marshall (2014) in Science is the key paper. The supplementary materials walk through the wavelength-discrimination methodology in detail — worth reading alongside the Zaidi et al. PMC analysis of why the computation is so different from ours.

🧠
Opponent process vs label lines

The Journal of Experimental Biology's 2022 review 'Colour vision in stomatopod crustaceans: more questions than answers' updates the picture — newer work shows the story is still not fully resolved, with some evidence for spectral opponency under specific conditions.

📷
The cancer-detection camera

Search 'Gruev mantis shrimp cancer detection Washington University' for the engineering work. The fabrication approach — stacked silicon photodiodes plus metallic nanowire polarisers — is elegantly simple and the cost target makes it plausible for field deployment in low-resource settings.

🌌
The redshift model

Merz et al. (arXiv 2501.09112, 2025) on the 'Mantis Shrimp' photometric redshift model is an unusual case where the biological metaphor is precisely mechanistic rather than decorative — the parallel-channel fusion architecture is the actual design choice, not just a name.
