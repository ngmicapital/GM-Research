Alpha — 25-Delta Risk Reversal

Monday, 6 July 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue 70 11 min read
TL;DR

The 25-delta risk reversal is the implied-volatility gap between an out-of-the-money call and an equidistant out-of-the-money put — a single number that tells you whether the options market is paying up for upside speculation or downside insurance, independent of which way spot is actually moving. On 5 July 2026, BTC's 25-delta RR sat at −6.93% (25d put IV 40.9% vs 25d call IV 33.9%) — a bearish skew — while spot chopped sideways near $62,700 and dealers sat in a positive-gamma regime. The practical takeaway: skew tells you where the crowd is paying for protection, not where price is going next, and a negative RR that refuses to normalize as spot grinds higher is the tell that longs are being carried, not conviction-held.

§01
What It Is
The IV spread that prices directional fear
Risk reversal is the implied-volatility difference between a 25-delta call and a 25-delta put — a clean read on whether the market is paying more for upside or downside.

Every options chain has two prices for the same distance from spot: an out-of-the-money call and an out-of-the-money put. If markets were symmetric, they'd carry the same implied volatility — a $5,000 move up should cost the same insurance as a $5,000 move down. It almost never does. The 25-delta risk reversal is the number that captures the asymmetry: take the strike whose call delta is closest to +0.25, take the strike whose put delta is closest to −0.25, and subtract call IV minus put IV. Positive means calls are pricier — the crowd is paying up for upside. Negative means puts are pricier — the crowd is paying up for downside protection.

The reason 25-delta specifically: it's far enough out-of-the-money to reflect a real directional or hedging view rather than noise around the at-the-money strike, but close enough that the contracts still trade with real open interest and tight markets. Go further out (10-delta) and you're pricing tail insurance almost nobody trades actively; go closer to 50-delta and you're just re-measuring at-the-money IV, which carries no skew information at all.

Risk reversal is a vol-market concept, not a spot-market one — it says nothing directly about where price is going. It says where the crowd is paying to be protected, which is a different and often more honest question. CME's own CVOL methodology treats it as the skew-line workhorse of their crypto vol index precisely because it isolates directional bias in implied vol from the noise of absolute IV level. [Sources differ: MenthorQ's public guide defines RR as Put IV − Call IV — the CME/Deribit/institutional-desk convention used throughout this brief is Call IV − Put IV, so negative always means put-rich here.]

§02
Mechanics
From two strikes to one skew number
Find the strike where delta ≈ +0.25 and the strike where delta ≈ −0.25 for the same expiry, pull each contract's implied vol, and subtract call IV minus put IV.

Computing a risk reversal is a four-step walk across one expiry's option chain:

Step 1 — Pick the expiry. Nearer expiries (weekly, the front two-week) carry the freshest sentiment; far-dated expiries smooth out short-term positioning and are noisier per-contract because open interest is thinner.

Step 2 — Find the 25-delta call. Walk the call strikes above spot and find the one whose Black-Scholes delta is closest to +0.25 — this typically sits 3–6% above spot depending on current IV level. Record its mark IV.

Step 3 — Find the 25-delta put. Same walk on the put side, targeting delta ≈ −0.25, typically 3–6% below spot. Record its mark IV.

Step 4 — Subtract.

RR25Δ = IV(CallΔ=+0.25) − IV(PutΔ=−0.25)
where both IVs are drawn from the same expiry's mark price via Black-Scholes inversion. A positive result = call-rich = bullish skew; negative = put-rich = bearish/defensive skew. This is the CME CVOL and Amberdata convention; some retail guides invert the sign.

A related but distinct read is the IV skew slope — instead of two single points, it averages a band of OTM puts (5–15% below spot) against a band of OTM calls (5–15% above), giving a steadiness metric less sensitive to one contract's noisy print. The two usually agree in direction but the slope reads how steep the whole wing is, while risk reversal reads how lopsided the specific 25-delta point traders actually use to hedge is.

Positive RR (call-rich)Speculative call buying dominates; typical crypto baseline given retail's structural long-call bias
Negative RR (put-rich)Institutional hedging or genuine fear dominates; rare in crypto historically, more common in 2025–26 as ETF-driven basis books hedge tail risk
Flat / near-zero RRBalanced positioning, no strong directional lean in the hedging flow

Crypto's baseline differs from equities. Equity index options are structurally put-rich almost permanently — funds buy protection, sell covered calls, and the skew reflects decades of that flow. Crypto's baseline has historically run the other way: speculative call buying dominates retail flow, so a flip to negative RR is the meaningful event, not the default state.

§04
In Practice
READING THE TILT WITHOUT A TERMINAL
Skew tells you where hedging flow concentrates, not where price is going — never trade risk reversal as a standalone directional signal.
1

Track the sign flip, not the level

Crypto's baseline RR is usually positive (call-rich) because retail structurally over-buys calls. A flip to negative is the actionable event — it means institutional hedging flow has overwhelmed the retail call bid, which happens rarely enough to matter when it does.

2

Pair RR with spot direction, never read it alone

Negative RR while spot falls = confirmation, everyone's scared together. Negative RR while spot grinds higher = the more interesting case — it usually means large longs are hedged rather than convicted, and the rally is more fragile than price action alone suggests.

3

Use it to price your own hedges, not just to read sentiment

When RR is deeply negative, OTM puts are rich — selling them (if you're a willing dip-buyer at that strike) collects inflated premium. When RR is near zero or positive, puts are cheap relative to their historical range — a good regime to buy protection instead of paying up for calls.

4

Compare against the term structure for the full picture

Skew (across strikes, one expiry) and term structure (across expiries, one strike) answer different questions. A backwardated term structure plus a deeply negative RR together is the strongest signal you can build from options data alone that the market has priced a near-term bad outcome.

Worked example — reading the 5 July 2026 BTC skew as a hedge decision

Spot $62,702. 25-delta put (strike $60,000, 17JUL26 expiry) trades 40.9% IV; 25-delta call (strike $65,000, same expiry) trades 33.9% IV. RR = −6.93%. A trader long spot BTC who wants downside insurance sees puts priced rich relative to calls — insurance is expensive right now specifically because everyone else is buying it too.

§07
Common Traps
WHERE SKEW READERS GET IT WRONG
The single biggest mistake: reading a negative risk reversal as a short signal — it usually just means someone big is hedging a long, not that a top is in.
1
Reading negative RR as a short signal

Risk reversal prices hedging demand, not forecast direction. Deeply negative skew during a rally more often means longs are protected, not that a top is forming — treat it as a positioning fact, not a trade trigger.

2
Ignoring the sign-convention trap

Institutional desks (CME, Deribit, Amberdata) compute RR as call IV − put IV; some retail explainer sites define it the opposite way. Confirm the convention before comparing two sources' numbers, or you'll read a bullish signal as bearish.

3
Using thin, far-dated expiries

Skew on an expiry with a handful of open contracts is mostly noise — a single $50k block trade can swing the 25-delta print by several vol points. Anchor reads to the most liquid near-dated expiry (here, the 17JUL26 contract with 636 total contracts).

4
Forgetting OI vs volume can diverge

On 5 July, BTC's structural OI put/call (0.388) still read call-heavy while the day's volume put/call (1.368) had flipped bearish — the stock of old positions and the day's fresh flow told different stories. Volume is the leading indicator; OI catches up slowly.

5
[Honest] Skew alone is a thinner edge than the vol-desk hype suggests

Risk reversal is real, well-documented, and genuinely informative about hedging flow — but on its own it is a slow-moving, low-frequency signal, not a scalping tool. It earns its place combined with GEX, term structure, and OI flow, not as a standalone system. Treat any account selling skew trading as a complete standalone strategy with real skepticism.
