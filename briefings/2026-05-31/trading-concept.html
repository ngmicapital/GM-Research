Alpha — The Wyckoff Spring

Sunday · 31 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #37 11-min read
TL;DR

A Wyckoff spring is a deliberate false breakdown below a trading range's support — a final shakeout where large operators trigger resting stops, absorb the forced selling, then snap price back inside the range; it matters because it is the cleanest signal that supply is exhausted and a markup is about to begin. The practical takeaway: never trade the break itself — trade the low-volume test that follows, because a spring with no test is just a breakdown that hasn't finished yet. Today BTC is running the live version of this — $2.6B of May ETF outflows flushed weak hands while price held $73,843 (→ see today's Morning Edge).

§01
What It Is
Phase C · the failed breakdown · the bear trap
A spring is a false breakdown below range support that quickly reverses — a bear trap engineered to shake out weak longs and let the Composite Operator finish accumulating.

A spring is not a pattern you trade because price broke support. It's a pattern you trade because price broke support and failed. The break is bait. The failure is the signal. Strip away the schematic vocabulary and a spring is one event: a probe below an obvious floor that harvests the stops sitting there, gets absorbed by passive demand, and refuses to follow through. The penetration is the cost the operator pays to acquire cheap inventory; the snap-back is the receipt.

Wyckoff's own definition is precise: "a price move below the support level of the TR that quickly reverses and moves back into the TR. It is an example of a bear trap because the drop below support appears to signal resumption of the downtrend." The "TR" is the trading range that forms after a downtrend exhausts itself. The spring lives in Phase C — the "moment of truth" — after the range has already built through Phases A and B (Preliminary Support, a Selling Climax, an Automatic Rally, a Secondary Test).

Behind it sits Wyckoff's Composite Operator — his device for treating all large, informed activity as if it were one actor. The spring is that actor's last move before markup: a manufactured panic that transfers shares from weak hands to strong hands at the exact moment retail conviction is lowest. The mirror image in a topping range is the UTAD (Upthrust After Distribution) — a false breakout above resistance that traps breakout buyers before markdown. Same mechanic, opposite polarity.

One distinction matters before you go further: a spring is a relatively shallow probe on light-to-moderate volume; a terminal shakeout is the violent cousin — a deep penetration on climactic volume. Both do the same job. They differ only in intensity, and that intensity changes how you trade them (§02).

SPRINGShallow probe below support that fails and reverses back inside the range SHAKEOUTDeeper, higher-volume version — the terminal flush of weak hands TESTLow-volume secondary probe confirming supply is exhausted PHASE CThe "moment of truth" where the spring/shakeout occurs SOS / LPSSign of Strength breakout, then Last Point of Support pullback UTADThe distribution mirror — false breakout above resistance, then markdown
If you only remember one thing The spring is the break; the test is the confirmation. No low-volume test that holds above the spring low, no trade — you're just guessing a wick is a bottom.
§02
Mechanics
Stops as fuel · effort vs result · the three spring grades
Stops below support become liquidity; passive demand absorbs the forced selling, the low-volume test proves no supply remains, then markup begins.

Walk it step by step. After a downtrend, price builds a range — a floor of obvious support (equal lows, a multi-week base, a round number) that everyone can see. That visibility is the point: every retail long parks a stop just beneath it, and every breakout-short trader is waiting to sell the break. The cluster of resting sell orders under support is the liquidity the operator wants.

The spring fires. Price probes below the floor and detonates that cluster — stops trigger, breakout shorts pile in, the sell flow surges. If informed demand is sitting there with passive bids, it eats the entire wave: aggressive market sells hit limit buys and get absorbed, volume spikes, but price barely progresses. Then it snaps back inside the range. That is the spring. What follows is the part amateurs skip — the test: a second, smaller probe back toward the spring low on materially lower volume. If the test holds above the spring low on a fraction of the volume, supply is gone. Price then breaks the range top (SOS), pulls back to a higher low (LPS), and marks up.

The diagnostic engine underneath is Wyckoff's law of effort versus result: "Divergences between volume and price often signal a change in the direction of a price trend." On the spring, effort (volume) is high and result (net downside progress) is near zero — selling pressure meeting demand stronger than the move itself. On the test, both collapse — no effort, no result — because there's nothing left to absorb.

Effort vs Result — the spring tell: Effort = volume on the breakdown probe Result = net price progress that holds below support Spring confirmed when: Effort HIGH (or climactic) AND Result ≈ 0 → price closes back inside the range Test validation (the green light): Test_volume < Spring_volume (ideally ≤ 50%, often far less) Test_low ≥ Spring_low (a higher low = supply exhausted) → demand in control → Phase D markup Invalidation: Second close BELOW spring low on RISING volume → not a spring; it's a breakdown. Stand down.
Volume is read in context, never in isolation — a "high-volume spring" only matters if the down-result fails to hold. Wide-spread down bars that do follow through are a breakdown, not absorption. The close location and the test are what separate the two.

Springs come in grades — the framework Rubén Villahermosa systematises from Wyckoff and Hank Pruden. They differ by how much supply shows up at the break, which dictates how much confirmation you should demand before committing size:

GradePenetration · VolumeWhat it tells you · how to trade it
#1 Strong supply
(terminal shakeout)		Deep break · high / climactic volumeHeavy supply still present. Needs a strong demand response to reverse and a clean test before entry. Widest stop, most patience — never front-run it.
#2 Supply in controlModerate break · average volumeThe standard spring. Supply present but fading; a normal demand response drives it back up. Trade the reclaim and the test in the usual way.
#3 Test spring
(minor spring)		Shallow probe · low volumeAlmost no supply left — the most bullish grade. Light penetration on light volume = sellers already exhausted. Tightest stop, best risk:reward.

The counter-intuitive part: the shallower, quieter spring (#3) is the more bullish one. Low volume on the break means there was hardly any supply to shake out in the first place. A deep, loud #1 shakeout looks more dramatic but carries more residual supply — which is exactly why it demands a test before you touch it.

The same engine, inverted. After a topping range (BC → AR → ST), the UTAD pokes above resistance to trigger breakout buyers and trapped shorts' stops — then fails back inside. The Sign of Weakness (SOW) breaks support; the Last Point of Supply (LPSY) is a weak rally back to the broken level that fails before markdown. A spring catches the bottom; a UTAD catches the top.
§04
In Practice
Demand the test · locate the liquidity · two entries · worked example
The one rule: never trade the spring itself — trade the test (or the LPS after the SOS), and your stop lives below the spring low.
1

Demand the Test

A spring without a low-volume secondary test is just a breakdown that hasn't finished yet. Wait for the retest of the spring low on shrinking volume that holds at or above it. That higher low on lighter effort is the green light — it's the market telling you the sellers are out of ammunition.

2

Locate the Liquidity First

The best springs occur below the most obvious, crowded support — equal lows, a multi-week range floor, a round number, a level every chart-watcher has drawn. The more obvious the floor, the more stops are stacked beneath it, and the more fuel the shakeout has. If there's no liquidity to harvest, there's no reason for a spring.

3

Two Entries, Two Risk Profiles

Aggressive: long the test as it reclaims support, stop below the spring low — best R, less confirmation. Conservative: long the LPS, the first higher low after the SOS breaks the range top, stop below the LPS — worse R, far more confirmation. Pick by conviction and grade, not by ego.

4

Size to the Spring Grade

A #3 test spring — shallow, low-volume — lets you size up on a tight stop because there's almost no supply left. A #1 terminal shakeout — deep, climactic — demands a wider stop and a confirmed test before you commit; front-running heavy supply is how you become the liquidity. Let the volume size the trade.

Worked Example — BTC Spring off the Range Floor (illustrative, today's levels)
SetupBTC ranging $73,350–$74,520 (per today's Morning Edge). May ETF supply forces a probe to ~$73,100 on a volume spike — stops below the $73,350 floor get triggered.
SpringPrice reclaims $73,350 within hours; the breakdown finds no follow-through. Effort high, result ≈ 0 — absorption.
Test (the trigger)A retest toward ~$73,250 on materially lower volume holds above the spring low. This is the entry signal, not the spring itself.
EntryLong the reclaim ~$73,500. Stop below the spring low: $72,950. Risk = ~$550 / ~0.75%.
TargetSOS through $74,520 trips the $112M short cluster; first target $74,520 (≈2:1), extension $75,400+ as the cascade fuels ($98.7M / $86.4M stacked above). Trail to entry on the reclaim of $74,520.
InvalidationA second close back below $72,950 on rising volume = not a spring, it's a breakdown. Cut without hesitation.

Honest note: this is the textbook script applied to live levels, not a called trade. As of writing, BTC has not yet printed the confirming low-volume test — the absorption is real, the follow-through is unproven (§05).

Timeframe fit: read the spring/test on 1h–4h, take the entry trigger on 5m–15m, anchor bias to the daily range. The higher the timeframe of the range that springs, the more reliable the event — a daily-range spring outranks a 15-minute one every time.

Until that test prints and the SOS fires, the absorption is real but the follow-through is unproven.

BTC ~$15,500 — The FTX Terminal Shakeout

The cleanest confirmed spring of the last cycle was a terminal shakeout. After the June 2022 Selling Climax near $17,600, BTC built a long base. Then the FTX collapse in November drove a deep, climactic probe to ~$15,480 on 21 November 2022 — breaking below the June low on heavy, forced-liquidation volume. A grade-#1 shakeout: maximum fear, maximum supply.

And then — nothing lower. BTC never printed a lower low. That failed undercut was the bottom. The 2023 markup followed: BTC cleared $20K within weeks and traded above $30K by April 2023, roughly +95% off the shakeout low. Deep penetration, climactic volume, zero follow-through, then markup — the spring at cycle scale, exactly as Wyckoff drew it a century earlier.

§07
Common Traps
Five ways traders misread the spring
The single biggest mistake: trading the break instead of the failure — which makes you the exact liquidity the spring is built to harvest.
1
Trading the Break, Not the Failure

The entire point is that the breakdown fails. If you short the break of support — or panic-sell your long into it — you are the stop-loss liquidity the operator is harvesting. The spring is designed to extract exactly that reaction from you. Wait for the reclaim and the test; let the trap close on someone else.

2
No Test, No Trade

A reclaim alone is not confirmation. A genuine spring is validated by a low-volume secondary test that holds above the spring low — that's what proves supply is exhausted rather than merely paused. Skip the test and you're trading a guess. Most "failed springs" are just springs that never got their test and were entered anyway.

3
Calling Every Wick a Spring

Not every poke below support is a spring. Without the accumulation context — a developed range, a prior SC/AR/ST, evidence that supply has been absorbed over time — a "spring" is just a support break on its way lower. The context is the concept. A wick with no story behind it is a wick, not a setup.

4
Ignoring the Effort on the Reclaim

A reclaim that happens on wide-spread down bars and rising volume still has supply present — that's a grade-#1 that needs another test, or is simply failing. Read the volume, not just the wick. Effort-vs-result is the whole engine: if the down-effort keeps producing down-results, demand has not yet won.

5
"Wyckoff Spring" as a Retrofitted Label

Frank take: "spring" is the most over-applied term in crypto TA. Every green candle off a low gets christened a spring in hindsight, and survivorship bias makes the pattern look far more reliable than live trading proves — because the failed "springs" that just kept dumping get quietly forgotten. The concept is real and powerful, but it's only obvious after the markup. In real time, most apparent springs are just support breaks. Trade the test and the stop, not the label.
