Headline CFTC Press Releases 9239-26 / 9240-26 / 9241-26 [Primary] Derivatives · Perpetual Futures · Market Structure
🟢 High — Order for approval issued; Kalshi BTCPERP live, Coinbase relief effective

CFTC Onshores Crypto Perpetual Futures In Its Biggest Single-Day Derivatives Move — Approves KalshiEX's BTCPERP As The First Fully-Regulated US Bitcoin Perpetual (29 May), Issues Coinbase A No-Action Letter Routing Customers To Deribit/Bermuda Foreign Perps With Crypto Posted As Margin, And Releases A Staff Advisory Blessing 24/7 Trading, Clearing And Settlement For Crypto Derivatives

"Today's action to onshore crypto asset perpetuals reflects the CFTC's commitment to fostering responsible innovation while ensuring that these novel products are traded on regulated exchanges that uphold customer protections and market integrity." — Michael Selig, Chairman, CFTC — statement accompanying the KalshiEX BTCPERP approval, 29 May 2026

On Friday 29 May the CFTC issued an Order for Approval to KalshiEX, LLC — a designated contract market — for its BTCPERP contract, "a perpetual contract that references the spot price of bitcoin, as a futures contract." Kalshi had submitted the contract under Commission Regulation 40.3 on 28 May; the Commission found it compliant with the Commodity Exchange Act and the DCM Core Principles under Section 5(d) and Part 38. It is the first true, fully-regulated bitcoin perpetual futures contract listed on a US exchange — a product that, until now, US persons could access only on offshore venues such as Binance, Bybit and OKX.

The Commission paired the approval with two structural actions the same day. First, a no-action letter to Coinbase Financial Markets (CFM) permitting it to route US customers to perpetual (and options) products treated as foreign futures through Coinbase's Bermuda venue and Deribit, and — critically — allowing CFM to post customers' digital assets (bitcoin, ether and stablecoins) as margin collateral. Second, a staff advisory from the Divisions of Clearing and Risk, Market Oversight and Market Participants setting expectations for round-the-clock trading, clearing and settlement: resilient redundant systems, real-time surveillance, and weekend/overnight compliance staffing. The advisory expressly judges crypto derivatives "well-suited for 24/7 trading," while cautioning that agricultural and other markets may not be.

Taken together — a domestic perpetual, a compliant offshore-routing pathway, and a 24/7 operating framework — this is the most consequential single day for US crypto derivatives regulation on record, and it was delivered by administrative action while the CLARITY Act remains stuck on the Senate floor.

📎 Historical Precedent: Perpetual swaps were pioneered offshore by BitMEX (2016) and became the dominant crypto instrument globally, but US persons were fenced out after the 2020–21 BitMEX/CEA actions. The CFTC's 2017 approval of CME/Cboe bitcoin dated futures was the last comparable "first" — this is the perpetual analog, eight years on, and the first time the funding-rate perpetual structure has been blessed under US DCM rules.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The mechanism matters: BTCPERP is approved as a futures contract under Reg 40.3 self-certification-plus-approval, not as a new product category requiring rulemaking — which is why it landed in days, not years (per the CFTC's own PR 9240-26). The Coinbase no-action letter is the cleverer instrument: by classifying Bermuda/Deribit perps as foreign futures and permitting crypto-as-margin, the agency gives the largest US exchange a compliant on-ramp to the deepest existing perp liquidity without waiting for its own DCM perp to scale. The Brief's read: This is Selig converting the March SEC-CFTC joint interpretation's "responsible innovation" rhetoric into hard product approvals, and doing it through no-action/40.3 channels precisely because they are fast and hard to challenge. The enforcement-trajectory signal is that the CFTC now wants to pull activity onshore rather than police it offshore — but every no-action letter is revocable by a future Commission, so the durability question (same as CLARITY) is unresolved. Expect copycat 40.3 submissions for ETH and SOL perpetuals within weeks.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Consumer advocates (Better Markets, Americans for Financial Reform commentary, 29–30 May) argue the CFTC just handed retail Americans the single most dangerous instrument in crypto — high-leverage perpetuals with funding-rate liquidation cascades — wrapped in a "regulated" label that implies a safety it does not confer, and did so via staff letters that dodge notice-and-comment. The Brief's read: The retail-harm concern is serious and the procedural shortcut is real — a no-action letter is not a rule, and the 24/7 advisory "does not create new regulatory requirements." But the counter-counter is that US persons already trade these perps on unregulated offshore venues with worse protections; onshoring at least imposes DCM surveillance, segregation and a US forum. The genuine risk is leverage limits: if BTCPERP launches with offshore-comparable leverage and no position caps, the "regulated" framing becomes marketing rather than protection.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Direct winner is Kalshi — completing its pivot "from prediction market leader to next-gen derivatives exchange" (CEO Tarek Mansour, 29 May) and opening a perps waitlist immediately — and Coinbase, whose CLO Paul Grewal called it "a massive first for the industry"; Coinbase's 2025 Deribit acquisition now becomes a compliant US-facing perp pipe. The Brief's read: Capital-flow signal: a slice of the multi-trillion-dollar offshore perp volume currently captured by Binance/Bybit/OKX becomes contestable by US-regulated venues for the first time — the structural moat of offshore perps (US-person access) just eroded. CME's bitcoin-futures franchise faces a new domestic competitor in the perpetual format it never offered. Losers are pure-offshore perp venues with US-person grey-market flow, and — longer term — the argument that the US needs CLARITY to be competitive, since the CFTC just demonstrated it can onshore the marquee product without it.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish COIN (Deribit/Bermuda perp pipe + crypto-as-margin), Kalshi (private, pre-IPO derivatives optionality), CRCL (stablecoin eligible as perp margin collateral) — US-regulated perps create a new fee pool.

🔴 Bearish Pure-offshore perp venues reliant on US grey-market flow (Binance, Bybit, OKX — private); CME's perpetual-format competitive gap widens.

⚪ Neutral BTC, ETH spot — structurally supportive (more regulated leverage and price-discovery rails) but no direct spot flow; effect is venue/structure, not asset.

European Commission Opens The First Formal Review Of MiCA — Asks Whether Its Landmark Crypto Framework Is Still "Fit For Purpose" Barely 18 Months After Full Application, With The Stablecoin Yield/Interest Ban, DeFi And Staking Gaps, And Third-Country Scope All On The Table; Industry Reads It As The Opening Move Toward A "MiCA 2"

"The Commission is seeking feedback on whether the EU's crypto-asset framework remains fit for purpose as digital-asset markets — and the global regulatory landscape — continue to evolve." — European Commission, MiCA review consultation announcement, 20 May 2026 [paraphrased — institutional statement; no individual official quoted]

On 20 May the European Commission launched a targeted consultation on the review of the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA), open until 31 August 2026. It runs as two parallel tracks — a public questionnaire and a more technical/legal "targeted" consultation — and invites crypto firms, banks, technology providers, academics and consumer groups to weigh in on how the regime has functioned since stablecoin rules took effect in June 2024 and the full framework became applicable that December.

The review covers all three MiCA pillars — crypto-assets, asset-referenced tokens and e-money tokens (stablecoins) — plus issuers and CASPs. Coverage in the specialist press (CoinDesk, bitcoin.com, Notabene, Cryptonomist) flags the live pressure points: the EU's prohibition on stablecoin interest/yield (now competitively awkward against US "rewards" models), the absence of a dedicated DeFi and staking regime, third-country firm treatment, and whether the e-money-token caps are throttling euro-stablecoin growth. The Commission must report to Parliament and Council on MiCA's application — and may attach a legislative proposal — by 30 June 2027, so today's consultation is the evidentiary foundation for any "MiCA 2."

📎 Historical Precedent: Mirrors the EU's standard "review clause" cadence — MiFID II's post-implementation review (begun ~2020) reopened large parts of the regime and produced MiFIR amendments years later. The notable feature here is speed: opening a fit-for-purpose review ~18 months after full application is unusually early, signalling the Commission already sees competitive and structural gaps.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Maples Group and Norton Rose Fulbright (Regulation Tomorrow, May 2026) note the consultation is "targeted" rather than a wholesale reopening — the Commission is scoping where MiCA needs surgical amendment, not rewriting it. The Brief's read: The regulatory-arbitrage subtext is the whole story. MiCA's stablecoin interest ban and EMT caps were drafted in a pre-GENIUS world; now that the US permits compliant payment stablecoins and "rewards," and the UK is building a stablecoin-payments regime, the EU is quietly asking whether its own rules are pushing euro-stablecoin issuance and CASP domicile offshore. Watch the targeted-consultation questions on DeFi and staking — MiCA deliberately punted on both, and how the Commission frames them now will set the perimeter for the next three years. Any firm with an EU strategy should file: this is the rare window where the rulebook itself is up for revision.

🔄 Contrarian Take

A skeptical reading (voiced by several Brussels-watchers) is that an early "fit for purpose" review is bureaucratic throat-clearing — the real legislative vehicle can't move before the 2027 report, so nothing binding changes for years, and the consultation mainly generates lobbying noise. The Brief's read: Half-right on timing, wrong on significance. Yes, no statute changes in 2026 — but consultations shape ESMA/EBA Level-2 and Level-3 guidance long before any MiCA 2, and a Commission signal that the yield ban is "under review" already changes how euro-stablecoin issuers plan. The deeper point: the EU opening this door at all, this early, concedes that the first-mover regime is now a competitiveness question, not just a consumer-protection one — a meaningful shift in Brussels' framing.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Most exposed: euro-stablecoin issuers (Circle's EURC, Société Générale-FORGE's EURCV, Banking Circle's EURI) constrained by the EMT caps and interest ban, and DeFi/staking providers operating in MiCA's grey zone. The Brief's read: Capital-flow signal: a credible path to relaxing the EMT caps or the yield ban would make the EU materially more attractive for euro-denominated stablecoin issuance — currently a rounding error against USD stablecoins. Competitive-dynamics winners are the large MiCA-licensed CASPs (Coinbase, Kraken, OKX, Crypto.com via Malta/Ireland) that can absorb a moving rulebook; losers are smaller CASPs that built compliance stacks around the current text and now face revision risk. The consultation also hands law firms and trade bodies (EU Crypto Initiative, Blockchain for Europe) a concrete advocacy target.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish CRCL (EURC upside if EMT caps/yield ban relaxed), GLE.PA (Société Générale — EURCV issuer) — euro-stablecoin growth optionality on a MiCA recalibration.

🔴 Bearish Small EU-only CASPs that hard-coded compliance to the current text — revision risk and re-papering cost during the review window.

⚪ Neutral COIN, Kraken (private), OKX (private) — already MiCA-passported via Malta/Ireland; absorb rulebook change without re-domicile.

High FDIC / ABA Banking Journal / National Law Review [Primary] US · Stablecoins · BSA/AML · GENIUS Act
🟡 Medium — Board-approved NPRM; 60-day comment period running

FDIC Proposes Bank Secrecy Act And Sanctions-Compliance Standards For GENIUS Act Stablecoin Issuers — Board Advances An NPRM Requiring FDIC-Supervised Permitted Payment Stablecoin Issuers To Run Full AML/CFT And OFAC Programs, With FinCEN Granted A 30-Day Pre-Enforcement Review; Comments Open For 60 Days

"Banks devote enormous resources to BSA compliance, yet it is often unclear whether that effort actually advances law enforcement or national security — and the threat of large fines pushes banks to simply deny or close customer accounts." — Travis Hill, Chairman, FDIC — statement on the proposed rule, 22 May 2026 [paraphrased]

On 22 May the FDIC Board approved a notice of proposed rulemaking establishing Bank Secrecy Act and sanctions-compliance standards for FDIC-supervised permitted payment stablecoin issuers (PPSIs) — the category of bank-affiliated stablecoin issuers created by the GENIUS Act. The rule would require covered issuers to maintain AML/CFT programs and OFAC sanctions programs and meet reporting obligations consistent with FinCEN and Treasury requirements. The comment period runs 60 days from Federal Register publication, and the proposal poses an unusually broad 144 questions — a tell that the FDIC wants the perimeter shaped by industry input rather than imposed.

A notable feature is the enforcement architecture: before the FDIC takes certain AML/CFT enforcement or significant supervisory actions against a PPSI, it would give FinCEN's director at least 30 days to review the planned action (absent urgency). Chairman Travis Hill paired the proposal with his standing critique that BSA effort is poorly calibrated to outcomes and drives de-risking/account closures — framing this rule as an attempt to apply bank-grade AML rigor to stablecoin issuers without simply importing the full weight of the existing bank BSA regime.

📎 Historical Precedent: This is the AML companion to the FDIC's earlier 7 April 2026 GENIUS Act implementation proposal (issuance-approval and the deposit-insurance question). It sits alongside parallel GENIUS rulemakings by the OCC, Federal Reserve and FinCEN — the same multi-regulator pattern as the post-Dodd-Frank Volcker Rule, where five agencies issued coordinated but separate rules for a single statutory mandate.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Hunton and the National Law Review (May 2026) read the FinCEN 30-day pre-enforcement review as the operative novelty — it builds inter-agency coordination directly into the enforcement workflow, which could slow but also legitimise FDIC actions against issuers. The Brief's read: The strategic question for issuers is scope creep: a "permitted payment stablecoin issuer" that must run a full bank-grade AML/CFT + OFAC program faces compliance costs that favour incumbents (banks and well-capitalised fintechs) over startups — exactly the consolidation dynamic GENIUS critics warned about. The 144 questions are the leverage point: this is where industry argues for tailored, risk-based programs rather than wholesale importation of the bank BSA stack. Enforcement-trajectory signal: the federal stablecoin regime is being built AML-first, which means the binding constraint on new issuers will be sanctions/Travel-Rule capability, not reserve mechanics.

🔄 Contrarian Take

A pro-industry counter is that layering full bank-style BSA/OFAC obligations onto stablecoin issuers — whose on-chain rails are already more transparent and traceable than the bank wire system — is redundant gold-plating that entrenches incumbents and pushes issuance offshore. The Brief's read: The transparency point has merit (public ledgers are a forensic gift), but it cuts both ways — on-chain visibility is worthless without the sanctions-screening and SAR machinery to act on it, which is precisely what the rule mandates. The real risk isn't redundancy; it's calibration. If the FDIC imports the bank BSA regime wholesale (the very system Hill says drives wasteful de-risking), it recreates the failure mode he criticises. The 60-day window is where that gets fought.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Directly covered: any stablecoin issuer that becomes a PPSI under an FDIC-supervised institution — the SoFi/Mastercard-style bank-rail issuers entering the market (28 May brief) now have their AML guardrails drafted. The Brief's read: Capital-flow signal: compliance cost becomes a moat — well-capitalised issuers (bank affiliates, Circle, PayPal) absorb a 144-question AML build; thinly-funded entrants do not, accelerating consolidation toward a handful of compliant issuers. Winners are RegTech/blockchain-analytics vendors (Chainalysis, Elliptic, TRM Labs) conscripted as the screening backbone, and the incumbent issuers who can pass the bar. Watch whether the OCC and Fed mirror the FinCEN 30-day review — if they do, it becomes the standard federal stablecoin enforcement choreography.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish CRCL (Circle), PYPL (PayPal USD), bank-affiliated issuers (JPM, MA via Mastercard rails) — AML compliance cost favours capitalised incumbents; blockchain-analytics vendors gain legislated demand.

🔴 Bearish Thinly-funded / non-bank stablecoin aspirants — 144-question AML/OFAC build raises the entry bar; offshore issuers face US-access friction.

⚪ Neutral USDC/USDT circulation — operational cost, not a demand-side catalyst; effect is issuer-structure, not token velocity.

📋 What To Do — This Week
TraderNo single-name catalyst, but treat AML compliance as a consolidation thesis — the federal stablecoin market is being built to favour a few capitalised issuers; position toward the compliant incumbents, not the long tail.
CounselFile on the 144 questions before the 60-day window closes — argue for risk-based, tailored AML programs and clarify the FinCEN 30-day review's interaction with FDIC supervisory timelines for any issuer client.
ComplianceIf your institution sponsors or plans a PPSI, scope a bank-grade AML/CFT + OFAC program now — sanctions screening and Travel-Rule capability are the gating items, not reserve management.
FounderIf a stablecoin is on your roadmap, budget for a full AML/OFAC build or partner with a chartered issuer — the FDIC just signalled the compliance bar will price out under-capitalised standalone issuance.

🟢 High — Signed into law 19 May; effective immediately

South Carolina Enacts S.163, A "Bitcoin Rights" Law — Bars State And Local Government From Requiring CBDC Payments Or Joining Federal CBDC Pilots, Protects Self-Custody And Hardware Wallets, Shields Crypto Miners From Discriminatory Zoning, And Exempts Node Operators, Developers And Crypto-To-Crypto Trading From Money-Transmitter Licensing

"No state or local government entity may require, or prohibit, any person from using a self-hosted wallet to maintain custody of digital assets, nor accept or require payment by central bank digital currency." — South Carolina Senate Bill 163, enacted text, signed 19 May 2026 [paraphrased from the bill's operative provisions]

Governor Henry McMaster signed Senate Bill 163 into law on 19 May 2026 (ratified 14 May), making South Carolina the latest state to enact a pro-crypto, anti-CBDC statute. The law prohibits state and local government entities from accepting or requiring payment in a central bank digital currency and bars them from participating in any Federal Reserve or federal CBDC pilot or testing program. It protects the right of individuals and businesses to use self-hosted and hardware wallets and to accept digital assets as payment.

On the industry side, S.163 shields digital-asset mining operations in industrially-zoned areas from discriminatory zoning rules, excessive noise limits and targeted restrictions, and — significantly for the licensing perimeter — exempts mining, node operation, on-chain software development and crypto-to-crypto trading from money-transmitter licensing requirements. The licensing exemptions track the policy logic of the federal Promoting Innovation in Blockchain Development Act (introduced February 2026): liability and licensing should attach to control of customer funds, not to writing code or validating transactions.

📎 Historical Precedent: S.163 follows the template of state "Bitcoin rights"/anti-CBDC laws pioneered by Wyoming, and the wave of 2024–2026 statutes (Oklahoma, Montana, Arizona, Texas) protecting self-custody and node operation. The money-transmitter carve-out for non-custodial actors mirrors the long-standing FinCEN 2019 guidance distinguishing custodial money transmitters from non-custodial software providers.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The Brief's read: The substantive teeth are in the licensing exemptions, not the anti-CBDC symbolism. The CBDC ban is largely expressive — there is no US retail CBDC to refuse — but the explicit state-law exemption of node operators, developers and crypto-to-crypto trading from money-transmitter licensing is a concrete safe harbour at the state level, exactly where the Tornado Cash/Samourai prosecutions created the most builder anxiety. The pattern-recognition signal: as federal money-transmission liability for developers stays unresolved in Congress, states are building their own statutory shields, creating a patchwork where the same non-custodial activity is expressly protected in SC, Wyoming and Texas but legally grey elsewhere. Counsel advising US-based protocols should now map state-by-state non-custodial safe harbours the way they map BitLicense exposure.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Direct beneficiaries: crypto miners siting in South Carolina (cheap industrial power, now with zoning protection) and non-custodial software teams seeking a US base with statutory licensing clarity. The Brief's read: Capital-flow signal: state-level mining protections and licensing carve-outs are becoming a competitive tool in the inter-state contest for crypto infrastructure investment — South Carolina joins Texas and Wyoming in courting miners and builders with legal certainty incumbents can underwrite. The losers are states with hostile or ambiguous regimes that watch capital and hashrate migrate to the friendly jurisdictions. The effect is marginal nationally but directionally clear: the US "crypto map" is fragmenting into builder-friendly and builder-hostile states, and that map increasingly drives siting decisions.

Cross-Cutting Implications

US Derivatives & Market Structure

⬆ Accelerating

The CFTC onshored crypto perpetuals, blessed crypto-as-margin via foreign-futures routing and endorsed 24/7 trading — all administratively, while CLARITY stalls. The US is now building market structure through agency action faster than Congress can legislate it.

Stablecoin Regulation

⬆ Accelerating

The FDIC's BSA/sanctions NPRM sets the AML guardrails for GENIUS Act issuers just as the EU reopens MiCA's stablecoin yield ban and EMT caps. Same instrument, divergent direction: the US builds an AML-first issuance regime; the EU asks whether its constraints are uncompetitive.

DeFi & Developer Liability

↗ Mixed Signals

South Carolina codifies a non-custodial licensing carve-out and the EU's MiCA review puts DeFi/staking gaps on the table — but the federal developer-protection bill remains stuck in committee. Protection is advancing at the state and EU level, lagging federally.

AI Governance

⚡ Widening

No new statute this window, but the state patchwork (IL/NY/CA frontier-audit laws) keeps hardening as the EU defers high-risk obligations to 2027–28. The transatlantic and federal-state divergence both continue to widen.

Tokenisation & RWA

⬆ Quietly Advancing

Tokenised RWA has cleared ~$32bn with BlackRock/JPMorgan/Franklin Templeton scaling, but the SEC's tokenized-stock "innovation exemption" stays shelved at OIRA. Wholesale rails advance; the retail equity-token path remains gated on market-structure objections.

UK / EU / US Regulatory Divergence

⚡ Widening

The US onshores marquee derivatives and drafts AML-first stablecoin rules; the EU reopens MiCA to stay competitive; the UK methodically builds its authorisation regime. Three different theories of crypto regulation pulling apart in the same fortnight.

Singapore / APAC
