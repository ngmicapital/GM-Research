Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

Fear is the common enemy this week, and courage is the discipline that always costs. Daily Stoic's "It Is A Lonely Thing" contrasts the many who knew but stayed silent during Caesar's rise with the few who stood alone — Cato, Thrasea, Helvidius, and the abolitionist Thomas Wentworth Higginson, who translated Epictetus and then stormed a federal courthouse to free a fugitive slave — arguing that courage and justice do not ask "is this safe?" but say "that's wrong," loudly and repeatedly, even when threatened. Senator Mark Kelly — Gulf War combat pilot, test pilot, and commander of the Space Shuttle Endeavour's final flight — reframes courage as trained composure on the Daily Stoic podcast: there are some situations where panic is not an option. And CAA co-founder Michael Ovitz, via David Senra's Founders interview, supplies the operator's version — "fear is the killer and enemy of business" — defeated by relentless follow-up, a deep curiosity about everything, and showing up at 6:30 for a 9:30 start. The convergence: all three name fear as the adversary, and each defeats it the same way — with a prior commitment (to what's right, to the procedure, to the standard) that the fear is not permitted to overrule.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Daily Stoic · Ryan Holiday · "It Is A Lonely Thing" · 29 May 2026

"Many People Knew" — Holiday on the Bystander as the Default, and the Lonely Stand as the Thing Courage and Justice Actually Demand

Holiday's Friday entry opens on the single observation that the contemporary professional cohort most reliably files under someone else's problem. "Many people knew. They knew they were flirting with danger. They knew he had designs. They knew that these were compromises that violated Rome's traditions. They knew he would never be satisfied. But they didn't do anything. They didn't say anything or try to stop it. Because they weren't sure how it was going to go down." The structural point is that the failure was not a failure of perception — the senators who watched Caesar's rise saw it accurately. It was a failure of action under uncertainty: they declined to act not because they were fooled, but because the outcome was unclear and the safe move was to wait and see. The waiting-and-seeing is the part the operator recognises, because it is the same calculation that governs the meeting where everyone privately knows the decision is wrong and no one says so.

The contrast Holiday draws is the load-bearing move of the entry, and it is built from named individuals rather than abstractions. It is the story of the bystanders — but it is not the story of Cato, who stood against Caesar; not the story of Thrasea Paetus and Helvidius Priscus, the Stoic senators who stood against Nero and paid with exile and their lives; and not the story of Thomas Wentworth Higginson. The Higginson detail is the one most worth the senior practitioner's attention, because it collapses the distance between the philosophy and the action. Higginson was the nineteenth-century translator of Epictetus — and, radicalised by the 1850 Fugitive Slave Act, he helped found the Boston Vigilance Committee and, in 1854, helped lead an armed assault on a federal courthouse in an attempt to free the fugitive slave Anthony Burns, an effort in which a deputy was killed. The man who rendered the Enchiridion into English did not treat Stoicism as a private consolation; he treated it as an instruction to act, at considerable personal risk, against a wrong his society had legalised.

"These were lonely positions. They were not, at first, successful positions, either. But that's not what a Stoic thinks about. They think about what's right. They don't ask, 'Is this safe?' They say, 'That's wrong.' And they say this loudly and repeatedly, even if it's unpopular, even if they're threatened for it." — Ryan Holiday, "It Is A Lonely Thing" (Daily Stoic, 29 May 2026)

The doctrinal frame Holiday closes on is the one that distinguishes this entry from a generic exhortation to be brave. The lonely stand is not optional heroism; it is what two of the four cardinal Stoic virtues — courage and justice — demand. The reframing matters because the contemporary cohort has quietly demoted both virtues to the status of dispositions one admires in others rather than obligations one is bound by. Holiday's correction is that courage and justice are not temperamental gifts the lucky are born with; they are duties, and a duty that is only discharged when it is safe and popular is not a duty at all. The operator's honest question, run against the entry, is uncomfortable: what is the thing I currently know is wrong, that I have declined to say loudly and repeatedly, because I am not sure how it is going to go down?

Daily Stoic Podcast · Senator Mark Kelly · via "Your Week with Daily Stoic" · 30 May 2026

"Panic Is Not An Option" — Mark Kelly on Courage as Trained Composure, and the Discipline That Runs When Fear Arrives

The week's podcast surfaces the second face of courage, and it is the one the dramatic-stand framing of the Holiday card tends to obscure. Daily Stoic's conversation with Senator Mark Kelly is framed, in the newsletter's own words, around "what his years as a Navy pilot, test pilot, and NASA astronaut taught him about fear, focus, humility, and staying calm under pressure" — and the line the newsletter draws out is the operative one: "There are some situations where panic is not an option." Kelly is an unusually credentialed source on the claim. He flew 39 combat missions in Operation Desert Storm, graduated from the U.S. Naval Test Pilot School, logged more than 5,000 hours in over 50 aircraft with 375-plus carrier landings, and as a NASA astronaut commanded the final flight of the Space Shuttle Endeavour in 2011. Each of those environments shares a defining property: the cost of a panicked response is not embarrassment but death, and the margin for it is measured in seconds.

The insight the contemplative literature most often misses — and that the aviator's discipline makes concrete — is that composure under pressure is not a personality trait but a trained substitution. The combat pilot and the astronaut do not defeat panic by feeling less fear; they defeat it by having installed, through thousands of repetitions, a procedure that the fear is not permitted to interrupt. When the warning light comes on, the trained response is not deliberation but the checklist: fly the aircraft, identify the problem, run the procedure, take the next correct action. The fear is still present; it has simply been denied the steering. This is the structural cousin of the Stoic dichotomy of control — the relentless narrowing of attention onto the next action that is actually the operator's to take — and it is the reason the Stoic and the test pilot arrive at the same composure by different routes.

The biographical fact that gives the lesson its weight is one the entry treats with restraint, and so will this. In January 2011, while Kelly was training to command Endeavour's final mission, his wife, then-Congresswoman Gabby Giffords, was shot and gravely wounded; Kelly went on to command the May launch months later. Whatever else can be said about that period, it is an unembellished demonstration that the composure in question is not the absence of catastrophe but the capacity to keep flying the aircraft through it. For the operator whose pressures are, by comparison, trivial — the launch, the board meeting, the difficult quarter — the transferable instruction is exact: composure is not summoned in the moment of fear; it is installed beforehand, in the rehearsal of the procedure, so that there is something for the fear to fail to override.

Newsletter · David Senra · Founders · "My Favorite Ideas From My Conversation With Michael Ovitz" · 28 May 2026

"Fear Is The Killer And Enemy Of Business" — Ovitz on the Operator's War Against Fear, Won by Relentlessness, Curiosity, and the Kept Word

David Senra's interview with Michael Ovitz — the co-founder of Creative Artists Agency, who built the firm in 1975 after being fired from the William Morris mailroom and ran it as the most feared agency in Hollywood until 1995 — supplies the third face of the week's theme, and it names the adversary explicitly. "Fear is the killer and enemy of business." Ovitz's answer to fear is not composure or moral clarity; it is sheer asymmetric effort, and the most-quoted line of the conversation is the one that operationalises it. "How did I distinguish myself from the 20 or 25 other people in the mailroom? Everyone showed up at 9:00 for a 9:30 start. Everyone showed up except me. I showed up at 6:30." Paired with it: "I'm going to learn everything I can faster than anyone else." The mechanism is not talent but a willingness to pay, in hours and preparation, a price the competition has decided is unreasonable — which is precisely why it works.

The two operating principles beneath the relentlessness are the parts most transferable out of the Hollywood context. The first is curiosity as a structural edge: "The most interesting people are the most interested," and "if you understand how to have a deep curiosity about everything — and I mean everything — you have an edge that cannot be beaten." The second is a near-verbatim restatement of the Stoic "conversations with the dead" the Praxis examined two issues ago: "Our agents knew history because the past is prologue. If you know history, you can predict the future. The more frame of reference you have, the more prepared you are to make difficult decisions because you've seen outcomes." Ovitz's edge and the Stoic's inherited counsel turn out to be the same instrument viewed from two trades: the accumulated frame of reference that lets the practitioner recognise a situation he has, in effect, seen before. And underneath both sits the integrity constraint that made CAA function at all: "When we were starting CAA we didn't even have a lawyer. We were making hundreds of deals with no contracts, so people had to keep their word."

"I'm interested in being the best at whatever I get into, to the point of it probably not being healthy. … I've never seen a guardrail I don't try to jump." — Michael Ovitz, quoted in David Senra, Founders (28 May 2026)

The card carries its own warning, and the honest reading requires stating it. Ovitz's relentlessness is the same trait the Praxis has spent recent issues qualifying: the unhealthy edge, the guardrail jumped, the 6:30 arrival that has no off-switch. Run against the Stoic frame of the other two cards, the Ovitz material is a study in a single virtue — courage-as-relentlessness — pursued without the temperance that would govern it. The operator's correct extraction is therefore selective rather than wholesale: the relentless follow-up ("Follow-up was the key to everything. Relentless follow-up. Relentless."), the deep curiosity, the inherited frame of reference, and the kept word are durable and exportable. The bottomless ambition that admits no end — "I don't want there to be an end to ambition, or enthusiasm, or curiosity" — is the cautionary half, and the operator who imports it without the temperance check imports the cost along with the capability.

The three cards converge on a single adversary, and the convergence is worth naming precisely. Holiday names fear as the question "Is this safe?" that keeps the bystander silent. Kelly names it as the panic that, untrained, takes the controls in the cockpit. Ovitz names it outright as "the killer and enemy of business." And each defeats it the same structural way: not by feeling less fear, but by installing a prior commitment that the fear is not permitted to overrule. For the Stoic it is the commitment to what is right ("they don't ask, 'Is this safe?'"); for the aviator it is the commitment to the procedure (the checklist that runs when the warning light comes on); for the operator it is the commitment to the standard (the 6:30 arrival, the relentless follow-up, the kept word). This is the same architecture James Clear's "live to a standard, not the circumstances of the day" described two issues ago, now stress-tested against the three environments where fear is loudest. The week's compression: courage is rarely the absence of fear and never the dramatic feeling of bravery — it is a commitment made in advance, in calmer conditions, that the fear arrives too late to revise.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · From Daily Stoic · "It Is A Lonely Thing"

The Stated Objection: A Standing Practice for Converting the Private "Someone Should Say Something" Into the Spoken One

The Holiday entry converts into a discipline calibrated against the specific failure mode it diagnoses — accurate perception paired with declined action under uncertainty. The structure has four steps:

  • Name the thing you currently know is wrong and have declined to say. Not the abstract injustice; the concrete one inside the operator's actual reach — the decision in the meeting, the practice on the team, the compromise everyone privately acknowledges. The Holiday diagnostic is that the bystander's silence is almost never caused by ignorance; it is caused by the unresolved "I'm not sure how it's going to go down." Writing the thing down is the practice's falsifiable first step: the operator who cannot name one has either nothing to object to or has stopped noticing, and the second is the more common.
  • Separate the two questions the bystander fuses: "Is this safe?" and "Is this right?" Holiday's whole entry turns on the observation that the Stoic refuses to let the first question answer the second. The practice is to answer them on paper as two distinct lines — the honest assessment of the risk, and the honest assessment of the rightness — precisely so the operator can see when the risk assessment has been quietly doing the work the rightness assessment should be doing. The fusion is the mechanism of complicity; the separation is its solvent.
  • State the objection once, plainly, on the record, without the cushioning that converts it back into silence. "Loudly and repeatedly" is Holiday's phrase, but the first instance is the hard one. The discipline is to make the objection legible — a sentence in the meeting, an email that exists, a position that is now attributable — rather than the deniable hedge that lets the operator feel he objected while leaving no trace that he did. The test of whether an objection was actually stated is whether a third party could later point to it; the hedge fails that test by design.
  • Pre-accept that the position may be lonely and may not, at first, succeed. The entry is explicit that the principled stands "were not, at first, successful positions, either." The practice's durability depends on the operator having decided in advance that the worth of the objection is not contingent on its immediate reception. Cato lost. Thrasea and Helvidius died. Higginson's raid failed and Burns was returned. The point of the practice is not that the stand wins; it is that the operator's self-respect, and the slow shift of what becomes sayable for others, are produced by the stand regardless of whether it wins.

The Checklist Against Panic: A Practice for Installing the Procedure Before the Pressure, So There Is Something for Fear to Fail to Override

The aviator's composure converts into a portable discipline for the operator whose cockpit is a launch, a negotiation, or a crisis. The structure has four steps:

  • Identify the two or three situations in which your panicked response would be most expensive. The pilot does not rehearse for every contingency; he rehearses for the ones where the cost of a wrong reflex is catastrophic. The operator's analogue is the short list of high-stakes, low-time-margin moments — the system outage, the hostile question in the room, the deal about to collapse — where the instinctive response is reliably worse than the considered one. The list is short by design; a checklist for everything is a checklist for nothing.
  • Write the procedure that will run in those moments, in advance, in calm conditions. "Fly the aircraft first" is the aviator's prime directive: stabilise before diagnosing, diagnose before acting. The operator writes the equivalent — the first three actions, in order, that the situation requires irrespective of how it feels. The procedure must be written when the operator is calm, because its entire value is that it encodes the judgment of the calm self for use by the frightened one.
  • Rehearse it until it is reflexive, not merely known. Kelly's 375-plus carrier landings are the point: the procedure that has been run only on paper will not survive contact with real fear. The operator rehearses through simulation, pre-mortem, and the deliberate running of the procedure in lower-stakes versions of the situation, until the response is installed below the level of deliberation. "There are some situations where panic is not an option" is not an act of will in the moment; it is the dividend of repetitions made long before the moment.
  • In the moment, narrow attention to the single next correct action. The trained response to overwhelm is not to take in the whole catastrophe but to execute the next item on the list, then the next. This is the dichotomy of control rendered as procedure: the operator cannot control the emergency, only the next action that is his to take. The fear is permitted to be present; it is not permitted to choose what happens next. That distinction, installed in advance, is the entire transferable content of the aviator's calm.
Practice · From Michael Ovitz · via David Senra, Founders

The 6:30 Discipline: A Practice for Building the Ovitz Edge — Asymmetric Preparation and Relentless Follow-Up — With the Temperance Governor Installed

The Ovitz material converts into an operating discipline calibrated to import the capability without the cost. The structure has four steps:

  • Choose the one arena where you will pay the asymmetric price, and confine it. Ovitz's 6:30 arrival worked because it was a deliberate, located bet on a specific edge — not an undifferentiated commitment to work more everywhere. The operator selects the single domain in which out-preparing the field actually compounds, and pays the asymmetric price there. The confinement is the temperance governor: the unhealthy version of Ovitz's relentlessness is the one with no boundary, the 6:30 that metastasises into every hour. The bet is powerful precisely because it is bounded.
  • Install relentless follow-up as a system, not a virtue. "Follow-up was the key to everything. Relentless follow-up. Relentless." The operator who relies on remembering to follow up will not; the one who builds the tracking system — the open loops logged, the commitments surfaced, the next contact scheduled — will. Ovitz's relentlessness was, in operating terms, a refusal to let loops close themselves through neglect; the exportable form of it is a mechanism, not a feeling.
  • Treat deep curiosity as a research practice with an output. "The most interesting people are the most interested." The operating version is the deliberate accumulation of frame of reference — the histories, the analogous situations, the outcomes already played out elsewhere — that lets the practitioner recognise a situation he has effectively seen before. This is the same "past is prologue" instrument the Stoic calls inherited counsel; the operator builds it by reading widely and on purpose, and the dividend is faster, better decisions under the difficulty where frame of reference is scarcest.
  • Make the kept word non-negotiable, and audit it. CAA ran on handshake deals because the word was the collateral. The operator's analogue is the deliberate decision that commitments are kept by default and that "I don't know, I'll call you back" replaces the confident guess. The integrity constraint is not moralism; it is the lowest-cost trust infrastructure available, and the operator who keeps his word builds a reputation that does work no contract can. Audit it the way Ovitz implicitly did: by counting the commitments dropped, not the ones honoured.

The three practices address the same adversary at three altitudes. The stated objection defeats the fear that keeps the operator silent when he is the one who should speak (Holiday); the checklist against panic defeats the fear that takes the controls when the margin is seconds (Kelly); the 6:30 discipline defeats the fear that the competition has already decided not to pay the price of out-preparing (Ovitz). Each works by the same move the week's cards isolate: a commitment installed in advance — to the rightness, to the procedure, to the standard — that the fear arrives too late to revise. The senior practitioner's integration is to notice which of the three fears is currently most expensive in his own life: the one he is paying for in unspoken objections, the one he is paying for in panicked reflexes, or the one he is paying for in preparation the field is willing to do and he is not. The practice that matters most this quarter is the one attached to the fear he has been most successfully avoiding naming.

Foundational Text

Epictetus — The Works of Epictetus: Discourses and Enchiridion, trans. Thomas Wentworth Higginson (first published 1865)

The most resonant resource of the week, because the translator is the example. The English Epictetus that the abolitionist Thomas Wentworth Higginson produced in 1865 is the same text the Daily Stoic card builds on — and Higginson is the man who, radicalised by the 1850 Fugitive Slave Act, helped found the Boston Vigilance Committee and in 1854 helped lead the armed attempt to free the fugitive slave Anthony Burns from a Boston federal courthouse. Reading his Epictetus is reading the philosophy through the hands of someone who treated it as an instruction to act. The translation is in the public domain and freely available via the Internet Archive and the Online Library of Liberty; for the operator who wants the un-paraphrased source behind the week's central card, it is both the primary text and a standing reminder that Stoicism was, for at least one of its translators, anything but quietist.

04 On the Horizon
Moral Courage

The Bystander Problem Re-Emerging as a Leadership Theme, Not a Historical One

Holiday's "It Is A Lonely Thing" is one node in a visible re-foregrounding of moral courage — the willingness to state the unpopular objection — as an operating capability rather than a historical virtue. The contemporary professional cohort has spent a decade optimising for the opposite reflex: the read-the-room adaptability that reliably converts private disagreement into public silence. The corrective theme, gathering across the contemplative and leadership literature, is that the institutions and teams that fail rarely fail for lack of people who saw the problem; they fail for lack of people who said so on the record. Expect "who will state the objection" to become an explicit, discussed property of teams — and the bystander calculation ("I'm not sure how it's going to go down") to be named as the specific failure it is, rather than tolerated as ordinary prudence.

Composure

The "Calm Operator" as the Counter-Ideal to Always-On Anxiety

The Mark Kelly material points at a quiet shift in what the high-performance literature treats as the mark of capability. The reigning model has been intensity — the always-on, maximally-responsive operator. The emerging counter-ideal is composure: the practitioner whose distinguishing trait under pressure is not speed but the trained calm that runs a procedure while everyone else reacts. The aviation and spaceflight framing is doing real work here because it supplies a vocabulary — checklists, "fly the aircraft," the next correct action — that translates cleanly into knowledge work and resists the dismissal that contemplative advice usually attracts. Watch for "composure under pressure" to be increasingly framed as installable through rehearsal rather than innate, which is the claim that makes it a skill rather than a temperament.

Relentlessness vs. Balance

The Ovitz Ethos Colliding With the Anti-Hustle Consensus

The Ovitz material lands into an unresolved tension the operator class has been managing for years: the relentlessness ethos ("I showed up at 6:30," "to the point of it probably not being healthy") versus the anti-hustle, sustainability-first consensus that displaced it. The interesting development is not that one wins, but that the more careful synthesis — visible in this week's own card and in Holiday's "Tom Junod" counter-read — is learning to separate the exportable components of relentlessness (asymmetric preparation in a confined arena, relentless follow-up as a system, the kept word) from the pathological whole (the ambition that admits no end). The horizon theme is the maturing of that separation: less "hustle vs. balance" as a binary, more a deliberate harvesting of the operator-greats' methods with the temperance governor explicitly installed.

Fear

Fear as the Cross-Domain Adversary the Disciplines Are All Built Against

The week's quiet convergence — the Stoic's "Is this safe?", the aviator's panic, the operator's "killer and enemy of business" — points at a useful reframing of a wide swathe of practical-wisdom advice: that courage, composure, and relentlessness are not three separate virtues but three disciplines built against a single adversary, fear, in three different theatres. The unifying insight, and the one worth tracking as it propagates, is structural rather than motivational: each discipline works not by reducing fear but by installing, in advance and in calm conditions, a commitment the fear is not permitted to overrule. As that frame spreads, expect the better practical-philosophy writing to stop treating "be brave" as an exhortation and start treating it as an engineering problem — what commitment do I install now, so that fear arrives too late to revise it?
