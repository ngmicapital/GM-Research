Nobody has ever seen a European eel reproduce — not Aristotle, not Freud, not a single scientist in 2,000 years of trying. The answer, only confirmed in 2022, involves a 6,000-kilometre open-ocean migration, a transparent leaf-shaped larva that drifts for years on the Gulf Stream, and a population that has collapsed by 95% without most people noticing.

TL;DR

For roughly 2,350 years — from Aristotle to the twentieth century — nobody knew where eels came from. They appeared in rivers in spring, grew fat over decades, then vanished into the sea in autumn. No one had ever found an eel with visible sex organs, seen them mate, or discovered their eggs. Aristotle concluded they generated spontaneously from mud. In 1876, a 19-year-old Sigmund Freud spent four weeks in a Trieste laboratory dissecting four hundred eels trying to find their testicles — his first scientific publication was the inconclusive result. The truth, pieced together between 1904 and 1922 and only directly confirmed in 2022, is this: every European eel swims 6,000 kilometres to a patch of open ocean in the western Atlantic called the Sargasso Sea, spawns once, and dies — and its larvae drift back on the Gulf Stream as transparent leaf-shaped creatures that look nothing like eels, for up to three years, before metamorphosing and entering European rivers. The species is now critically endangered. Its population has fallen by around 95 percent since the 1980s. And no scientist has ever witnessed one reproduce.

Section 01

The Story: The Animal That Defeated Two Thousand Years of Curiosity

Aristotle was not a credulous man. He dissected hundreds of animals, described their anatomy with extraordinary precision, and built the foundational taxonomy of the natural world. But eels stopped him cold. In Historia Animalium, written around 350 BCE, he reached the only conclusion his evidence permitted: "Eels are derived from the so-called 'earth's guts' that grow spontaneously in mud and in humid ground." He noted that eels had been seen emerging from earthworms, and that they reappeared in marshy pools after rain — both true observations, both leading to the wrong conclusion. The reason his conclusion was wrong was that eels genuinely have no visible reproductive organs during the years they spend in freshwater. He was not being careless. There was simply nothing to find.

That explanation stood, essentially unchallenged in European natural philosophy, for the next two millennia. Medieval naturalists added refinements — eels hatched from dew, or from horsehairs fallen in water, or from the friction of two adult eels rubbing together — but the core mystery remained intact. Nobody had found eel eggs. Nobody had found eel sperm. Nobody had ever seen two eels in the act of reproduction. The creature was common, edible, economically important, and completely opaque.

The first crack appeared in 1874, when a Polish anatomist named Szymon Syrski announced he had found a small lobed organ in eels he believed were male reproductive glands — the so-called "Syrski organ." If confirmed, it would finally prove eels reproduced sexually like other fish and break the Aristotelian spell. His mentor, the Darwinist zoologist Carl Claus at the University of Vienna, needed verification. He dispatched his 19-year-old student to the zoological research station in Trieste to do it.

"Over four weeks, Freud dissected approximately 400 eels, working amid stench and slime for long hours. He found nothing conclusive. His first scientific publication was the inconclusive result."

— Freud's 1877 paper: Observations on the Form and the Finer Structure of the Lobular Organs of the Eel

The 19-year-old was Sigmund Freud. In March 1876, before he was a psychologist, before he had developed any theory of the unconscious, Freud arrived in Trieste with a scalpel and a single assignment: find a male eel. He couldn't. His published paper, his first scientific work, neither confirmed nor denied that the Syrski organ was testicular tissue. The deeper irony, only understood later: eel sex is not genetically determined at birth. Young eels have no visible gonads at all — sex is assigned later in life by environmental triggers. Freud was cutting open creatures that hadn't yet decided what they were. He moved on, eventually, to study the brainstem, then the human psyche. The eel moved on too.

The man who eventually cracked the geographic mystery was a Danish biologist named Johannes Schmidt. Beginning in 1904, he began trawling the Atlantic and collecting leptocephalus larvae — the oceanic juvenile stage of eels, which at that point were still not firmly established as eel larvae at all. His method was elegant: measure the larvae, note their location, and reason backwards. The smallest larvae must be closest to the spawning ground. Over seventeen years, interrupted by the First World War, Schmidt's expeditions moved steadily westward across the Atlantic, finding ever-smaller larvae as they went, until he reached a patch of warm, calm ocean in the western Atlantic — the Sargasso Sea — where the larvae were almost microscopic. He published his conclusion in Nature in 1922: every European eel hatches in the Sargasso Sea.

Schmidt never saw adult eels spawning. He never found eggs. His entire conclusion rested on inferring origin from larval distribution — a brilliant piece of reverse engineering. It was accepted, celebrated, and remained unconfirmed by direct observation for exactly a hundred years. The 2022 satellite-tagging study — which tracked 26 silver eels from the Azores, six of which transmitted data confirming arrival in the Sargasso — provided the first direct evidence that adult European eels actually make it there. The act of spawning itself has never been observed, in any species of the genus Anguilla, anywhere on Earth.

Section 02

The Mechanism: A Life Cycle So Strange It Was Rejected as Impossible

The European eel's life cycle is among the most improbable of any vertebrate. It has six distinct phases, each involving a radical physical transformation, and the whole thing is structured around a single reproductive event that occurs once, costs the animal its life, and happens in a location 6,000 kilometres from where the eel spent the previous decade or two quietly living in a river.

The Six Phases of an Eel's Life

1. Spawning (Sargasso Sea). Adult eels arrive in the western Atlantic between roughly February and April. Spawning is believed to occur at depth. Nobody has seen it. After spawning, both parents die.

2. Leptocephalus larvae. Eggs hatch into transparent, flat, willow-leaf-shaped creatures called leptocephali — utterly unrecognisable as eels. They drift passively on the Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Drift for 17 to 28 months, travelling up to 9,000 km across the open ocean, feeding on dissolved organic matter and marine snow.

3. Glass eels. As larvae approach European continental shelves, they metamorphose into tiny, transparent, eel-shaped juveniles called glass eels. They ride tidal currents inshore and begin entering estuaries.

4. Elvers. Pigmented juveniles ascending rivers. They can climb damp stone walls, cross wet grass, and wriggle through saturated sand to reach headwater ponds. Individually, they weigh less than a gram.

5. Yellow eels. The long residential phase. Eels settle in freshwater — or brackish, or even marine — habitats and feed for anywhere between 6 and 25 years. Females generally stay longer. They are mostly nocturnal and cryptic. A large female can exceed a metre in length.

6. Silver eels. The final transformation. Eyes enlarge for deep-sea vision. Flanks turn silver-white. Fat reserves massively increase. Digestive organs atrophy — the eel will never eat again. Gonads begin developing for the first and only time. The eel stops feeding, moves downstream, enters the sea, and swims to the Sargasso.

The sex determination mechanism is one of the strangest facts in vertebrate biology. European eels are not born male or female. Sex is assigned during the freshwater phase, apparently triggered by population density: low-density environments, where food is plentiful and competition low, tend to produce females (larger body, more eggs); high-density environments tend to produce males. A young eel has no gonadal tissue whatsoever in any developed form. This is why Aristotle found nothing, why Freud found nothing, and why the 19th century spent decades arguing about the Syrski organ. There was nothing to find, because the organs hadn't formed yet.

The navigation mechanism is equally perplexing. A silver eel leaving a river in the south of England somehow crosses the North Atlantic to a specific patch of warm Sargasso Sea water — a journey of six thousand kilometres through open ocean with no landmarks, no prior experience of the route, and no living guide (its parents are dead). Research has demonstrated that glass eels possess a magnetic compass linked to the tidal cycle, which helps them navigate into estuaries. Silver eels appear to use a combination of magnetic sensing, current-following, and possibly olfactory cues. The full mechanism is not understood. The eel simply knows where to go.

One more detail earns its place here: once a silver eel begins its final migration, its digestive system degenerates completely. It runs the entire 6,000-kilometre journey on stored fat alone. This is physiologically extreme — broadly equivalent to a human running at marathon pace for a year on nothing but body reserves. The eel's body has, in effect, pre-digested its own intestines to fuel the last journey.

Section 03

The Connections: A 95% Collapse That Almost Nobody Has Noticed

The European eel was listed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN in 2008. Its population — measured by glass eel recruitment arriving at European coasts — has fallen to somewhere between 2 and 15 percent of its 1960–1979 baseline, depending on the monitoring site. Some rivers that were historically thick with eels show declines of 93 to 99 percent over the monitoring period. The overall population is estimated to have fallen by around 95 percent since the 1980s.

This is a collapse comparable in scale to the most dramatic vertebrate crashes in modern conservation history. It has attracted relatively little public attention, partly because eels are cryptic, partly because they are not charismatic in the way dolphins or tigers are, and partly because the collapse happened slowly enough — across four decades — that each generation adjusted its expectations downward without registering the cumulative loss.

The Three Main Causes

  • River barriers. Europe has roughly 1.2 million river barriers — weirs, dams, sluices, culverts. Glass eels cannot ascend most of them. Silver eels descending are killed in hydroelectric turbines. A barrier at any point on the river disconnects all habitat above it.
  • Anguillicola crassus. A parasitic nematode worm native to Japanese eels, accidentally introduced to Europe in 1982 via aquaculture imports. Colonises and scars the swimbladder. An eel with a heavily parasitised swimbladder cannot regulate buoyancy at depth — it likely cannot complete the Atlantic migration.
  • Overfishing. Glass eels are a delicacy in Spain and Portugal, commanding prices up to €1,000/kg. Hundreds of millions of juveniles are removed before they can mature. Adult yellow and silver eels are also commercially fished across Europe.

What We Still Don't Know

  • Nobody has ever directly observed European eels spawning — not once, not ever, in any controlled or wild setting.
  • The exact spawning ground within the Sargasso Sea is unconfirmed — a 2020 paper proposed the Mid-Atlantic Ridge as an alternative or supplementary site.
  • The full navigation mechanism for both the outbound adult migration and the inbound larval drift is not understood.
  • The environmental triggers that cause a yellow eel to silver — after 6 to 25 years of waiting — are not fully characterised.

The worm deserves special attention because it illustrates how globalisation can destroy an ecosystem quietly and indirectly. Anguillicola crassus is harmless to its native host, the Japanese eel, which evolved alongside it. When Japanese eels began to be imported into European aquaculture in the early 1980s — because Japanese eel stocks were also declining and European eels were considered a viable substitute — the worm came with them. First detected in northern Germany in 1982, it spread across European eel populations within a decade. European eels had no immune response to it. The worm colonised their swimbladders, causing inflammation, haemorrhaging, and permanent scarring. An eel whose swimbladder has been repeatedly infected may be physically incapable of the pressure adjustments required for a deep-sea Atlantic crossing. The parasite, in other words, may be preventing already-weakened eels from completing the only reproductive event of their lives.

The connection to the mystery is direct: an animal this poorly understood is also an animal very difficult to protect. Conservation efforts — eel passes at weirs, closed seasons, restocking programmes — have been implemented across Europe since the 2000s. Glass eel recruitment has not meaningfully recovered. Whether the eel's life cycle, which requires a 6,000-kilometre open-ocean migration it completes exactly once before dying, can survive the accumulated pressures of fragmented rivers, parasitic infestation, industrial fishing, and changing ocean temperatures is genuinely unknown. The species has outlasted the dinosaurs. It may not outlast the weir.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

Åle, the Immortal Well Eel

In the village of Brantevik in southern Sweden, a well eel named Åle was reportedly introduced in 1859 and was still alive in 2014 — potentially 155 years old. The longevity claim is disputed, but European eels in low-productivity northern habitats genuinely can spend 25+ years in freshwater without silvering. The biology supports extreme ages.

The Sargasso Sea Itself

The Sargasso is the only sea on Earth bounded not by land but by ocean currents — the Gulf Stream to the west, the North Atlantic Current to the north. Its warm, calm, nutrient-poor water has made it an ecological oddity: home to floating sargassum seaweed mats, to the larval stages of multiple eel species, and to virtually no commercial fish.

Freud's Trajectory

After failing to find eel gonads, Freud studied the nervous systems of crayfish and lampreys, then the human brainstem. He was a skilled anatomist who gave up laboratory science not from lack of ability but from lack of money — he couldn't afford to marry on an academic salary. Clinical practice was the financially viable path. The eel redirected the history of psychology.

Eels and Electric Fields

European eels can detect electric fields in the microvolt range. This sensitivity is now a conservation concern: submarine power cables generate electromagnetic fields that disrupt eel migration behaviour, causing them to stop, reverse direction, or choose wrong routes. Offshore wind expansion and subsea cabling have added a fourth pressure to an already-overwhelmed species.

The American Eel Problem

The American eel (Anguilla rostrata) is thought to share the Sargasso Sea spawning ground with the European eel — two species, two continents, one patch of ocean. How larvae of each species end up on the correct continent, drifting passively on currents that don't obviously sort them by destination, remains poorly understood. One leading hypothesis: hatching timing and depth differences place the two species on subtly different current trajectories.
