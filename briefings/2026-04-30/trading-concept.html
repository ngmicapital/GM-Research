Alpha — Footprint Charts

Thursday · 30 April 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #8 11-min read
TL;DR

A footprint chart is a candle with the autopsy attached — every price level inside the bar shows bid volume on the left and ask volume on the right, so you read who actually traded where, not just OHLC. Stacked imbalances (3+ adjacent levels with one side ≥3× the other diagonally) mark conviction zones; absorption (heavy aggressive volume that fails to move price) marks reversals; delta-vs-price divergence inside a single candle is the cleanest exhaustion signal in any chart type. In a range like BTC's $76K–$78K week, footprint is the only tool that tells you whether the boundary holders are real or about to fold.

§01
What It Is
A candle's autopsy report · bid volume left, ask volume right, every tick
A footprint chart explodes a single candle into a vertical stack of price levels and prints the bid-side and ask-side volume at each level — giving you the full record of aggression inside the bar instead of just the four OHLC values.

Standard candles compress every trade in a 5-minute window into four numbers. Footprint refuses the compression. For each candle, every price the tape touched gets its own row, and that row carries two numbers: volume traded at the bid (aggressive sellers hitting passive buyers) on the left, and volume traded at the ask (aggressive buyers lifting passive sellers) on the right. The candle becomes a stacked grid — a fingerprint, hence the name.

This is the closest a charting product gets to watching the tape directly. Where Cumulative Volume Delta gives you one summary line for the whole bar, footprint shows you where inside the bar the delta came from — and that location is everything. A +500 delta candle whose buying happened at the high (chasing) reads completely differently from a +500 delta candle whose buying happened at the low (defending). Both look identical on a CVD strip; they look opposite on a footprint.

The format originated in the futures pits — Trevor Harnett's Market Delta coined the term "footprint" in the early 2000s — and has migrated wholesale into crypto via Bookmap, Exocharts, ATAS, Cignals, TradingLite, Quantower, and TradingView's native cluster type. Bookmap's framing for the crypto desk is blunt: footprint lets you "watch algo activity, spoofing orders, aggressive market buying and selling, and price absorption and exhaustion, in real-time". That's the whole pitch.

Tony, in your Wyckoff vocabulary: footprint is where you confirm or invalidate effort vs result at the bar level. A spring with heavy bid-side volume that fails to mark down price is the textbook absorption read; a markup that runs out of ask-side prints two ticks before the high is the textbook exhaustion read. The footprint puts the receipts on the candle.

BID VOLUMEAggressive sellers — market sells eating passive bids (left side) ASK VOLUMEAggressive buyers — market buys lifting passive asks (right side) DELTAAsk − Bid at a level (or summed for the whole candle) POCPoint of Control — price level inside the candle with the most volume IMBALANCEOne side ≥3× the diagonal opposite — flagged automatically STACKED IMBThree or more imbalances on the same side at adjacent levels
If you only remember one thing OHLC tells you what happened. Footprint tells you who did it. The two sides of a price level are not symmetric — one is aggressive, the other passive — and that asymmetry, repeated across levels, is the signal everyone else's chart is hiding from them.
§02
Mechanics
Bid-vs-ask classification · the imbalance ratio · four chart variants
Each trade is tagged bid-side or ask-side by the price it crossed; the engine bins those tags by candle and price level; an imbalance flag fires when one side hits a configurable multiple (default 300%) of the diagonal opposite on the next level up or down.

The classification is mechanical. When a market order crosses the spread upward, it lifts an existing limit ask — that volume is logged as ask-side (a buyer was the aggressor). When a market order crosses downward, it hits an existing limit bid — that volume is logged as bid-side (a seller was the aggressor). Trades that occur exactly at mid-price during fast tape (the "trade-at-mid" edge case) are usually classified by tick rule: same direction as the previous classified print.

Once classified, every trade lands in a bucket defined by (candle, price level). At end of bar — or in real time, refreshing tick-by-tick — the engine writes the two numbers into the row, computes the delta for the row, and sums the deltas for the bar.

Per-level delta: delta(p, c) = ask_volume(p, c) − bid_volume(p, c) Bar delta: delta(c) = Σ delta(p, c) for all p in candle c Imbalance flag (Trader Dale / industry default): ASK imbalance at level p when: ask_volume(p) ≥ R × bid_volume(p − 1 tick) BID imbalance at level p when: bid_volume(p) ≥ R × ask_volume(p + 1 tick) where R = 3.0 (300%) by default, often raised to 4.0 for HF crypto Stacked imbalance: ≥ 3 imbalances on the same side at consecutive price levels → marked as a strong support (BID stack) or resistance (ASK stack) zone
Note the diagonal: an aggressive buyer at price p has to compare to the seller one tick lower (where the chase started). Comparing same-level bid vs ask is the most common rookie misread — same level just tells you total trading at a price, not who pressed the gas.

Crypto presents two structural complications futures don't have. First, aggregated venues: the tape on Binance perp is not the tape on Coinbase spot, and most retail platforms either pick one or aggregate poorly. Cignals and Hyblock aggregate cleanly; Exocharts publishes per-venue. Second, tick granularity: BTC at $77K trades in $0.10 ticks on Coinbase but $1 ticks on Binance perp — the same footprint candle will look completely different depending on which side you're reading.

Four flavours dominate. Pick the one that matches what you're trying to see:

VariantWhat it showsBest for
Bid × AskTwo numbers per row, side by sideReading aggression at every level — the default
DeltaSingle number per row (ask − bid)Spotting per-level pressure; cleaner visual
Volume ProfileTotal volume per row, no bid/ask splitFinding POC and high-volume nodes inside the bar
Imbalance-onlyHighlights only flagged imbalance levelsRange-trading; isolating stacked-imbalance walls

Under the hood the data feed has to be unaggregated trade prints with bid/ask flags — i.e. an L1 trade tape with quote context, not a 1-minute OHLCV bar. That's why footprint is unreliable on TradingView's free crypto feed (no quote context) and reliable on Bookmap/Exocharts (direct exchange WebSocket). [Sources differ] on whether Binance's public WebSocket reports the maker side accurately for every print — most desks treat it as ~95% reliable and worth using anyway.

§04
In Practice
What to actually do with the bid/ask split during a session
Trade stacked imbalances as conviction zones, trade absorption as reversal evidence at structural levels, treat per-level delta divergence as the cleanest single-bar exhaustion signal, and ignore everything else as noise.
1

Stacked imbalances at structural levels = entries, not signals on their own

Three or more imbalances stacked on the same side become a real S/R zone only when they print at a level you'd already drawn — range high, range low, prior POC, HTF Wyckoff event. Inside the noise zone of a range, stacked imbalances are just an algorithm pinging through thin liquidity. Combine them with structure or skip the trade.

2

Absorption at the extreme is the strongest reversal evidence on a chart

If the candle that prints the new range low has both heavy bid-side (sellers) and heavy ask-side (passive buyers stepping in) volume, and price refuses to extend, that's absorption. Mind Math Money's framing: "Large passive buyers are sitting at that level with size. They are accumulating without pushing price up." Wait for the equilibrium bar then enter on the conviction-markup candle, stop just below the absorption low.

3

Delta-vs-price divergence inside one bar is the cleanest exhaustion read

Bar makes a new high, but the top one or two ticks show negative per-level delta (heavy bid prints — sellers fading the chase). This is per-bar effort-vs-result: aggressive buyers pressed and got no new tick. Fade the next bar's open if HTF supports a turn. Works on both extremes.

4

POC migration tells you who's winning the bar

The Point of Control (highest-volume row inside the candle) usually sits near the open in continuation moves and migrates toward the close in conviction moves. A candle whose POC is at the high with positive delta is healthy bullish; a candle whose POC is at the low with positive delta is a chase about to fail. Read POC location before reading the colour of the bar.

Worked example — the range-low fade
SETUPBTC trades 76,140 within a multi-day range whose low is 76,100. ETF outflows dominate the wire, funding is negative, but realised vol is contracting.
FOOTPRINT TRIGGER5-min bar prints 76,098 (one tick under the level). Bid volume at the low row is 412, ask volume 395, both extreme outliers vs the rest of the candle. No follow-through bar.
CONFIRMATIONNext bar prints inside, bar delta ≈ 0, range collapses. Third bar opens, prints 3× stacked ask imbalance from 76,170 to 76,200.
ENTRY76,160 on the conviction bar (long).
STOP76,070 (under the absorption low + buffer).
R DEFINITION~$90 risk per coin. Target range mid 77,000 = ~$840 = ~9R if it pays in full.
FIRST PARTIAL76,500 (4–5R) — VPOC of prior session.
WHAT KILLS ITTwo consecutive bars closing under 76,070 with positive bid-side delta. Cut and walk.
Timeframe fit: 1m–5m for execution and trigger reads. 15m–1h to align with intraday structure. Higher than 1h dilutes the bid/ask asymmetry — by then the candle is too coarse and CVD is the better summary.

§07
Common Traps
Where footprint readers actually lose money
Single imbalances are noise. Same-level bid-vs-ask comparison is the wrong axis. And the entire concept misfires on illiquid retail platforms with no real bid/ask classification.
1
Trading single imbalances as signals

One row hitting the 3× ratio means almost nothing in crypto, where HFT can spoof a single level in 50ms. Wait for stacks (≥3 adjacent same-side imbalances) and a structural level. Single-imbalance trades are a 50/50 with extra steps.

2
Comparing same-level bid-vs-ask instead of diagonal

The imbalance ratio is diagonal — ask at price p vs bid at price p−1tick. Aggressive buyers chase upward; the comparison has to track that. Same-level only tells you total trading at the price, which is a different question.

3
Using TradingView's free crypto footprint without verifying the data feed

TradingView's native cluster works on futures (CME, Eurex) where there's a centralised tape, and on a few crypto venues with proper L1 quote feeds. Free tier on most spot crypto pairs is unreliable — the bid/ask classification is a tick-rule approximation, not a real maker/taker split. If your platform doesn't say "trade-side classified" in its docs, treat the numbers as advisory.

4
Worshipping bar delta and ignoring per-level location

+500 delta at the high (chase) and +500 delta at the low (defence) are opposite trades. CVD averages them; footprint distinguishes them. If you're reading the delta number without checking where in the bar it came from, you've thrown away most of the information.

5
The honest "overhyped" callout — footprint sells better than it works on a chop day

On low-volatility chop tape (~70% of crypto sessions outside London/NY overlap), footprint generates more false stacked-imbalance signals than real ones. The genuine edge is concentrated in session opens, range boundaries, and post-news minutes. The rest of the day, watching footprint will hurt you more than help. The platforms selling 24/7 footprint subscriptions don't advertise that.
