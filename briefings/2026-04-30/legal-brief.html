UpdateEU AI Act Digital Omnibus Trilogue Collapses After 12-Hour Talks — August 2026 High-Risk Deadline Now the Default Path Unless 13 May Session Closes Annex I Dispute

"Big Tech is probably popping champagne. While European companies that care about safety and did their homework now face regulatory chaos." — Kim van Sparrentak MEP (Greens/EFA), reacting to the breakdown of the 28 April trilogue

The fourth and supposedly final political trilogue on the EU's AI Act Digital Omnibus collapsed late on 28 April after roughly 12 hours of negotiation between Council, Parliament and Commission representatives. The Cypriot Presidency confirmed there was "no agreement with Parliament", and a follow-up trilogue has been pencilled in for approximately 13 May — leaving the Cypriot Council Presidency only seven weeks before its term ends on 30 June and the Lithuanian Presidency takes over.

The unresolved file is Annex I — specifically the conformity-assessment architecture for AI systems embedded in products already regulated under sectoral safety law (medical devices, in-vitro diagnostics, machinery, toys, connected vehicles). Parliament wants those systems to remain inside the AI Act's high-risk perimeter; Council and parts of industry want them carved out, on the theory that double conformity assessment under Regulation 2024/1689 plus the relevant product-safety regulation is duplicative and unworkable.

The practical consequence is that the original Article 113 applicability dates in Regulation 2024/1689 remain legally in force. Without an Omnibus regulation adopted before 2 August 2026, the high-risk obligations bite on that date — including Annex III conformity assessments, fundamental-rights impact assessments for public-sector deployers, registration in the EU AI database and the full Article 26 deployer-obligation stack.

📎 Historical Precedent: Mirrors the AI Act's own 2023 trilogue endgame, when the foundation-model carve-out went to a fifth round and a December all-nighter — last-minute French/German pushback nearly killed the file before agreement was reached on tiered systemic-risk thresholds.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per the Ropes & Gray AI Omnibus tracker and DLA Piper's 28 April note, deployers and providers of high-risk systems should now plan against the 2 August 2026 default — not the December 2027 deferral the Omnibus would have created. A&O Shearman's pre-trilogue note flagged Annex I as the most likely failure point, and that prediction held. Legal teams that paused gap analyses pending Omnibus relief have lost three months of runway. The Brief's read: the political economy now favours one further attempt in May, but if Cyprus cannot close the file before 30 June, Lithuania inherits a half-cooked dossier and the Council loses the ability to negotiate across files — making the December 2027 deferral materially less likely than market consensus has assumed.

🔄 Contrarian Take

CDT Europe's April AI Bulletin argues the failure is itself a feature, not a bug — that compliance fatigue is being weaponised to roll back rights protections, and a clean 2 August activation would force regulators to do their job. The Brief's read: if the Commission tables a narrow technical fix on Annex I in the next week — rather than the full Omnibus package — Parliament could accept it without the political cost of rolling back high-risk obligations. The "all-or-nothing" framing is partly tactical.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Per OneTrust's April implementation note, US foundation-model providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta) had been scoping the December 2027 timeline; they now must accelerate Article 53 GPAI compliance documentation that was already partially in force from 2 August 2025. European industrial AI deployers — Siemens (SIE.DE), Schneider (SU.PA), ABB (ABBN.SW), Philips (PHIA.AS) and the medical-device cluster — face the worst Annex I outcome: high-risk classification on top of MDR/IVDR compliance with no harmonised conformity-assessment relief. EU cloud (OVH (OVH.PA), Aruba) and AI-governance-tech vendors (Holistic AI, Credo AI) gain near-term demand as in-house projects revert to outsourced GRC.

OKX, BlackRock and Standard Chartered Launch BUIDL Off-Exchange Collateral Framework — First G-SIB Custodian for Tokenised RWA Margin, OKX Middle East as Trading Venue

"BUIDL was designed to bring the benefits of tokenization to short term treasury exposure, allowing qualified investors to earn US dollar yields on blockchain rails. The framework with OKX and Standard Chartered allows qualified investors to unlock new opportunities in how they deploy collateral." — Samara Cohen, Global Head of Market Development, BlackRock (28 April 2026 joint announcement)

On 28 April, Standard Chartered, BlackRock and OKX announced a joint framework that allows qualified institutional clients of OKX Middle East to post BlackRock's BUIDL — the tokenised short-term US Treasury fund issued via Securitize — as off-exchange collateral, with custody held at Standard Chartered. This is the first publicly announced arrangement in which a globally systemically important bank (G-SIB) acts as the custodian inside a crypto-exchange margin workflow using a tokenised real-world asset.

The structure uses Standard Chartered's Bahrain regulated custody footprint for the asset and OKX's VARA-licensed Middle East entity for execution and margining. BUIDL collateral keeps accruing yield while serving as posted margin — collapsing the historical opportunity cost of idle stablecoin/cash collateral on centralised crypto venues. Bloomberg reported the explicit goal as targeting the "idle cash piling up on crypto exchanges", which Galaxy and a16z research have estimated at $30–50bn across major venues.

📎 Historical Precedent: Echoes CME Group's 2017 admission of treasury collateral via DTCC for crypto futures margining — institutional plumbing precedes institutional flow by 18–24 months.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per the Standard Chartered press release and Davis Polk's tokenisation tracker, the structure is built on existing Bahrain Central Bank crypto-asset rules and VARA's institutional licence — not US securities law, which still treats BUIDL as a 3(c)(7) private fund. Sidley's Data Matters note on parallel SEC/CFTC tokenisation guidance from March 2026 implies the same structure could be replicated onshore once the SEC's promised innovation exemption ships, but for now offshore is the only path. The Brief's read: the meaningful precedent is not the BUIDL listing — it is Standard Chartered taking custodial risk on tokenised collateral inside an exchange margin model, which forces every other G-SIB to decide whether to compete or cede the business to State Street and BNY's parallel projects.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The BIS MCI report (see Story #4) argues precisely this kind of vertically integrated intermediation is the systemic risk regulators should be capping, not enabling. The Brief's read: if the framework is replicated by Binance, Bybit and Coinbase International over the next two quarters, expect the FSB to add tokenised-RWA collateral to its 2027 thematic review — and for ESMA to question whether MiCA's Title V CASP rules cover off-venue collateral custody at all.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Direct beneficiaries: BlackRock (BLK) — BUIDL AUM was ~$2.5bn pre-announcement and the institutional collateral channel is the cleanest path to $10bn+; Securitize — fee on every transfer agent action; Standard Chartered (STAN.L, 2888.HK) — first-mover G-SIB premium on a category that BNY (BK), State Street (STT) and JPMorgan (JPM) all want. Pressured: tokenised-treasury competitors without a major-exchange relationship — Ondo (ONDO), Franklin Templeton's BENJI, Hashnote's USYC need their own venue deals. Stablecoin issuers — Circle (CRCL) in particular faces a structural threat: yield-bearing collateral undercuts the case for non-yield-bearing USDC float on exchanges.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish BLK, STAN.L (2888.HK), Securitize private secondaries, ONDO (if it announces a parallel deal), tokenisation infra (Chainlink LINK, RedStone).

🔴 Bearish CRCL — yield-bearing collateral cannibalises USDC exchange float; pure-play stablecoin issuers without a tokenised-treasury arm.

⚪ Neutral Coinbase (COIN), Binance — must respond, but the response is opex not revenue in the near term.

CFTC Prediction Markets ANPRM Comment Window Closes Today — 800+ Submissions Already In, Tribal/State Gaming and Polymarket/Kalshi on Opposite Sides of "Public Interest" Test

"Today's action is an important step in the Commission's continued effort to promote responsible innovation in our derivatives markets... grounded in a rational and coherent interpretation of the Commodity Exchange Act, while reassuring the American people that the CFTC will exercise its exclusive jurisdiction over prediction markets." — Chair Michael Selig, CFTC release 9194-26 (12 March 2026)

The Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (ANPRM) on event-contract derivatives — published in the Federal Register on 16 March — closes its 45-day comment window at 11:59 pm ET tonight. Per the CFTC and Norton Rose Fulbright's tracker, more than 800 submissions have been logged from industry participants, academics, state gaming commissions, tribal gaming operators, and consumer-protection advocates.

The ANPRM asks four core questions: which event contracts may be prohibited under Section 5c(c)(5)(C) as "contrary to the public interest"; how the Commission's Core Principles apply to prediction markets; the cost-benefit framing for any new rules; and the line between federally regulated event contracts and state-regulated gaming. Sidley's February note flagged this last question as the litigation flashpoint — a position vindicated by the Massachusetts/Wisconsin/38-AG amicus push covered earlier this week.

📎 Historical Precedent: Mirrors the CFTC's 2012 Nadex event-contract review, which produced the original "public interest" guidance still being argued over today — and which the current ANPRM expressly proposes to update.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Cleary Gottlieb's "Prediction Markets for Those Who Don't Predict" memo and Wilmer Hale's 17 March client alert, expect the proposed rule to land in Q3 2026 with a tiered "public interest" test — sports outcomes likely treated more conservatively than political/economic event contracts, and tribal/state gaming compact carve-outs explicitly preserved. Alston & Bird's April note suggests the CFTC is using the ANPRM to cement exclusive-jurisdiction language before any state-court adverse ruling. The Brief's read: the volume of submissions matters less than two specific filings — the National Indian Gaming Association comment and the Coalition of State Gaming Regulators comment, which together carry the political weight to move Selig's "public interest" test toward sports carve-outs even if the legal test does not require them.

🔄 Contrarian Take

SBA Office of Advocacy filed comments arguing the rulemaking should not differentiate between event categories — that consumer protection is achieved through disclosure and exchange supervision, not contract-by-contract prohibition. The Brief's read: if the Commission accepts a category-neutral test, Kalshi and Polymarket win the regulatory war; if it accepts a "public interest by category" test, sports markets get severed and litigation continues for years. Today's filings are the last chance to shape that fork.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Per The Block Research and DraftKings' (DKNG) own comment letter, traditional sportsbook operators see prediction markets as the most acute existential threat since DFS — DKNG, FanDuel (FLUT.L), Penn (PENN), Caesars (CZR) all face a federal market entrant unconstrained by state gaming compact economics. Polymarket (private), Kalshi (private) and any future CFTC-licensed event exchange capture upside if Core Principles are confirmed; Robinhood (HOOD) — which integrated Kalshi event contracts in March — gets a free option on the regulatory outcome. Tribal operators (Hard Rock, Pechanga, Mohegan) face the largest revenue risk and are funding the most aggressive opposition.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish HOOD, IBKR, COIN (event-contract integrations), Polymarket/Kalshi private secondaries.

🔴 Bearish DKNG, FLUT.L, PENN, CZR — federally licensed prediction markets erode the moat that state gaming compacts built.

⚪ Neutral CME (CME), ICE — well-positioned regardless of outcome; event contracts are a sleeve, not a core franchise.

📋 What To Do

BIS Report Names Major Crypto Exchanges "Multifunction Cryptoasset Intermediaries" — Calls for Bank-Style Capital, Liquidity and Group-Level Consolidated Supervision

"What looks like a high-yield savings product is, in reality, an unsecured loan to a lightly regulated shadow bank... the combination of high leverage, volatile collateral and vertically integrated intermediation functions makes MCIs structurally prone to cascade events under stress." — BIS, "Cryptoasset service providers as financial intermediaries: risks and policy approaches" (28 April 2026)

The BIS released a report on 28 April naming a new regulatory category — "multifunction cryptoasset intermediaries" (MCIs) — to describe the largest crypto exchanges. The paper draws on a structured review of the terms and conditions of Binance, Bybit, Coinbase, Crypto.com, Kraken, MEXC and OKX conducted between November 2025 and March 2026. It argues these venues simultaneously perform trading, custody, lending, market-making, listing and yield-product issuance — functions that in traditional finance trigger consolidated supervision and prudential rules.

The headline regulatory gap: only 11 of the 28 jurisdictions surveyed by the FSB in 2025 have a finalised framework addressing financial-stability risks from crypto platforms; only two cover borrowing and lending; only three cover earn products. The BIS recommends prudential capital and liquidity requirements, governance standards, group-level consolidated supervision and stress testing — explicitly the Basel III toolkit translated to crypto exchanges.

📎 Historical Precedent: Mirrors the BIS's 2009 G-SIFI framework that produced Basel III for banks, and the FSB's 2018 designation of CCPs as systemically important — same playbook, new institutional class.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per The Block's coverage and Linklaters' April analysis of the FSB peer review, the BIS paper is the staging document for the FSB's 2027 thematic review — it gives FSB members a shared analytical frame and a defined regulatory category to work against. Coinbase Institute's response (issued same day) called the paper "directionally reasonable but operationally unworkable" — a tell that the largest US exchange expects this to land in actual rulemaking. The Brief's read: the meaningful follow-on is which jurisdiction moves first. Switzerland (FINMA), Singapore (MAS) and Hong Kong (HKMA/SFC) are the most likely venues for MCI-style rules to ship before the FSB has consensus, because each has both crypto exchange concentration and a single integrated prudential supervisor capable of writing the rule.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Per the Cato Institute and Coin Center responses, applying Basel III to crypto exchanges fundamentally misreads the business — exchanges hold customer assets in segregated wallets (the Lehman/Celsius failure mode), not on balance sheet, so capital ratios solve the wrong problem. The Brief's read: the contrarian view is partly right and partly outdated — segregated custody addresses the Celsius failure mode, but does not address the BIS's actual concern, which is the lending/yield/market-making book where customer assets ARE rehypothecated. The right rule is a narrow earn-product capital charge, not full consolidated supervision.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Direct cost vectors: Binance and Bybit — non-listed, opaque, structurally most exposed to consolidated-supervision rules; Coinbase (COIN) — listed with disclosure already, but the lending/staking/earn book becomes a capital headwind; Kraken (private) and Crypto.com (private) face the same structural pressure pre-IPO. Beneficiaries: pure-play custodians (Anchorage, BitGo, Komainu) — the BIS framework implicitly favours functional unbundling. Banks with crypto trust charters (BNY, JPM via Kinexys, State Street) — gain a credibility advantage if MCI rules treat them differently from native exchanges.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟢 Bullish BNY (BK), STT, JPM (Kinexys), independent qualified custodians, single-purpose tokenisation issuers.

🔴 Bearish COIN — earn/lending revenue lines face capital headwinds; private crypto exchanges with planned 2026/2027 IPOs face new diligence questions.

⚪ Neutral CME (CME), ICE — already prudentially regulated; if anything a competitive moat widens.

📋 What To Do

TraderWatch COIN earn/staking revenue disclosure in next 10-Q; that line is the cleanest read on MCI capital exposure.

CounselBrief institutional clients that the FSB 2027 thematic review now has a named category to organise around — diligence questions about exchange counterparty risk just got teeth.

ComplianceIf your firm has earn-product or staking exposure, scope what a 4–8% notional capital charge would mean for unit economics — model the FINMA path first, FSB second.

FounderIf you are building an exchange in MAS, FINMA or HKMA jurisdiction, design now for functional separation between trading, lending and custody — retrofitting after rules ship costs 5–10x more.

MAS Integrates USDC and USDT into FAST Payment System — Licensed FIs Cleared to Offer Real-Time SGD/Stablecoin Conversion, $4.5bn Annual Volume Targeted by Year-End

The Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) has integrated USDT and USDC into the national Fast and Secure Transfers (FAST) system, allowing MAS-licensed financial institutions to offer instant SGD-to-stablecoin conversion through licensed channels. The pilot — covering trade-finance and cross-border-payment use cases — is sized at $4.5bn annual transaction volume by year-end 2026 per Coin Reporter and The Asian Banker. Singapore Gulf Bank's USDC mint/redeem service on Solana, launched 18 April, slots into the same architecture.

📎 Historical Precedent: Mirrors MAS Project Orchid (2023) and Project Guardian — Singapore's pattern is to integrate, then regulate, then export. PayNow→FAST→stablecoin is the same arc as PayNow→cross-border PayNow-PromptPay (2021).
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Davis Polk's MAS stablecoin tracker and Allen & Gledhill's April note, the FAST integration sits on top of MAS's 2023 Single-Currency Stablecoin (SCS) framework — but the use of USDC and USDT (neither MAS-regulated stablecoins) signals a deliberate "pragmatic gateway" approach: licensed bank intermediation substitutes for direct issuer regulation. The Brief's read: the regulatory innovation here is making the bank the perimeter, not the stablecoin — a model the UK FCA could adopt for its October 2027 regime, and one MiCA's "significant EMT" rules expressly do not allow.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Beneficiaries: Circle (CRCL) and Tether — distribution into a sovereign payment rail without direct regulatory burden; DBS (D05.SI), OCBC (O39.SI), UOB (U11.SI), Standard Chartered (STAN.L) — all in scope as licensed FIs; Singapore Gulf Bank gains first-mover credibility. Pressured: MAS-licensed SCS issuers (StraitsX XSGD) — the FAST integration favours USD-stablecoin liquidity over SGD-stablecoin development.

SEC Chair Atkins Promises Tokenised-Securities "Innovation Exemption" Within Weeks — 12-to-36 Month Sandbox for On-Chain Issuance and Trading, Reg Crypto Token-Sale Framework Following

"We are going to release the innovation exemption within weeks. Firms will be able to build and trade securitised tokens on-chain inside the United States — under supervision, with guardrails, but without the registration friction that has pushed every meaningful issuance offshore." — Chair Paul Atkins, Bitcoin Las Vegas 2026 keynote (paraphrased — full text not yet posted to SEC.gov speeches page)

SEC Chair Paul Atkins, speaking at Bitcoin Las Vegas 2026 earlier this week, said the Commission will issue an "innovation exemption" within weeks that creates a 12-to-36 month supervised sandbox for tokenised-securities issuance and trading on public blockchains and DeFi protocols. He also previewed a separate "Reg Crypto" framework permitting on-chain token sales for fundraising. Per Decrypt and Cointelegraph coverage, Atkins framed the package as the natural follow-on to the 17 March SEC/CFTC joint interpretive release that classified 16 named tokens as digital commodities.

⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per Skadden's "SEC Moves to Lighten Regulation" April note and Sullivan & Cromwell's GENIUS Act memo, the innovation exemption will likely use the Commission's Section 28 exemptive authority — the same statutory basis as the 2003 NMS pilot — with conditions plausibly mirroring Reg A+ Tier 2 (issuer caps, audited financials, blue-sky preemption). Cleary's tokenisation update flagged that the Commission can move without notice-and-comment rulemaking under Section 28, which is why "weeks" is plausible rather than aspirational. The Brief's read: the live question is whether the exemption pre-empts state blue-sky law. If yes (Reg A+ model), domestic tokenisation issuance moves onshore in Q3; if no, the exemption is partial relief and offshore venues retain their advantage.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Direct beneficiaries: Securitize, Ondo (ONDO), Backed, Superstate — issuers with US ambitions that have been operating from offshore wrappers; Coinbase (COIN) and Robinhood (HOOD) — equity-in-tokenised-form distribution channels onshore; Apollo (APO), KKR (KKR), Hamilton Lane (HLNE) — private-credit and PE distribution candidates if tokenised LP interests fall inside the sandbox. Pressured: pure offshore tokenisation venues that built their entire model around US regulatory friction.
