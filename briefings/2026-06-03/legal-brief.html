UpdateCLARITY Act Formally Placed on Senate Legislative Calendar — Galaxy Bets $10M on 2026 Passage as 60-Vote Floor Test Looms

"The Clarity Act entering the Senate Legislative Calendar is one of the biggest regulatory events for crypto in 2026." CNBC, 14 May 2026, on Senate Banking Committee clearance

The Digital Asset Market Clarity Act was formally placed on the United States Senate Legislative Calendar on 1 June 2026, the procedural step that makes the bill eligible for full Senate floor debate and a vote. The bill cleared the Senate Banking Committee on 14 May with a 15-9 bipartisan margin — Senators Gallego and Alsobrooks providing the Democratic crossover votes — and the calendar placement follows Senate leadership’s decision to advance the legislation. Galaxy Digital has publicly wagered $10 million on the bill passing in 2026, crystallising the institutional bet that the Trump-era legislative window closes before year-end.

Calendar placement does not mean a floor date has been announced. Senate leadership must schedule the bill for floor debate, allowing amendments; a 60-vote cloture threshold remains the critical barrier. The bill as passed by the House in July 2025 differs from the Senate Banking Committee version, meaning a conference reconciliation process is likely before any final enactment. The House-Senate gap centres on the scope of CFTC exclusive jurisdiction over “digital commodity” spot markets and the definition of “investment contract asset.”

The CLARITY Act would settle the jurisdictional map that has left crypto markets operating in a no-man’s land since the SEC-CFTC March 2026 MOU: CFTC would have exclusive jurisdiction over Bitcoin, Ethereum, and most Layer-1 tokens; SEC retains jurisdiction over assets that remain “investment contract assets.” The 16-cryptocurrency classification list in the March 2026 SEC-CFTC joint interpretation would be codified or superseded by the statute.

📎 Historical Precedent: The Commodity Exchange Act passed the Senate 65-31 in 1974 following its committee vote; the critical analogue is that Senate floor scheduling — not committee approval — has historically been where financial-services legislation stalls, as seen with Dodd-Frank’s eight-month gap between House passage and Senate floor consideration.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Calendar placement is a necessary but insufficient condition for enactment. The 60-vote cloture threshold means the bill needs at least 7 Democratic senators beyond the 2 committee crossover votes, and Senate floor time is finite before the 119th Congress recesses. The Brief’s read: Galaxy’s $10M bet is directionally correct — the political window is genuinely open — but the House-Senate reconciliation adds 6-8 weeks of conference time that compresses the viable legislative calendar into a Q3 sprint. Firms should model both enacted and non-enacted scenarios for year-end compliance budgets.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Latham & Watkins’ US Crypto Policy Tracker notes that structural differences between the House and Senate bills — particularly on the timing of the “decentralisation” test for asset reclassification — could require substantial amendment processes that extend the timeline well into 2027. Senate Minority Leader Schumer has not committed floor time, and the debt-ceiling calendar in late June competes for Senate attention. The Brief’s read: the downside scenario is a year-end pocket veto by Senate procedural rules, not substantive opposition.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Coinbase stands to be the largest single winner: exclusive CFTC jurisdiction eliminates the residual SEC securities-law risk that has overhung COIN since the 2023 lawsuit. Circle’s IPO timeline, delayed by the 20%+ stock drop in March on the CLARITY Act yield-ban draft, becomes cleaner if the bill passes before their S-1 window. The Brief’s read: the yield ban on stablecoins is the one provision that materially harms Coinbase (4% USDC yield program), while benefiting Ripple’s RLUSD (payments-only model). Any meaningful floor amendment period will re-open the yield-ban fight.

Three GENIUS Act Comment Windows Close June 9 — FinCEN/OFAC AML, FDIC Prudential, and OCC Licensing Rules All Enter Final Week of Comment Period

"It would be highly irresponsible for the OCC to grant new national trust charters to crypto entities while the five major federal agencies are still drafting the complex rulebooks mandated by the GENIUS Act." American Bankers Association letter to OCC, February 2026 [paraphrased]

Three parallel GENIUS Act NPRM comment periods reach their June 9 deadline this week, creating the most concentrated stablecoin regulatory comment opportunity in US history. The FinCEN/OFAC joint proposed rule (published 10 April) would require all Permitted Payment Stablecoin Issuers (PPSIs) to establish Bank Secrecy Act AML programs and OFAC sanctions compliance frameworks; Treasury’s “substantially similar” state-regime NPRM had a June 2 deadline (yesterday). The FDIC’s separate proposed rule establishes prudential standards for FDIC-supervised PPSIs, including deposit-insurance treatment of stablecoin reserves.

The OCC’s NPRM implementing licensing requirements for nationally chartered stablecoin issuers already closed on 1 May, making it the first of the five-agency rulemaking wave to enter the agency review-and-finalise phase. Chapman & Cutler’s public GENIUS Act Rulemaking Tracker identifies active NPRMs across five agencies (OCC, FDIC, FinCEN/OFAC, Treasury, Federal Reserve), with the Fed the only agency not yet at NPRM stage. Final rules are expected no earlier than Q4 2026 given the 12-month implementation period built into the FinCEN/OFAC proposal.

The practical effect: any entity planning to issue a US-regulated stablecoin by 2027 must have submitted comments on multiple NPRMs this week. Firms that fail to comment on the FDIC or FinCEN rules forfeit their ability to challenge the agency’s interpretive choices on record during any future APA-based litigation. Comment letters from Coinbase Institute, Circle, and Ripple are expected; the Blockchain Association filed on the OCC NPRM and is expected to file again this week.

📎 Historical Precedent: The Bank Secrecy Act’s 1970 implementing regulations took 14 months from enactment to effective date; the GENIUS Act’s 12-month implementation runway is historically tight for a framework of this scope, analogous to the compressed timeline that produced recurring Dodd-Frank no-action extensions from 2011 to 2014.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The FDIC and FinCEN/OFAC NPRMs are the two most consequential for market access: the FDIC rule determines whether stablecoin reserves qualify for deposit insurance pass-through, directly affecting investor protection marketing; the FinCEN/OFAC rule determines who qualifies as a PPSI and therefore is subject to the full AML framework rather than lighter-touch non-bank status. Mayer Brown’s April 2026 alert notes that the FinCEN rule would classify PPSIs as “financial institutions,” triggering full 31 CFR Part 1010 obligations. The Brief’s read: the most under-commented risk is the 12-month effective date countdown beginning from final rule publication — firms should model a Q4 2026 final rule as triggering a Q4 2027 compliance deadline, not a rolling “when practical” standard.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Brookings Institution’s analysis of the GENIUS Act implementation notes that the five-agency rulemaking creates a coordination problem: if final rules conflict on definitions (e.g., what constitutes a “permitted” issuer under the OCC rule vs. FinCEN rule), the statutory supremacy question is unresolved. Bankers asking Treasury to extend the comment deadline (reported by FindKnowDo, 8 May) signal that even regulated incumbents believe the timeline is unworkable. The Brief’s read: an extension of the FinCEN/OFAC deadline by 30-60 days is possible but not expected given the election-year regulatory pressure to show progress.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Circle and Tether face the starkest compliance choices: Circle’s IPO S-1 disclosure must now address five parallel NPRM processes and the risk that final rules arrive after the IPO window. Tether, operating offshore, is not a PPSI and is exempt from the GENIUS Act framework but risks effective exclusion from US institutional distribution channels if US custodians and exchanges apply PPSI-only rails. The Brief’s read: the comment period asymmetry (OCC closed May 1, others June 9) means OCC-supervised national bank stablecoin issuers (Ripple National Trust, Paxos) have first-mover advantage in shaping the final framework relative to state-chartered issuers still in the FDIC comment window.

UpdateFCA Cryptoasset Perimeter Guidance Consultation Closes Today — Seven Activity Categories Formally In-Scope as September Authorisation Window Approaches

"Any firm holding client crypto assets for more than 24 hours, or with the ability to override client authority, would be treated as a regulated custodian and require a full safeguarding license." FCA CP26/13 — Cryptoasset Perimeter Guidance, April 2026 [paraphrased — The Block, 16 April 2026]

The FCA’s consultation on cryptoasset perimeter guidance (CP26/13) closes today, 3 June 2026, marking the formal transition from consultation to rules-drafting for the UK’s forthcoming cryptoasset regulatory regime. The paper, published 15 April, maps seven activities against the new regulated perimeter: issuing qualifying stablecoins in the UK; safeguarding of qualifying cryptoassets; operating a qualifying cryptoasset trading platform; dealing as principal; dealing as agent; arranging deals; and arranging qualifying cryptoasset staking. Firms providing any of these services to UK retail or professional clients must register for FCA authorisation from 30 September 2026.

The 24-hour custody rule is the provision generating the most industry comment: any firm that holds client crypto assets for more than 24 consecutive hours, or that has unilateral ability to override client authority over those assets, is classified as a regulated custodian and requires a full safeguarding licence. CoinDesk’s 16 April analysis noted this would sweep in wallet providers, yield products, and DeFi interfaces that enable address control, far beyond traditional exchange custody models. Linklaters’ alert on CP26/13 identified the stablecoin issuance rule — UK establishment required for the full issuance lifecycle — as the most significant barrier to overseas stablecoin distribution in the UK.

The FCA is targeting publication of final rules in “summer 2026,” with crypto firms eligible to apply for authorisation from 30 September 2026. Full regulatory enforcement begins October 2027. The FCA has already placed one financial promotion approver in remediation and blocked web pages from retail access as part of its live supervisory cycle ahead of the authorisation gateway opening.

📎 Historical Precedent: The FCA’s 2019 cryptoasset perimeter guidance (PS19/22) took 14 months from consultation to final policy statement; the accelerated 2026 timeline reflects political pressure from HM Treasury, which committed the UK to “becoming a global crypto hub” in its 2022 policy statement, though CP26/13’s 7-activity scope is substantially broader than the 2019 guidance.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The closure of CP26/13 starts a tight 14-week window for the FCA to publish final rules before the 30 September authorisation gateway opens — a pace that Skadden’s April 2026 alert describes as “aggressive.” The practical implication: firms that have not yet begun their FCA authorisation pre-application engagement are now behind the curve. The 24-hour custody threshold will require most DeFi protocol front-ends and yield aggregators to either restructure their UK offering or formally apply. BCLP’s CP26/13 analysis notes that “arranging qualifying cryptoasset staking” is new and sweeps in staking-as-a-service providers. The Brief’s read: the UK is moving faster than the EU’s MiCA implementation at the perimeter level, creating a genuine first-mover advantage for firms that get authorised in the September 2026 window before the October 2027 enforcement date.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Finance Monthly’s analysis of FCA Crypto Regulation 2026 points out that the FCA’s authorisation track record for financial promotions approvers — two years of backlogs pre-Brexit — suggests the September gateway could open significantly later than planned. Firms that over-invest in UK authorisation infrastructure ahead of a delayed FCA publication risk sunk costs. The Brief’s read: the FCA Section 21 remediation action (one firm in remediation, web pages blocked) signals that the FCA is willing to use existing powers before the new regime is in force, making the authorisation timeline a floor, not a ceiling, on enforcement risk.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Coinbase UK, Binance UK, Kraken, and OKX are the four largest registered UK exchanges; all will need to transition from their existing Anti-Money Laundering registration to the new cryptoasset activities authorisation by October 2027. The Brief’s read: the 24-hour custody rule is the provision most likely to generate the first post-authorisation enforcement action — it is precisely the kind of bright-line rule the FCA uses to make compliance failures easily demonstrable in tribunal. Firms operating offshore yield products with UK retail customers should prioritise legal advice on this specific threshold before the September gateway opens.

ABA Demands OCC Pause Nine Conditional Crypto Trust Charter Reviews — Ripple, Coinbase, Circle, Paxos, Fidelity Named as GENIUS Act Rulemaking Remains Incomplete

"It would be highly irresponsible for the OCC to grant new national trust charters to crypto entities while the five major federal agencies are still drafting the complex rulebooks mandated by the GENIUS Act." American Bankers Association letter to OCC, February 2026 [paraphrased from ABA Banking Journal]

The American Bankers Association, representing the largest US banking lobby, has called on the OCC to immediately pause its review and finalisation of national trust bank charter applications for nine crypto and fintech firms, including Ripple National Trust Bank, Coinbase (COIN), Circle, BitGo, Fidelity Digital Assets, Paxos, Crypto.com (Foris DAX), Protego, and Stripe-linked Bridge. The ABA’s central argument: granting charters while the GENIUS Act’s five-agency rulemaking remains in NPRM phase is procedurally premature and creates a regulatory arbitrage pathway for crypto firms to access bank-like distribution before compliance obligations are finalised.

The OCC conditionally approved Ripple, BitGo, Fidelity Digital Assets, Paxos, and First National Digital Currency Bank in December 2025 for national trust bank charters, with full authorisation contingent on satisfying OCC conditions including capital requirements, exam readiness, and systems testing. Senator Elizabeth Warren’s 1 June letter demanding OCC records on all nine charter approvals — including all Trump-family communications in the approval process — adds a political dimension that reinforces the ABA’s procedural argument. FinTech Weekly’s analysis notes that eleven companies have filed charter applications across 83 days of OCC review activity in 2026, the highest pace in the OCC’s modern history.

The ABA has also requested that the OCC amend its naming conventions to bar any non-bank chartered trust company from including the word “bank” in its entity name, directly targeting “Ripple National Trust Bank.” This naming dispute echoes the 2018 fintech charter litigation (OCC v. Vullo, 2nd Circuit) and could generate parallel state-level challenges from state banking supervisors who view national trust charters as preempting state financial regulation.

📎 Historical Precedent: The OCC’s 2018 special-purpose fintech charter attracted 22 states filing amicus briefs opposing federal preemption; the NYDFS (OCC v. Vullo) successfully challenged the charter’s preemptive reach in the Second Circuit before the Supreme Court declined cert in 2019. The same state-banking preemption arguments are available here and are likely already being drafted.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The ABA’s pause demand has no binding legal force: the OCC is an independent bureau of the Treasury Department and charter decisions are within its statutory discretion. However, the combination of ABA lobbying, Warren’s oversight letter, and five parallel incomplete NPRMs creates a political environment in which a charter grant before June 9’s comment deadlines would face immediate congressional scrutiny. The Brief’s read: the practical outcome is a de facto 60-90 day pause while the OCC waits for the FinCEN/OFAC NPRM comment period to close and the first batch of final rules to emerge. Ripple and Paxos, whose OCC NPRM comment period already closed May 1, are structurally advantaged over firms whose FDIC comment period runs until June 9.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Crypto industry group submissions to the OCC — reported by TradersUnion, citing Coinbase Institute and Blockchain Association letters — argue that the ABA’s position is protectionist rather than prudential: national bank charters for trust companies do not accept deposits and therefore do not create the systemic risk that AML rulemaking is designed to address. The Troutman Financial Services blog notes that OCC-chartered trust banks are already subject to examination under 12 CFR Part 9 fiduciary standards, making the “no rules yet” argument legally weak. The Brief’s read: the ABA is losing the legal argument but winning the political timeline — which is what matters for 2026 charter issuance.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Ripple is the most exposed: its RLUSD stablecoin strategy depends on the National Trust Bank charter to offer bank-grade custody and distribution rails in the US. A six-month charter delay pushes the RLUSD institutional rollout into 2027, during which Circle’s USDC — which does not require a new charter — cements its institutional-market position. BitGo, whose trust bank operations already serve institutional clients under its state trust charter, has the most to gain from OCC approval and the most to lose from delay. The Brief’s read: watch for Ripple to file a formal OCC comment on the ABA letter and for Coinbase Institute to publish a legal white paper distinguishing trust companies from deposit-taking institutions before the end of June.

ASIC No-Action Relief Expires June 30 — 27 Days for Crypto Platforms Serving Australian Users to Achieve AFSL Compliance

"Many widely traded digital assets are financial products under current law — and will remain so under the Government's proposed law reform — meaning many providers require a financial services licence." ASIC INFO 225 No-Action Position, confirmed 2026

ASIC’s sector-wide no-action position under INFO 225, which provided regulatory comfort for digital asset platforms to operate while the Digital Assets Framework Bill 2025 was finalised, expires on 30 June 2026 — 27 days from today. After this date, any platform that holds, deals in, or provides advice on digital assets that qualify as financial products under the Corporations Act 2001 must hold an Australian Financial Services Licence or cease providing those services to Australian clients. ASIC has repeatedly stated it will not extend the no-action position.

The Digital Assets Framework Bill 2025, which introduces specific AFSL-style licensing for Digital Asset Platforms and Tokenised Custody Platforms, is expected to clear Parliament in 2026 but commences on 9 April 2027 following an 18-month transition. This creates a 9-month compliance gap between June 30 and April 2027, during which firms must hold a Corporations Act AFSL (or be exempt) to legally serve Australian retail clients. AUSTRAC’s AML/CTF and VASP obligations, which are separate from ASIC’s no-action position, are already live for most operators.

Chainalysis’s “Australia’s Crypto Crossroads” report identifies that while Australia is the 5th-largest crypto market by on-chain volume, fewer than 15% of active platforms have completed a full AFSL application. Platforms relying solely on the no-action position without an AFSL application in progress are at acute risk of enforcement after June 30.

📎 Historical Precedent: ASIC’s 2019 relief for managed discretionary accounts expired on schedule despite industry calls for extension; ASIC subsequently issued 18 stop orders against non-compliant MDA providers in the six months following expiry. The enforcement pattern is expected to recur.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Piper Alderman’s analysis of ASIC’s INFO 225 guidance identifies the key legal question as whether a platform’s specific digital assets qualify as “financial products” under the Corporations Act 2001 s.764A, which includes interests in managed investment schemes, derivatives, and securities. ASIC’s 2025 updated guidance confirmed that most widely-traded digital assets are financial products. The Brief’s read: the safest interpretation for any platform with Australian retail customers is that at least some of the assets they deal in are financial products and an AFSL is required. An “all our assets are commodities” argument has not been tested before ASIC or the Federal Court and is not a credible defence at the June 30 line.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Binance, OKX, and Bybit each have substantial Australian retail user bases and none has publicly disclosed an AFSL holding or application. The Brief’s read: the most likely enforcement scenario is an ASIC interim stop order in August-September 2026 against an offshore platform that continued operating after June 30 without an AFSL — analogous to the FCA’s 2022 stop order against Binance Markets Limited. Australian investors using non-compliant platforms post-June-30 lose AFSL-related dispute resolution and compensation scheme protections.

Three GENIUS Act NPRMs closing June 9 and the CLARITY Act on the Senate calendar simultaneously define the US stablecoin regulatory architecture in real time. Circle and Ripple are in active comment-filing mode; Tether’s offshore status is becoming a structural disadvantage for US institutional distribution.

UK / EU Regulatory Divergence

⚡ Widening

FCA CP26/13 closing today reveals a UK regime with tighter custody thresholds (24-hour rule) than MiCA’s safeguarding requirements, while the UK authorisation window (September 2026) opens ahead of full MiCA enforcement (July 2026). The divergence creates genuine jurisdiction-shopping decisions for exchanges serving both markets.

Banking Charter / Institutional Infrastructure

↗ Mixed Signals

OCC conditional charters for nine crypto firms represent the most significant structural shift in US crypto banking infrastructure since the 2013 FinCEN guidance. The ABA standoff introduces procedural delay but not substantive legal barriers; the charter approvals are likely to be finalised in Q3 2026 as GENIUS Act rulemaking matures.

Australia / APAC Licensing

⬇ Tightening

ASIC’s no-action expiry on June 30 and AUSTRAC’s live VASP obligations create a dual-track compliance demand for Australia-facing platforms. Japan’s June 9 FIEA comment deadline and MAS Singapore’s active licensing programme make APAC the most compressed regulatory geography for platform operators in June 2026.

DeFi & Tokenization

↗ Quietly Advancing

MiCA’s July 1 enforcement brings DeFi into the ESMA spotlight — CASPs that route through DeFi protocols to avoid MiCA registration are a named supervisory priority. RWA tokenization at $29B is growing but only $2.47B is active in open DeFi, reflecting compliance rails limiting composability.

AI Governance Intersection

⬆ Accelerating

EU AI Act Article 50 Code of Practice expected this month creates AI-content labelling requirements that sweep in crypto platforms using AI commentary tools. Texas enacted its standalone AI governance law in June 2026 with disclosure requirements for automated decision-making systems, adding a US state dimension to the compliance matrix.
