Alpha — Trade Classification (Aggressor Side)

Wednesday, 3 June 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #40 11 min read
TL;DR

Trade classification is the rule that tags every executed print as buyer-initiated or seller-initiated — the aggressor side — and it is the single atom every delta, CVD and footprint tool you own is built on. In crypto you mostly get it for free because exchanges stamp the taker side natively; the moment you aggregate venues, mix spot and perp, or trust a reconstructed feed, your delta starts lying exactly when the tape moves fastest. Today's BTC markdown into 67K is unambiguously seller-aggressed — the edge is knowing what that classification can and cannot tell you.

§01
What It Is
Definition · the aggressor side
Trade classification is the rule that decides whether each executed trade counts as a buy or a sell — the aggressor side — and it is the atom sitting underneath every delta, CVD and footprint reading you trust.

Every delta number you have ever read is a stack of decisions about who hit whom. A trade always has two sides — one resting in the book (the maker), one crossing the spread to take it (the taker, the aggressor). Trade classification is simply the act of tagging which side was aggressive: a buyer lifting the ask is an aggressive buy (+volume); a seller hitting the bid is an aggressive sell (−volume). Stack those tags and you get delta; run a cumulative sum and you get CVD; bucket them by price and you get a footprint.

In crypto you are spoiled. Exchange websockets hand you the aggressor side directly — Binance's trade stream carries an isBuyerMaker flag, so no inference is needed. In equities, FX, and on any aggregated or reconstructed feed, classification is a guess made from price and quotes after the fact. That distinction is the whole point of this brief: the same CVD chart is hard data on one venue and an estimate on another, and the estimate breaks in fast, one-sided tape — precisely when you are leaning on it.

If you only remember one thing Delta is not a measurement — it is a classification. On a native exchange feed the classification is given to you; everywhere else it is inferred, and an inferred delta is only as honest as the algorithm that built it.
§02
Mechanics
Exchange flag vs the tick rule
When the venue tells you the aggressor, delta is exact; when it doesn't, you fall back to rules that compare each trade to the quote or to the prior price — and those rules misfire most where the spread is wide or the tape is fast.

There are two worlds. In the tagged world, the matching engine knows which order was resting and which crossed, and it ships that fact with every trade. Binance's isBuyerMaker (the m field) is the canonical example: when it is true, the buyer was the passive maker, so the seller crossed — the print is an aggressive sell; when false, the buyer crossed — an aggressive buy. No estimation. This is why exchange-native crypto CVD is the cleanest delta data available to a retail trader.

In the inferred world you only see trades and quotes, so you reconstruct the aggressor with heuristics. The Quote Rule compares the trade price to the bid-ask midpoint — above mid is a buy, below is a sell. The Tick Test compares the trade to the previous trade — an uptick is a buy, a downtick a sell, unchanged inherits the prior direction. The Lee-Ready algorithm (1991) chains them: use the Quote Rule, and only fall back to the Tick Test for prints that land exactly at the midpoint. For pre-aggregated bars, Bulk Volume Classification (BVC) assigns a probability of buy volume from the standardised price change over the bar rather than labelling individual trades.

§04
In Practice
Trust native, distrust aggregate
Trust the aggressor side from a single venue's native feed; distrust delta the moment it is aggregated, reconstructed, or read without open interest beside it.
1

Single venue, native feed, first

Read CVD off one exchange's tagged taker stream before you trust any multi-venue aggregate. Aggregators stitch feeds with different tagging conventions and latencies, and the sum smears the exact edge you came for.

2

Spot CVD is not perp CVD

Spot taker flow is real money changing hands; perp taker flow is leverage repositioning. When they diverge, the classification is fine — it is the positioning that is split, and that split is where trapped longs and shorts live.

3

Classification gives flow; OI gives intent

A wall of aggressive sells with rising open interest is new shorts initiating; the same wall with falling OI is longs capitulating. Identical delta, opposite meaning — never read one without the other.

4

At the midpoint, it is a coin-flip

On feeds without a native flag, midpoint prints get tick-tested, and in fast one-sided tape the tick test mislabels precisely when it matters. Treat sub-second delta on reconstructed feeds as noise, not signal.

§07
Common Traps
Where the tally lies
The single biggest mistake is reading delta as a clean measurement when it is a chain of classifications — and trusting it most where the chain is weakest.
1
Trusting aggregated CVD

Multi-venue CVD sums feeds with different tagging conventions, liquidity profiles and latencies. The aggregate looks authoritative and smears the very signal you wanted. Read one venue's native flag first; use aggregates for context, not triggers.

2
Reading delta without open interest

Aggressive selling is meaningless until you know if positions are opening or closing. Rising OI into selling is new shorts; falling OI is capitulation. The classification is identical — the trade is not.

3
Micro-delta on a reconstructed feed

If your feed infers the aggressor via a tick test, fast one-sided tape mislabels prints exactly when liquidity thins. Sub-second delta on a non-native feed is closer to a coin-flip than a signal — zoom out.

4
Confusing perp flow with conviction

Perp taker flow is leverage, not money committed to a directional view. A perp-led CVD dump while spot CVD stays flat is positioning and hedging — often a trap set-up — not distribution.

5
Honest take — "CVD is not order flow"

CVD is one derived statistic: the running sign of aggressor volume. It gets sold as a window into "smart money," but it is blind to order size intent, icebergs, hedging and absorption. It is a useful lens on who is crossing the spread — not x-ray vision into why.
