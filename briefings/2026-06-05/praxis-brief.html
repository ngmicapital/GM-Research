Praxis · Intelligence Briefing · 5 June 2026

Praxis

Key Ideas · Strategy · Tools · Horizon

Four sources this issue converge on one lever: the gap between the story in your head and the situation in front of you. Mark Manson contrasts Dave Mustaine — cast out of Metallica, built Megadeth, and still wept that he was a failure — with Pete Best, fired by the Beatles, who called it the best thing that ever happened to him: same loss, opposite lives, because the reference point is a choice. The Daily Stoic dismantles the catastrophic “if-this-then-that” assumptions we manufacture and then suffer from. Richard Lang’s “Experiments in Having No Head” on Sam Harris’s Waking Up treats the felt sense of separation as a construction attention can dissolve. And Tim Ferriss, via Mike Maples Jr.’s Pattern Breakers, names the founder’s most dangerous story — customers who are interested, not desperate.

01 Key Ideas & Insights
Mark Manson · Breakthrough #217 · “After You Lose Everything” · 4 June 2026

The Reference Point Is a Choice: Why Dave Mustaine Wept and Pete Best Smiled

In 1983, Dave Mustaine was thrown off a tour bus in New York with a one-way ticket home and no explanation. The band that fired him was Metallica. By the time the bus reached Los Angeles, he had decided to build something bigger, louder, meaner — and he did. Megadeth, Manson recounts, would go on to sell more than 25 million albums and reach metal royalty. And yet, years later, in an interview, Mustaine broke down in tears and admitted he still felt like a failure. The reason was a single number: Metallica had sold 180 million. He had spent a career measuring his life against the one band he could never catch.

Pete Best was fired from the Beatles in 1962, weeks before they conquered the planet. He crashed hard — depression, drinking, lawsuits. But he rebuilt: a marriage, children, a life that still had music in it. Decades later, asked about being cut from the biggest band in history, Best said: “I’m happier than I would’ve been with the Beatles. It was the best thing that ever happened to me.” Same category of loss. Opposite lives. The difference was not talent, money, or fame — it was the reference point each man chose to measure himself against.

One chased what he lost; the other learned to love what he found. The facts of a loss are fixed the moment it happens. The reference point you measure it against — the imaginary life you compare your real one to — is the one variable that stays in your hands. Almost all of the suffering is in the comparison, not the event.

Daily Stoic · Ryan Holiday · “You Made That Up” · 2 June 2026

The Catastrophe You Are Suffering Through Has Not Happened: On the Faulty Logic We Manufacture and Then Obey

“If you don’t answer this call, you could lose your job. If you keep passing on these opportunities, you could get passed over. If you miss this flight, the vacation is ruined. If you let your employees do that, they’ll think you’re a pushover.” Holiday’s observation is that we make hundreds of these silent if-this-then-that assumptions a day — causal chains we never state out loud and never examine — and they generate a continuous low hum of worry. The crazy part, he writes, is that most of this logic is spurious. Most of the assumptions are simply faulty. We are making them up, and then suffering as though they were facts.

This is why reason and reflection sat at the center of Stoic practice. Not as intellectual ornament, but as a tool for interrupting the manufacturing line. Pausing to ask “what is actually real here, and what am I adding?” reveals that one missed call, one awkward meeting, one travel snag does not determine the trajectory of a life. The employees do not think you’re a pushover; they probably appreciate what you did. The remedy is to look at the situation without the lens of anxiety laid over the top of it — to subtract the self-inflicted assumptions and respond to what remains.

Anxiety is mostly a tax on events that have not occurred and largely will not. The Stoic move is not positive thinking — it is subtraction: strip the invented causal chain and ask what is left. What is left is almost always survivable, and far smaller than the story.

Waking Up · Richard Lang · “Experiments in Having No Head” (final installment) · 4 June 2026

The Distance Was Never There: A Contemplative Experiment in Dissolving Separation

The newest sessions on Sam Harris’s Waking Up app come from Richard Lang, the long-time custodian of Douglas Harding’s “headless way.” The premise sounds strange until you actually run the experiment: a distant star, the feeling of your own body, an intrusive thought, another person’s voice — we habitually place all of these at different distances from the thing we take ourselves to be. Lang’s claim is that this felt sense of distance, and of being a separate self looking out at a separate world, begins to dissolve the moment you attend closely to raw experience rather than to your concept of it. Looked at directly, the world is not “out there” at arm’s length; it is appearing, already, in the same awareness as everything else.

What makes this more than mysticism is that it is a falsifiable, first-person experiment, not a belief to adopt. Harding’s original instruction — point back at your own face and report what you actually find there, rather than what you assume is there — is the contemplative cousin of the Stoic move above. In both cases the practice is the same: notice the difference between the model you carry and the experience actually presenting itself, and let the model lose its grip. Lang’s sessions — “Sitting in the Center,” “Soft Eyes, Soft Being,” “Stress-Free” — are guided runs of that single experiment.

The tension you feel is rarely as personal, the world rarely as remote, and other people rarely as separate, as the felt sense insists. The separation is part of the story, not the situation — and attention, not argument, is what loosens it.

Tim Ferriss · tim.blog · “On the Importance of Desperate Customers” (via Mike Maples Jr.) · 1 June 2026

Interested vs. Desperate: The Most Flattering Story a Founder Can Tell, and the Test That Kills It

Revisiting Mike Maples Jr.’s Pattern Breakers, Ferriss surfaces a distinction that doubles as the issue’s business-world case study. Many start-ups execute well, raise money, ship a product — and quietly fail anyway, because “customers are interested in what they have built, but they are not desperate for it.” Interest is the flattering signal: it feels like traction, it fills a pipeline, it produces polite meetings and warm intros. But interest is the story, not the situation. Maples is blunt about the dividing line: “There is a huge cultural difference between finding any number of desperate customers versus zero desperate customers. Many start-ups have a huge theoretical market opportunity but never find a single customer desperate for what they propose to build.”

The practical correction is to hunt for desperation specifically rather than letting interest masquerade as demand. Maples recommends building an implementation prototype to find the most acutely pained users and to learn which benefit actually matters most to them — with the early questions all pointed at urgency: what problem is most pressing, and what are people already willing to pay to make it go away? The trap is the “mild middle” — the comfortable zone where everyone likes your product and no one needs it.

“Interested” is what people say to be kind. “Desperate” is what people do when they have a fire to put out. The same gap that separates a self-soothing story from an honest look at your life separates a founder’s deck from their market — and only the look at the situation, never the story, tells you whether you have a business.

02 Strategy & Practice
Practice · Synthesised from Manson / Daily Stoic / Lang

The Story Audit: Separating the Situation From the Story You’ve Wrapped Around It

The common thread across this issue is that the interpretive layer — the reference point, the invented causal chain, the felt sense of separation — governs more of our experience than the underlying facts do, and is far more changeable than the facts. This practice is a five-minute audit for prying the two apart when something is weighing on you.

  • State the situation in flat, observable facts. Write what actually happened, in language a security camera could confirm: “The deal did not close.” “I was not included.” No adjectives, no consequences, no “which means.” This is the situation. Everything you are about to add is the story.
  • Surface the if-this-then-that chain. Holiday’s move: write the implied causal logic out loud — “if this, then I’ll be passed over, then I’ll never recover.” Stated explicitly, most of these chains are visibly spurious. Mark each link “known” or “made up.” You will be surprised how few are known.
  • Name the reference point. Manson’s lesson: what imaginary alternative are you measuring this against? Mustaine measured Megadeth against Metallica and lost a life he had actually won. Ask whether the benchmark you’re using is one you’d consciously choose — or just the one the comparison defaulted to.
  • Subtract, then respond. Remove every link marked “made up” and every reference point you wouldn’t choose on purpose. Respond to what remains. As Lang’s experiment shows, the tension is rarely as personal, or the threat as close, as the felt sense first insisted.

You cannot always change the situation. You can almost always change the story — and most weeks, the story is doing the damage.

Practice · From Mike Maples Jr. / Tim Ferriss

The Desperation Test: A Reality Check for Any Bet — a Product, a Pitch, or a Career Move

The same discipline applied outward. Before you commit serious time to a venture, a feature, a side project, or a job, run Maples’s test to find out whether you have demand or merely interest — and do it before, not after, you’ve sunk a year into the “mild middle.”

  • Find one genuinely desperate user. Not ten interested ones — one desperate one. If you cannot name a single person or organisation with a fire your idea puts out today, that is the finding. Zero desperate customers is a different universe from one.
  • Ask urgency questions, not approval questions. Replace “would you use this?” (which invites politeness) with “what is the most pressing version of this problem for you right now, and what are you already paying — in money, time, or workarounds — to deal with it?” Approval is the flattering story; spending is the situation.
  • Build the smallest implementation prototype. Maples’s tool: a rough, real version put in front of the most pained user, specifically to learn which single benefit they actually grab for. Watching what they reach for beats any survey of what they say.
  • Treat the “mild middle” as a stop sign. Broad, lukewarm liking is the most expensive trap in the set, because it looks like progress. If everyone likes it and no one needs it, change the target before you scale the effort.
03 Tools & Resources
📖
Book

Pattern Breakers — Mike Maples Jr.

The source of the interested-vs-desperate framework and a broader study of how breakthrough start-ups actually begin — with a non-consensus insight and a small set of customers in acute pain, rather than a large, lukewarm market. Maples is a founding partner at Floodgate and an eight-time Forbes Midas List investor; the “implementation prototype” and “mild middle” ideas travel well beyond venture into product, hiring, and any bet on what people will actually adopt. Ferriss’s excerpt is at tim.blog/2026/06/01/desperate-customers.

🧠
Tool

Waking Up — “Experiments in Having No Head” (Richard Lang)

Sam Harris’s meditation app added a final set of four guided sessions from Richard Lang — “Sitting in the Center,” “Soft Eyes, Soft Being,” “Stress-Free,” and a closing meditation. Unlike breath-focused mindfulness, these are short, structured runs of a single first-person experiment in dropping the felt sense of separation. A clean entry point if you want to test the claim in this issue rather than take it on faith. Waking Up offers a free trial and waives the fee for anyone who genuinely can’t afford it.

👀
Concept

On Having No Head — Douglas Harding (1961)

The original text behind the Waking Up sessions. Harding’s “headless way” reframes awakening not as belief or achievement but as a set of repeatable pointing experiments — look back at where your head should be and report only what you actually find. It is the most concrete instance of this issue’s recurring instruction: distinguish the model you carry from the experience in front of you. Short, eccentric, and best read as a manual to run rather than a doctrine to accept.

Podcast / Newsletter

Mark Manson — “How to Change Your Life, Solved”

The companion episode to the Mustaine/Best newsletter, on Manson’s Solved podcast: why change is hard even when you want it, and what the research says actually works. Pairs naturally with the Daily Stoic essay “You Made That Up” (dailystoic.com/you-made-that-up) — one targets the reference point you measure against, the other the assumptions you manufacture; both are about editing the interpretive layer rather than the circumstances.

04 On the Horizon
Crypto

The Selloff as a Story-vs-Situation Event: Leverage Is the Gap Between the Two

This week made the issue’s theme literal. Bitcoin broke below $62,000 on 4 June — roughly 51% under its October 2025 all-time high near $126,000 — as more than $1.5 billion in leveraged crypto longs were liquidated in 24 hours (over $800M in BTC, ~$386M in ETH), and US spot Bitcoin ETFs logged an 11–12 day streak of net outflows totalling about $3.45 billion, the longest since launch. Leverage is the gap between the story (price will keep rising) and the situation (a margin call cares nothing for your thesis). When forced selling arrives, the narrative is the first thing margin clerks ignore. Watch whether the $60,000 level holds; the deleveraging, not the headlines, is the real signal. (Sources: CoinDesk, crypto.news, 4 June 2026.)

Work

The Rising Premium on Desperation Signals in an Interest-Saturated World

Maples’s distinction is about to matter more, not less. As AI makes it nearly free to manufacture the appearance of interest — personalised outreach, synthetic enthusiasm, inflated engagement metrics, polished demos — the flattering signal is becoming abundant and therefore worthless. The scarce, expensive-to-fake signal is genuine desperation: someone actually paying, switching, or hacking together a workaround today. Expect the operators who win the next cycle to be those who build explicit desperation tests into product and go-to-market, and who treat vanity interest as noise rather than traction.

Mind

Contemplative Practice Moving Past Mindfulness Into First-Person Phenomenology

Waking Up’s push into the Harding headless tradition is a small marker of a larger shift: the mainstream wellness stack is moving beyond stress-reduction breathwork toward sharper, stranger first-person experiments about the structure of experience itself — non-dual attention, the felt sense of self, the construction of distance. The throughline with Stoic practice is striking and probably not coincidental: both are technologies for noticing the difference between a model and reality. Track whether “selfless” or non-dual protocols become the next layer after basic mindfulness goes fully commoditised.

Culture

The Reference-Point Economy: Feeds That Manufacture Mustaines at Scale

Manson’s lesson has a structural edge in 2026. Algorithmic feeds are, in effect, reference-point engines: they continuously supply the unreachable comparison — the peer who exited, the founder on the cover, the highlight reel — against which an objectively good life reads as failure. More people are becoming Mustaines by default, measuring real wins against imaginary ceilings they never consciously chose. The counter-practice — deliberately selecting the benchmark you measure against, and noticing when a feed has chosen it for you — is quietly becoming one of the higher-leverage psychological skills of the decade.
