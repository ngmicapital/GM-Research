CFTC Scraps Three-Decade 'No-Deny' Settlement Rule — Defendants May Now Settle and Publicly Deny Wrongdoing, Retroactively

"For nearly three decades, the Commission has refused to settle cases unless the defendant promised not to publicly deny the Commission's allegations. I am pleased that we are rescinding the no-deny policy consistent with regulators throughout the government." Michael S. Selig, Chairman, CFTC — Press Release 9247-26, 3 June 2026

On 3 June the CFTC formally repealed Appendix A to Part 10 of its rules — the 1998 "neither-admit-nor-deny" gag that, since its adoption, has barred any settling defendant from publicly contesting the agency's allegations. The move lands two weeks after the SEC rescinded its own near-identical "gag rule" on 18 May (Press Release 2026-45), and the two actions together dismantle a settlement architecture that has governed federal financial enforcement for a generation.

Crucially, the CFTC's Enforcement Division said the change applies retroactively: the agency will decline to enforce legacy no-deny clauses already embedded in past consent orders. Director of Enforcement David Miller said the action "harmonizes the Commission's settlement approach with those taken by other agencies and ensures fairer resolutions in enforcement matters." For crypto defendants who settled during the 2021–2024 enforcement wave, that re-opens the door to public denial of allegations they were once contractually silenced on.

The practical effect cuts both ways. Defendants gain the right to settle while protecting reputation and parallel private litigation; regulators lose the implicit admission that a silent settlement once signalled. Expect settlement negotiations to lengthen and the agencies to lean harder on explicit factual findings inside orders to compensate.

📎 Historical Precedent: The SEC's gag rule dates to 1972; for 50+ years a settling party who later publicly denied the SEC's claims could have the settlement reopened — a constraint long criticised on First Amendment grounds (e.g., Cato Institute v. SEC challenges).
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

This is a structural win for the defense bar and a quiet rebalancing of leverage away from the agencies. Cleary Gottlieb's memo "Deny With Care" frames it precisely — settling defendants now gain optionality but also risk: a public denial that contradicts an order's stipulated facts invites a fresh enforcement or contempt exposure. The retroactivity hook is the sleeper issue. The Brief's read: watch for crypto firms that settled under the prior SEC/CFTC regime (Kraken-style staking orders, exchange registration sweeps) to issue carefully lawyered "we always disputed this" statements — reputational rehabilitation is now legally available where it was contractually barred.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The counter-case, voiced by former SEC enforcement officials quoted in Bloomberg, is that the gag rule's death weakens deterrence: if a defendant can pay a fine and then tell investors it did nothing wrong, settlements stop functioning as accountability and become a cost of doing business. The Brief's read: partly right, but the deterrent was always thin — the bigger shift is that agencies will now front-load detailed findings into orders, so the "admission" migrates from silence to the four corners of the document.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Direct beneficiaries are litigation-heavy crypto names that carry legacy consent orders — Coinbase, Kraken, Gemini, and any firm weighing settlement in an open matter. Defense-side enforcement practices (Sullivan & Cromwell, Davis Polk, Cleary) gain billable complexity; plaintiffs' securities bar loses the "they settled, so they're guilty" inference in follow-on class actions. The Brief's read: the net capital-flow effect is mildly bullish for regulated-exchange equity risk — settlement is now less reputationally radioactive.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish COIN — settling open matters becomes less reputationally costly; exchanges with legacy orders gain narrative flexibility.

🔴 Bearish Plaintiff-side securities litigation franchises — loss of the "silent settlement = admission" inference weakens follow-on damages cases.

⚪ Neutral Broad crypto majors (BTC, ETH) — process reform, not a market-moving substantive rule change.

White House Signs June 2 AI Executive Order on Innovation and Security — Tasks CISA and Treasury With an AI Cyber-Defense Clearinghouse

"It is the policy of the United States to promote AI innovation and security by working collaboratively with the private sector to modernize government and private sector information systems." Executive Order, "Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security," 2 June 2026

President Trump signed a new executive order on 2 June — "Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security" — pivoting the administration's AI agenda from last December's state-law-preemption fight toward cyber-defense and protection of American AI intellectual property. It directs agencies to use AI to harden both government and private-sector information systems.

The order sets aggressive clocks: within 30 days, DHS/CISA must issue directives to protect civilian federal IT and expand AI-enabled cyber tools, while Treasury, the Department of War (formerly Defense) and DHS stand up an AI-cybersecurity clearinghouse to coordinate vulnerability disclosure. Within 60 days, OMB must determine whether federal grants can fund AI vulnerability detection and OPM must expand technical hiring. This is the security counterpart to the deregulatory "America's AI Action Plan" — innovation framed through a national-security lens.

📎 Historical Precedent: Echoes the 2021 EO 14028 "Improving the Nation's Cybersecurity" (post-SolarWinds) — but recast for the AI era, with the vulnerability-coordination clearinghouse as the structural successor to CISA's earlier disclosure mandates.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Per the Latham & Watkins and Holland & Knight trackers of this administration's AI orders, the through-line is federal centralisation — first preemption of state AI law (Dec 2025 EO), now centralised cyber coordination. The Brief's read: the operative risk for industry is not the headline but the 30/60-day deliverables — a CISA directive plus a vulnerability clearinghouse will create de facto disclosure expectations for any AI vendor selling into federal systems, well ahead of any statute Congress passes.

🔄 Contrarian Take

Skeptics (Tech Policy Press commentary on prior orders in this series) argue these directives are aspirational — clearinghouses and "Tech Force" hiring have been announced repeatedly without durable funding, and an EO cannot appropriate money. The Brief's read: fair on funding, but procurement leverage is real — agencies can condition contracts on the new expectations without a single new dollar, which is exactly how cyber baselines have spread before.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Beneficiaries: AI-security vendors (CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, SentinelOne) and the hyperscalers (Microsoft, Google, AWS) positioned to supply "AI-enabled" federal cyber tooling. Pressure point: smaller AI model providers selling into government, who will face new vulnerability-disclosure overhead. The Brief's read: capital flows toward incumbents with FedRAMP footprints; the clearinghouse rewards scale.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish CRWD, PANW, MSFT — federal AI-cyber demand and clearinghouse plumbing favour established security and cloud incumbents.

🔴 Bearish Small/independent model vendors — new disclosure overhead raises the cost of selling into government.

⚪ Neutral Frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic) — IP-protection framing is supportive but operationally light near-term.

UpdateCLARITY Act Placed on Senate Legislative Calendar (No. 423) After 15-9 Committee Win — Floor-Vote Window Opens as Senate Returns

"We are closer to a functioning digital asset market structure than we have ever been. Now is not the time to flinch." Sen. Cynthia Lummis (R-WY), via X, June 2026

The new development: the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act was formally placed on the Senate Legislative Calendar on 1 June as Calendar No. 423 — clearing the procedural gate that prior briefs flagged as missing — after the Senate Banking Committee passed it 15-9 on 14 May, with Democrats Ruben Gallego (AZ) and Angela Alsobrooks (MD) crossing over. Senators returned to Washington on 3 June, and the bill is now eligible for floor scheduling for the first time.

Being on the calendar is necessary but not sufficient: leadership has not announced a floor date, the Banking text must still be merged with the Senate Agriculture Committee version, and a contested conflict-of-interest provision remains unresolved. Sixty votes are required to pass. JPMorgan analysts warned this week that the legislative runway before the summer recess is tight, and stablecoin-yield treatment has hardened into a sticking point.

📎 Historical Precedent: The House passed its market-structure framework (FIT21 / H.R. 3633 lineage) in prior sessions only to stall in the Senate — calendar placement without a scheduled vote is exactly where momentum has died before.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

The Latham US Crypto Policy Tracker frames the two-lane SEC/CFTC split as already operative via the March interpretation; CLARITY would codify it, removing the litigation risk that an interpretation alone carries. The Brief's read: calendar placement is the second-most-meaningful step after committee passage, but the binding constraint is floor time, not votes — with eight working weeks to recess and a crowded calendar, the realistic question is whether Thune prioritises it over appropriations, not whether 60 votes exist.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The bear case, per CoinDesk's 2 June analysis, is blunt: "CLARITY Act survival depends on the Senate getting a lot of non-crypto work done." The conflict-of-interest provision (aimed at presidential crypto ventures) is a genuine poison pill for some Democrats. The Brief's read: credible — this is the single most likely failure mode; if the merged Ag/Banking text cannot finesse the conflict clause, the bill slips past the recess and into 2027 risk.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Coinbase, Circle and Ripple have all lobbied hard for CLARITY; codified "digital commodity" status under CFTC jurisdiction is the prize for token issuers seeking exit from SEC ambiguity. The Brief's read: passage would be a structural re-rating event for US-domiciled token projects and exchanges; failure keeps the regulatory premium on offshore venues.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish COIN, XRP, US-listed token projects — codified CFTC commodity lane removes SEC litigation overhang if it passes.

🔴 Bearish Regulatory-arbitrage offshore venues — a US framework erodes the "come onshore later" optionality they sell.

⚪ Neutral BTC, ETH — already classified as digital commodities in the March interpretation; marginal incremental benefit.

European Commission Opens 'MiCA 2.0' Review — 86-Question Consultation Weighs Pulling DeFi, Staking and Lending Into Scope

The review will assess whether MiCA "remains fit for purpose in light of the initial implementation and market and policy developments since its entry into application." [paraphrased — Commission consultation notice, 20 May 2026] European Commission, Targeted Consultation on the Review of MiCA

The Commission launched a targeted review of the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation on 20 May, running parallel public and technical (86-question) tracks with a deadline of 31 August 2026. Tellingly, it lands days before MiCA's transitional period fully expires on 1 July — the framework is being reopened almost as soon as it takes full effect.

The review explicitly probes whether emerging activities MiCA left out — DeFi, staking, lending and tokenised deposits — should be brought within a broader, more coherent regime, alongside the treatment of tokenised financial instruments and the adequacy of stablecoin and CASP rules. Taylor Wessing has branded it "MiCA 2.0."

📎 Historical Precedent: Mirrors the EU's MiFID I → MiFID II arc — a first-generation markets regime reopened within years once implementation exposed perimeter gaps (here, DeFi and staking).
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Norton Rose Fulbright and Maples both read the consultation as the EU signalling that the MiCA perimeter — deliberately narrow on DeFi — will widen. The Brief's read: the strategic tell is timing: reopening MiCA before the ink dries on full application concedes that the original carve-outs for decentralised protocols are untenable, and it puts the EU on a convergence path with the UK's FCA, which is wrestling with the identical DeFi-interface perimeter question right now.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The counter-view: a consultation is not a rule, and an 86-question targeted exercise can take years to yield legislation — the near-term reality for CASPs is still 1 July compliance under MiCA-as-written. The Brief's read: correct on timing, but the direction of travel is the actionable signal — protocols designing EU market entry should assume a DeFi perimeter is coming, not bet on the current gap persisting.

🏗️ Industry Implications

DeFi protocols (Uniswap Labs, Aave) and staking/lending venues currently outside MiCA face the prospect of authorisation obligations; tokenised-deposit issuers (Société Générale-Forge, and bank consortia) gain clarity they have lobbied for. The Brief's read: capital and compliance spend will pre-position toward whichever entity structure survives a widened perimeter — expect EU DeFi front-ends to start the same "are we in scope?" triage the FCA's CP26/13 just forced in the UK.

Ripple Expands DC Policy Office as CME Launches 24/7 XRP Futures With Ripple Prime as Day-One Clearing Partner

"Ripple has always believed the future of digital assets should be built with policymakers and regulators, not around them." Stuart Alderoty, Chief Legal Officer, Ripple — 2 June 2026

Two coordinated moves bracketed 2–3 June. Ripple announced an expanded Washington, D.C. office (2 June) to deepen engagement on market structure, stablecoin regulation, payments modernisation, custody and tokenisation. A day later, CME Group launched regulated 24/7 trading for crypto futures and options — including XRP futures — with Ripple Prime as the day-one clearing and financing partner for institutional clients.

The pairing is a regulated-rails play: a policy footprint to shape the rules and a CFTC-regulated, round-the-clock derivatives venue to capture the institutional flow those rules unlock. CME noted XRP futures were its fastest product to cross $1B in open interest, hitting that mark in three months.

📎 Historical Precedent: Mirrors CME's 2017 Bitcoin-futures launch and 2021 Ether futures — each preceded a wave of institutional access and, eventually, spot-ETF demand; 24/7 trading is the structural next step.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Alderoty's framing is a deliberate contrast with the litigation posture Ripple was forced into during the SEC's prior XRP case. The Brief's read: with the SEC matter behind it and CLARITY in play, Ripple is executing the textbook post-litigation pivot — convert legal vindication into regulated-market positioning before the rules are finalised, so it is incumbent rather than applicant when they are.

🏗️ Industry Implications

CME's 24/7 model pressures crypto-native venues (Coinbase Derivatives, Deribit) on their core advantage — always-on trading — by adding it to regulated, institutionally-cleared infrastructure. Ripple Prime's clearing role positions it against prime brokers like Galaxy and Hidden Road. The Brief's read: institutional flow migrates toward regulated 24/7 rails; the moat shifts from "always open" to "always open and CFTC-cleared."

Weekly Rollup — Week Ending June 5

Executive Summary

The week's defining theme was enforcement-posture liberalisation in the US. The CFTC's repeal of its 1998 "no-deny" settlement rule, following the SEC's matching 18 May rescission, retroactively dismantles a generation-old constraint on settling defendants — a structural shift in the leverage balance between regulators and the regulated. Layered on top: a 2 June presidential AI order pivoting from preemption politics to cyber-defense execution, and the CLARITY Act finally reaching the Senate calendar after a bipartisan committee win.

Abroad, the EU reopened MiCA for a "2.0" review even before its transitional period fully closes on 1 July, signalling that the DeFi/staking perimeter gap will be addressed — a convergence with the UK's live FCA perimeter work. The cross-jurisdictional picture: the US is loosening enforcement while building market-structure scaffolding; the EU and UK are tightening the perimeter around decentralised activity.

Single Most Important Development

The CFTC/SEC "no-deny" repeal — it changes how every future financial-enforcement settlement is negotiated and lets past crypto defendants reclaim their narrative.
