The City No Government Would Claim

How a single diplomatic footnote in 1898 accidentally built the most densely populated place in human history

A one-line exception in an 1898 treaty accidentally created a lawless city-within-a-city in Hong Kong that became the most densely populated place in human history. With no building codes, no police, and no government claiming it, 50,000 people self-organized into the world's greatest accidental experiment in urban survival.

TL;DR

Kowloon Walled City was a 2.6-hectare patch of Hong Kong where no law applied for nearly a century — the accidental product of a diplomatic loophole neither Britain nor China ever bothered to close. What grew inside that void was 50,000 people, 350 interconnected buildings up to 14 storeys tall, unlicensed dentists, triad gangs, noodle factories, kindergartens, and the world’s best fish ball soup. At its peak it was 200 times denser than the rest of Hong Kong. In 1993, the governments finally agreed to demolish it. They replaced it with a park.

Section 01

The Story

On 9 June 1898, at the Second Convention of Peking, the Qing dynasty signed over the New Territories to the British Empire on a 99-year lease. The deal was straightforward by the standards of late-colonial land-grabbing: Britain needed the buffer zone to protect Hong Kong island. China needed to keep face. The negotiators agreed on almost everything except one small, walled fort on the Kowloon peninsula — a Qing garrison post that had been there since the Song dynasty. That fort, just 2.6 hectares, was explicitly carved out. Chinese officials would be allowed to remain “except in so far as may be inconsistent with the military requirements of Hong Kong.”

It was a footnote. Nobody thought it mattered. Within eleven months, a British military expedition marched in, expelled the Chinese garrison, and unilaterally declared the walled city part of the colony. China protested. Britain ignored the protest. And then, crucially, both governments stopped paying attention entirely.

Britain said it was British. China said it was Chinese. In practice, it belonged to no one.

For decades the walled city sat mostly empty — a Qing ghost, a legal anomaly nobody wanted to resolve. Then World War II arrived. The occupying Japanese tore down the stone walls themselves to use as fill material for the expansion of nearby Kai Tak Airport. When the war ended in 1945, hundreds of thousands of mainland Chinese refugees were flooding into Hong Kong. Squatters, seeing an abandoned site where neither colonial police nor mainland officials could touch them, moved in. Then more came. Then more. By the time anyone thought to stop it, stopping it would have required both a British colonial government and a Chinese Communist government to cooperate — and in the early 1950s that was not something either was inclined to do.

The Walls Are Gone

The irony: the defining feature of Kowloon Walled City — the stone walls themselves — had already been demolished before the city’s wild years even began. What remained was purely a legal and cartographic fiction: a rectangle of ambiguous sovereignty inside a modern city, the walls existing only on paper.

The city that grew in the 1950s, 60s, and 70s was not planned by anyone. It was built by people who had no other option, on land no government would police, using materials they could afford. Single-storey shacks became two-storey structures; those were built over again and again until the entire site was a mass of buildings between 10 and 14 storeys tall. By 1987, a government survey counted approximately 35,000 residents. Other estimates put the true figure — including unregistered occupants — closer to 50,000. On 2.6 hectares, that worked out to a population density of roughly 1.25 million people per square kilometre: the most crowded permanent human settlement ever recorded.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The physics of Kowloon Walled City confounded conventional engineering. None of the 350 buildings were constructed with architectural drawings or certified structural analysis. Foundations were minimal. There was little uniformity in height, shape, or material. By any normal standard, the structures should have been catastrophically unsafe — and some were. But the City as a whole did not collapse. The reason is both simple and extraordinary: the buildings held each other up.

As the city densified, the structures were packed so tightly against one another that they effectively became a single load-bearing mass. A building that would have been dangerously unstable in isolation was stabilized by the walls of its neighbours pressing against it on every side. The City had evolved, organically and without any engineer’s hand, into a form of structural mutualism. Remove one piece and you might destabilise the whole; leave it in place and the whole held together through sheer compression.

No government. No architect. No building code. And yet, for fifty years, it stood.

The same mutualism operated socially. With no police presence, the absence of law could easily have produced pure predation. For a period in the 1950s it largely did — five Triad gangs controlled the site, running opium dens, gambling parlours, and brothels. But as the permanent residential population grew and diversified, something else emerged. Residents formed volunteer brigades to fight fires. They organized informal systems to clean the narrow alleys and manage shared infrastructure. They established schools. They negotiated with the utility companies that ran electrical and water lines through the site. Informal norms crystallized into something recognizable as community governance, without any state apparatus to enforce it.

The Unlicensed Economy

Because Hong Kong’s professional licensing laws didn’t apply inside the City, unlicensed dentists and doctors from mainland China — barred from practising legally in the colony — set up cheap clinics there. Their signs hung from the exterior walls in clusters, advertising services that were technically illegal everywhere else in Hong Kong. Patients came from across the city to use them. The result was an informal medical district operating in plain sight of the colonial government, which simply pretended it wasn’t there.

The jurisdictional gap was the mechanism. It created a space where the normal costs of operating — licensing fees, building permits, tax registration, police payments — simply did not exist. Entrepreneurs who could not afford to compete in the regulated economy set up inside. Residents who could not afford regulated housing found it there. The City was cheap because it was lawless, and it was productive because it was cheap. It wasn’t utopia. But it was, demonstrably, livable.

Section 03

The Connections

Emergent order without the state. Kowloon Walled City has become a canonical reference point in debates about spontaneous social order — the idea, associated with economists like Friedrich Hayek, that complex functional arrangements can arise without central planning. What the City demonstrated is that this is real, but partial: spontaneous order did emerge, and it produced schools, sanitation, and a functioning economy. It also produced Triad gangs and opium dens. The City was not evidence that governments are unnecessary; it was evidence that people will organize in the absence of governments, and that the results are mixed.

The special economic zone parallel. China’s own experiment with the Special Economic Zone — beginning with Shenzhen in 1980, just across the border from Hong Kong — follows the same structural logic: carve out a jurisdiction where normal rules don’t apply, and watch capital and people flood in. The difference is that the SEZ loophole was deliberate, state-designed, and directed toward manufacturing exports rather than survival. Kowloon Walled City was the accidental, uncontrolled version of the same phenomenon. Both produced extraordinary density and economic activity. Only one of them was a plan.

The cyberpunk substrate. William Gibson coined the word “cyberspace” in 1984. He has said Kowloon Walled City was central to his imagination of what an unregulated information network would look like — dense, layered, ungoverned, full of competing services operating outside the law in the gaps between legitimate institutions. The City became a visual shorthand for the entire cyberpunk aesthetic: its stacked, improvised architecture appeared directly in the video games Sleeping Dogs (2012) and Call of Duty: Black Ops, and inspired the fictional architecture of dozens of science-fiction cities. The idea of a place that exists because no authority claims it — physical or digital — turns out to be one of the defining anxieties of the late 20th century.

The end and the park. In 1987, with the 1997 handover of Hong Kong to China approaching and diplomatic relations improving, Britain and China finally agreed to demolish the City. The eviction was brutal: riot police cleared the last holdouts in July 1992. Residents received compensation averaging HK$82,000 per person (roughly US$10,600 at the time) — a sum that accurately reflected how little the colonial government thought their homes were worth. Demolition was completed in April 1994. In December 1995 the site was reopened as Kowloon Walled City Park: manicured lawns, a preserved yamen building, tasteful heritage signs. The most extraordinary urban experiment in modern history had been turned into a pleasant afternoon walk.

City of Darkness: The Book

In 1993, photographers Greg Girard and Ian Lambot published City of Darkness: Life in Kowloon Walled City — a documentary record compiled in the months before demolition. It remains the primary visual archive of the City’s interior, and it revealed something that the “lawless slum” narrative had obscured: that most residents lived quiet, hardworking lives, knew their neighbours, and didn’t particularly want to leave.

What the density number actually means. 1.25 million people per square kilometre is an abstraction. To make it concrete: Manhattan at its densest is about 27,000 people per km². Dhaka, one of the most crowded cities on earth, averages around 44,000. Kowloon Walled City was approximately 28 times denser than Manhattan, and roughly 200 times denser than the rest of Hong Kong. If the entire population of the United States lived at Kowloon’s density, they would fit inside a single borough of New York City. The City wasn’t just the densest place in history. It was densely populated at a scale that has no modern equivalent.

Further Down the Hole

📷
City of Darkness: Revisited

Greg Girard and Ian Lambot’s 1993 photobook documented the City weeks before demolition. A 2014 expanded edition added oral histories from former residents. The photographs are extraordinary.

🏭
Shenzhen’s Thirty Years

China’s first Special Economic Zone began as a fishing village of 30,000 in 1980. By 2020 it had 12 million people. The deliberate version of “make a jurisdiction where rules are different” may be the most consequential urban experiment of the 20th century.

📚
Hayek’s Spontaneous Order

Friedrich Hayek’s concept of “spontaneous order” — that functional complexity can emerge from individual actions without central design — finds one of its most vivid real-world illustrations in the Walled City’s informal institutions.

🌐
Enclaves and Exclaves

Baarle-Hertog in Belgium contains 22 Dutch enclaves, some themselves containing Belgian sub-enclaves. The resulting jurisdictional tangle means some buildings straddle two legal systems. Property law, tax, and licensing all depend on where exactly your front door falls.

🏠
The Walled City Park

Kowloon Walled City Park opened in December 1995 on the site of the demolished city. The yamen building — the old Qing administrative hall at the centre of the original fort — has been preserved and is the only structure that survives from before the squatter era.
