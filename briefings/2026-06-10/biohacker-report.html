Sprint Intervals & Mitochondrial Remodelling Issue #BIO-032

Sprint interval training triggers a mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt) — a stress-and-remodel mechanism mechanistically distinct from endurance training’s biogenesis pathway — with direct implications for how PPL athletes should structure cardio. Plus: lion’s mane acute vs. chronic evidence divergence, cold water immersion timing for resistance-trained athletes, and the POLYCAD spermidine cardiovascular trial approaching its August 2026 completion date.

Driving Thesis

Sprint interval training doesn’t just do more of what endurance training does — it activates a structurally distinct mitochondrial programme. A 2025 Nature Communications study (Botella, Perri, Caruana et al.) used electron tomography to show that sprint efforts disrupt cristae architecture and trigger the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt), enriching protein quality control pathways rather than simply building more mitochondria. Eight weeks of SIT vs. MICT diverge at the proteome level even when respiratory outcomes converge — suggesting the two modalities provide non-redundant stimulus for anyone training for both performance and longevity.

Elsewhere: a Phase 1 Alzheimer’s trial of senolytics (dasatinib + quercetin) returned no clinically meaningful signal — a reminder that clearing senescent cells in tissue doesn’t automatically translate to cognitive rescue. Cold water immersion timing matters for PPL athletes: effective for endurance recovery, but mTOR-blunting when applied within 1 hour of resistance training. And lion’s mane’s cognitive story is almost entirely a chronic supplementation narrative — single-dose protocols appear ineffective.

Section 01
Longevity & Performance Science

Two stories this cycle update the mechanistic map of how we age at the cellular level. The sprint interval mitochondria paper forces a revision of the simple “exercise = more mitochondria” model: the pathway activated by intensity-dependent stress (UPRmt) is fundamentally about quality control, not quantity expansion — a distinction with direct programming implications. The senolytics Alzheimer’s Phase 1 result is the counterpoint: eliminating senescent cells in tissue is not the same as restoring function, and the cognitive translation gap remains wide.

The mechanistic divergence between SIT and MICT now has nanometre-resolution electron tomography evidence behind it. This matters beyond exercise physiology: the UPRmt pathway is an active therapeutic target for metabolic disease and age-related mitochondrial dysfunction, and the sprint stimulus is one of the only known behavioural ways to activate it in vivo.

Sprint Interval Exercise Disrupts Mitochondrial Ultrastructure and Triggers the Mitochondrial Unfolded Protein Response — Nature Communications, 2025
Mechanistic / Human

What they did: Botella, Perri, Caruana et al. (2025) used advanced electron microscopy and electron tomography to examine mitochondrial ultrastructure in skeletal muscle before and after sprint interval exercise (SIE) vs. moderate-intensity continuous exercise (MICE), then followed participants through 8 weeks of sprint interval training (SIT) vs. moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT).

Key acute finding: SIE — but not MICE — disrupted cristae architecture: transient fragmentation and altered curvature patterns were quantified at nanometre resolution. This structural disruption activated the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt) and the integrated stress response (ISR), with upregulation of mitofusins and Drp1 (the fission/fusion regulatory proteins). The signal was a stress-and-remodel event, not progressive accumulation.

8-week training divergence: MICT increased mitochondrial content, complex I activity, and enriched TCA cycle and OXPHOS proteins — the classic biogenesis signature. SIT, by contrast, enriched mitochondrial 1-carbon metabolism and protein quality control pathways, with improvements in mitochondrial respiratory function via a structurally distinct route. Both improved function; neither route was redundant.

Why it matters: The UPRmt is increasingly understood as a longevity-relevant pathway — it removes misfolded and dysfunctional proteins within mitochondria, a process that declines with age. Sprint exercise is one of the few behaviourally accessible activators of this pathway. The implication for training design is that SIT and endurance work are not interchangeable stimuli even when VO2max outcomes are similar.

Source: Botella J, Perri E, Caruana NJ et al. · Nature Communications · 2025 · DOI: 10.1038/s41467-025-66625-8
Dasatinib + Quercetin Phase 1 Trial in Alzheimer’s Disease: No Clinically Significant Signal — Neurotherapeutics, 2025
Phase 1 Human Trial

A Phase 1 safety trial of the senolytic combination dasatinib + quercetin (D+Q) in Alzheimer’s disease patients — published in Neurotherapeutics, 2025 — returned very few statistically significant results and no suggestion of clinical effectiveness. The trial was designed primarily for safety and feasibility (not powered for efficacy), but the absence of any biomarker signal is notable given the field’s expectations.

Context: the same D+Q combination reduced adipose tissue senescent cell burden within 11 days in a 2019 open-label pilot with diabetic kidney disease patients — so cellular clearance does appear to occur in peripheral tissue. The Alzheimer’s null signal suggests either that senescent cell clearance in the brain requires different dosing or duration, or that neuroinflammation in AD is maintained by mechanisms beyond senescent cell accumulation alone.

The senolytic field is maturing past the simple “clear senescent cells → slow aging” narrative. An ongoing pilot (NCT05838560) is testing D+Q in schizophrenia and treatment-resistant depression, which may provide the clearest signal on whether CNS function is addressable at all by this drug class.

Source: Neurotherapeutics (2025) · Phase 1 D+Q Alzheimer’s trial · Mayo Clinic / UT Health San Antonio
⚡ Implication: Program sprints explicitly as a separate stimulus from endurance work — not a harder version of it. Even 4×30s all-out efforts (Wingate protocol) or 8×20s hill sprints are sufficient to engage the UPRmt pathway. Keep them to 1–2 sessions per week on non-resistance-training days. On the senolytics front: D+Q remains a research-grade intervention; the currently available quercetin supplements activate AMPK and have some anti-inflammatory evidence independently (see §03), but should not be positioned as a senolytic protocol without medical supervision.
Section 02
Training & Performance

Cold water immersion (CWI) and contrast therapy are firmly established as effective recovery modalities for endurance and team-sport athletes. The nuance that matters for PPL training is that the same vasoconstriction/inflammation-suppression mechanism that reduces DOMS and accelerates sprint recovery also directly attenuates the mTOR-mediated hypertrophy signalling that makes resistance training work. Timing your cold exposure is not optional if you care about maximising both.

Cold Water Immersion and Contrast Therapy: Recovery Benefits Are Real, But Timing Relative to Resistance Training Matters — Meta-Analysis Evidence
Meta-analysis

What the evidence shows: A meta-analysis of 23 studies with 606 participants found CWI enhanced sprint performance 24 hours post-exercise significantly over passive recovery. A systematic review of 18 contrast therapy trials found reduced muscle soreness at 24h, 48h, 72h, and 96h post-exercise versus passive recovery. A 2026 sauna + cold plunge protocol (3 rounds of 15 min sauna / 5 min CWI) showed near-doubling of serum IL-6 — consistent with a systemic inflammatory signalling cascade that may mediate some of the adaptation benefit.

The resistance training conflict: The issue is mechanistic. Acute inflammation and mTOR activation after resistance training are not just discomfort — they are the anabolic signalling cascade. Studies including Roberts et al. (Journal of Physiology, 2015) showed CWI applied within 1 hour of resistance training attenuated long-term muscle mass and strength gains versus active recovery. The effect persisted over 12 weeks. CWI appears to blunt satellite cell activity and downstream IGF-1/mTOR signalling in a time-sensitive window.

Practical protocol: Optimal contrast therapy ratio is approximately 3:1 hot-to-cold (e.g., 15 min sauna + 3–5 min cold). Evidence strongest for recovery from team sport, sprinting, and high-volume endurance; weaker for resistance-only training. Minimum separation from resistance training: 6+ hours, or confine CWI to rest days and cardio/sprint days only.

Source: Multiple meta-analyses · Roberts LA et al., Journal of Physiology 2015 · Contrast therapy duration review, Conscious Body Recovery 2026
Use CWI / Contrast Therapy For
  • Sprint days, HIIT days, and endurance sessions
  • DOMS management after high-volume lower body PPL sessions (if >6h after training)
  • Match-play or back-to-back training days where recovery speed matters
  • Systemic recovery via IL-6 / anti-inflammatory cascade
Avoid CWI Within 6h Of
  • Hypertrophy-focused resistance training sessions
  • Strength / power sessions where progressive overload is the goal
  • Post-RT window when satellite cell activation is at peak
  • Immediately post-eccentric-dominant loading (mTOR suppression risk is highest)
⚡ Implication: On a PPL split, reserve the cold plunge for leg cardio days, sprint sessions, or rest-day recovery — not post-push or post-pull sessions. If you use a sauna + cold routine daily, move it to the morning and train in the afternoon/evening so the signalling windows don’t overlap.
Section 03
Supplements & Nutrition

Two supplements with genuine mechanistic interest — lion’s mane and berberine — are both substantially better understood than their marketing suggests. Lion’s mane’s cognitive story collapses under acute dosing and only survives in chronic supplementation trials using standardised erinacine A-enriched mycelium extract. Berberine’s AMPK story has been consolidated into a multi-system model — metabolic, immune, and microbial simultaneously — but the longevity endpoint data in humans remains absent. Evidence ratings are moderate for both.

Lion’s Mane (Hericium erinaceus): Acute Dose RCT Shows No Global Cognitive Benefit; Chronic Erinacine A Evidence Stronger — Frontiers in Nutrition, 2025
RCT (Acute, N=18)

Evidence rating: ★★ (chronic, standardised extract) / ★ (acute dosing)

The 2025 acute RCT (Surendran et al., Frontiers in Nutrition): 18 healthy adults (18–35 years) received a single 3g dose of 10:1 fruiting body extract (equivalent to 30g fresh mushroom) in a double-blind crossover design. Primary outcome: no significant improvement in global cognitive performance or mood. Fine motor speed improved on the Pegboard test (p<0.001 both hands), but executive function tasks (Flanker, Trails B) were actually worse post-dose. The authors concluded single-dose fruiting body extract is insufficient to produce cognitive benefit and flagged the need for chronic supplementation studies.

The chronic erinacine A case: A 49-week double-blind RCT using erinacine A-enriched Hericium erinaceus mycelium — not fruiting body — showed meaningful MMSE improvement and better contrast sensitivity and white matter organisation in older adults with mild cognitive impairment. The mechanism is NGF (nerve growth factor) upregulation: hericenones and erinacines upregulate NGF synthesis in nerve cells, but the pathway requires sustained bioavailability across the blood-brain barrier, which erinacines from mycelium cross more reliably than hericenones from fruiting body.

Practical conclusion: If supplementing for neurological benefit, the evidence supports mycelium-sourced extract standardised for erinacine A content — not fruiting body powder — and requires at least 12 weeks of consistent daily use. Nammex has released a standardised erinacine A mycelium ingredient with analytical verification (2026), which raises the bar for product selection.

Source: Surendran S et al. · Frontiers in Nutrition · 2025 · PMC12018234 · 49-week erinacine A RCT: Mori K et al., J Agric Food Chem · Nammex standardised ingredient launch, 2026
Berberine as a Negentropic Modulator: AMPK-Centred Multi-System Coordination Across Metabolic, Immune, and Microbial Pathways — PMC Review, 2026
Narrative Review

Evidence rating: ★★ (metabolic endpoints, T2D populations) / ★ (longevity/aging endpoints, healthy adults)

A 2026 review (PMC12841219) frames berberine not as a single-target compound but as a “negentropic modulator” — a compound that restores network coordination across three simultaneous systems: metabolic (glucose/lipid regulation via AMPK), immune (NF-κB inhibition, anti-inflammatory), and microbial (gut microbiome composition). The mechanistic convergence point is AMPK activation, which simultaneously reduces hepatic glucose output, improves insulin sensitivity, modulates mitochondrial biogenesis, and coordinates with microbiome species that independently regulate the same pathways.

The human clinical evidence is strongest in T2D populations: a landmark 2008 Metabolism trial (500mg TID vs. metformin) showed near-identical HbA1c, fasting glucose, and postprandial glucose reductions over 3 months. However, no long-term trial has tested whether berberine extends lifespan or slows biological aging measured by epigenetic clocks (PhenoAge, GrimAge, DunedinPACE) in healthy adults — this is the critical evidence gap.

Practical note: standard bioavailability is low (~5%), which is why doses of 500mg TID (not once daily) are used in efficacy trials. Dihydroberberine (DHB) formulations claim 5× higher bioavailability at lower doses — one small 2022 trial (N=33) supported this — but DHB product-specific evidence is still thin. Berberine is competitive with metformin on glucose metrics for metabolically compromised individuals; evidence for use in healthy, well-trained adults is not well-established.

Source: PMC12841219 · Berberine: A Negentropic Modulator for Multi-System Coordination · 2026 · Yin J et al., Metabolism 2008
⚡ Implication: For lion’s mane: if you’re taking it as a daily capsule of generic “lion’s mane powder,” check whether it specifies erinacine A content and whether it’s mycelium-sourced — most off-the-shelf products are fruiting body and unstandardised. For berberine: meaningful at 500mg TID if metabolic markers (fasting glucose, HbA1c, lipids) warrant it; not established as a longevity compound for otherwise healthy individuals with normal metabolic function.

POLYCAD Trial — Spermidine (24mg/day) vs. Placebo in Coronary Artery Disease — First Cardiovascular Endpoint Data
The POLYCAD trial (NCT06186102, Aarhus University Hospital, Denmark) is the first randomised double-blind placebo-controlled study of high-dose oral spermidine in cardiovascular disease. 200 patients, 1:1 randomisation, 24mg/day vs. placebo for one year. Primary endpoints include cardiovascular, skeletal muscle, metabolic, inflammatory, and cognitive outcomes. Recruitment completed August 2025; trial completion anticipated August 2026. Spermidine activates autophagy, improves mitochondrial function, and has shown cardiovascular ageing benefits in preclinical models — this is the first adequately powered human trial. Watch for the preprint release, likely Q4 2026.

Sprint Interval Training UPRmt — Validation Needed in Women, Older Adults, and Trained Athletes
The Botella et al. Nature Communications study was conducted in healthy young men only — a standard limitation for invasive muscle biopsy protocols. The UPRmt activation signal needs replication in women (hormonal cycle affects mitochondrial dynamics), older adults (where baseline UPRmt function is impaired), and trained athletes (where adaptation rates differ from untrained). Watch for follow-up from the Botella/Caruana group and from labs at the Karolinska and Copenhagen that have existing SIT infrastructure. Timeline: likely 2027.

D+Q Senolytics in Mental Disorders — NCT05838560 Safety & Feasibility Pilot
Following the null Alzheimer’s Phase 1 result, a pilot study (NCT05838560) is now evaluating D+Q in schizophrenia/schizoaffective disorder and treatment-resistant depression. The hypothesis is that senescent cell accumulation contributes to the neuroinflammatory substrate underlying both conditions. This is the most directly translatable senolytic CNS trial currently enrolling. Status: ongoing. Watch for feasibility data publication — if safety holds and any biomarker signal emerges, it would justify a Phase 2 in a better-powered design.

The 49-week Mori et al. erinacine A trial remains the strongest lion’s mane cognitive evidence, but it was conducted in older adults with mild cognitive impairment in Japan (N=30). suPAR (Soluble Urokinase Plasminogen Activator Receptor) — Emerging Longevity Inflammatory Marker
suPAR has outperformed CRP and IL-6 as a mortality predictor in multiple European cohort studies and is now entering the direct-to-consumer longevity testing market. Unlike hsCRP, which reflects acute inflammation, suPAR reflects chronic immune activation and predicts incident cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, and all-cause mortality independently of traditional risk factors. DirectCareLabs is now offering it; Australian labs are beginning to follow. Worth adding to the next comprehensive blood panel alongside ApoB, HbA1c, and a biological age clock.
