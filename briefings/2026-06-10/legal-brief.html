Supreme Court Hands the SEC a Sharper Sword: Sripetch v. SEC Lets Courts Order Disgorgement Without Proof That Any Investor Lost a Dollar

“Whatever else traditional equitable principles demand, they do not require a showing of pecuniary loss before a court may issue an award of unjust profits.” Justice Neil Gorsuch, writing for a unanimous Court — Sripetch v. SEC, No. 25-466 (decided 4 June 2026)

On 4 June the Supreme Court ruled 9–0, in an opinion by Justice Gorsuch, that the SEC need not prove investors suffered actual financial loss to obtain disgorgement of a defendant’s “ill-gotten gains.” The measure, the Court held, is the wrongdoer’s gain, not the victim’s loss — resolving a circuit split and removing a defense that securities-fraud targets had increasingly raised since Liu v. SEC (2020).

The defendant, Ongkaruck Sripetch, ran classic pump-and-dump schemes across at least 20 penny-stock companies. After consenting to judgment on six counts of securities fraud and one count of selling unregistered securities, he argued the SEC could not collect the more than $4 million it sought because it had not shown his schemes caused identifiable investor losses. The Court rejected that framing: the threshold equity has always required is “interference with a legal right,” not a showing of pecuniary harm.

For crypto, the significance is structural rather than headline-grabbing. Disgorgement is the SEC’s workhorse remedy against token issuers, ICO-era promoters and unregistered-offering defendants — precisely the population where “no investor was actually harmed” (token went up, buyers profited) has been a recurring defense. Sripetch closes that escape hatch even as the Atkins-era SEC formally retreats from regulation-by-enforcement.

📎 Historical Precedent: Liu v. SEC, 591 U.S. 71 (2020), capped disgorgement at net profits “awarded for victims”; Kokesh v. SEC (2017) subjected it to a five-year limitations clock. Sripetch is the third instalment — and the first to expand, rather than constrain, the remedy.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Sullivan & Cromwell’s June memo reads the decision as “declining to limit” SEC disgorgement and notes it removes a defense that had real settlement leverage; Gibson Dunn and DLA Piper June alerts agree it resolves a circuit split in the Commission’s favor. The practical effect: an SEC that is dismissing crypto cases on the front end keeps a maximal remedy on the back end for the cases it does bring. The Brief’s read: the wins-and-losses split is counter-intuitive — a deregulatory Commission just got handed a tougher disgorgement tool, which raises the stakes of which cases Atkins chooses to file. Expect disgorgement demands to anchor higher in negotiated resolutions, because “you can’t prove anyone lost money” is now off the table.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The Public Knowledge / defense-bar view is that Sripetch changes less than the headline suggests: the SEC still faces Jarkesy (2024) limits on in-house penalty adjudication, disgorgement remains subject to the Kokesh five-year clock, and a deregulatory Commission filing fewer crypto cases blunts the real-world impact. The Brief’s read: true at today’s enforcement tempo, but remedies law outlives administrations — a future Commission inherits a disgorgement tool with one fewer constraint, and that is a durable shift regardless of the current filing pace.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Most directly exposed: ICO-era issuers and promoters with unresolved SEC matters, penny-stock and micro-cap fraud defendants, and any token project where the “buyers profited, so no harm” argument was load-bearing. D&O insurers and enforcement-defense practices (Gibson Dunn, S&C, DLA Piper all published within hours) will reprice disgorgement exposure upward. Large exchanges whose cases were already dismissed in 2025 (Coinbase, Kraken, Consensys) are insulated — their resolved matters are not reopened by a remedies ruling. The Brief’s read: watch settlement math, not new filings, for the first evidence of impact.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish Enforcement-defense and D&O insurance complexes reprice risk; broadly neutral-to-positive for compliant majors whose legacy cases are already closed.

🔴 Bearish Micro-cap / ICO-era issuers and promoters with live or latent SEC exposure — disgorgement demands now anchor higher with no “no-loss” offramp.

⚪ Neutral COIN, large CEX equities — their dismissed 2025 matters are untouched by a remedies holding.

📋 What To Do — This Week
TraderRe-screen micro-cap and ICO-vintage token exposures for unresolved SEC matters; the “price went up, no harm” thesis no longer caps downside.
CounselStrike “SEC can’t prove investor loss” from your disgorgement-defense playbook; re-model settlement ranges against gross gains, not net investor harm.

Visa, Mastercard, Stripe and Coinbase Reportedly in Talks to Launch a Joint Stablecoin — Circle Stock Drops 11% as the GENIUS Act Opens the Duopoly

“If anything comes of this, it will have big implications for a stablecoin market whose value is above $300 billion — this move integrates stablecoins more deeply into traditional payment rails, directly threatening Circle’s business model.” [paraphrased] Fortune Crypto, summarising the reported Visa–Mastercard–Stripe–Coinbase consortium, 8 June 2026

Visa and Mastercard are reportedly in talks with Stripe and Coinbase to stand up a jointly governed stablecoin platform, according to The Information (3 June) and Fortune (8 June). There is no signed deal — sources stress there may not even be MOUs yet — but the market reacted immediately: Circle (CRCL) fell roughly 11% and Coinbase (COIN) also slipped on the report.

The legal enabler is the GENIUS Act. By creating a federal “permitted payment stablecoin issuer” charter and routing issuance through regulated banks and trust companies, the statute lets card networks and payment processors participate without each becoming a de facto bank holding company. Circle and Tether together command roughly 80% of the market; the reported consortium pairs that regulatory on-ramp with distribution networks — billions of cards and merchant endpoints — that dwarf anything crypto-native.

This is a distinct development from the France-led Qivalis euro-stablecoin consortium covered earlier this month: that is a 12-bank European, euro-denominated play; this is a US dollar, card-network play aimed squarely at USDC’s payments moat.

📎 Historical Precedent: Libra/Diem (2019–2022) was the last time payment giants tried a consortium stablecoin — killed by the absence of a legal framework. The GENIUS Act is precisely the framework whose absence sank Diem.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Finovate’s banking analysis frames the alliance as a warning shot to banks: distribution, not issuance mechanics, is the contested moat now that GENIUS standardises the issuer side. The open legal questions are governance and antitrust — a jointly owned stablecoin among horizontal competitors (Visa and Mastercard are direct rivals) invites the same network-governance scrutiny that has dogged card interchange for two decades. The Brief’s read: the regulatory risk has migrated from “can they issue?” (yes, under GENIUS) to “can they co-own it?” (antitrust). Expect any real structure to route issuance through a single chartered entity (likely a bank partner) with the networks as distributors, to keep the horizontal-collusion surface small.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The skeptical read — echoed in CoinDesk’s own framing — is that this is “talks,” and talks happen constantly; Diem proved that incumbents’ consortium stablecoins die in committee over governance even when the tech is trivial. The Brief’s read: the 11% CRCL move says the market is pricing distribution risk regardless of whether this specific deal closes — the threat to USDC’s payments margin is real even if Visa/Mastercard never ship a coin, because it signals the networks will not cede stablecoin rails by default.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Most exposed: Circle (CRCL) — USDC is the product in the crosshairs and its premium valuation assumes payments adoption the networks could capture instead. Beneficiaries if it ships: Visa (V), Mastercard (MA), privately held Stripe, and Coinbase (COIN) if it joins as distributor and on/off-ramp. Tether (USDT) is more insulated — its offshore, dollar-shadow franchise is less dependent on US card rails. Bank partners providing the GENIUS charter (Anchorage, Paxos and trust-chartered peers) gain a wholesale issuance role. The Brief’s read: Circle’s equity is now a proxy bet on whether stablecoin economics accrue to issuers or distributors.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish V, MA — optionality on capturing stablecoin settlement rails; bank/trust GENIUS charter-holders (Paxos, Anchorage) as issuance partners.

🔴 Bearish CRCL — USDC’s payments moat is the direct target; −11% on the report and premium multiple now contested.

⚪ Neutral COIN (ambiguous — loses if competitor coin wins share, gains if it joins as distributor); USDT.

📋 What To Do — Next 30 Days
TraderTreat CRCL as a distribution-vs-issuance proxy; size for headline whipsaw — confirmation or denial of MOUs will move it double digits either way.
CounselIf advising any party, pre-clear the horizontal-governance/antitrust structure now; a single chartered issuer with networks as distributors is the cleaner build.
ComplianceMap which GENIUS “permitted issuer” charter any consortium coin would sit under, and the AML/sanctions program that comes with it (FinCEN/OFAC rules closed comment 9 June).
FounderIf you build on USDC, add a settlement-asset abstraction layer now — don’t hard-code a single issuer into payment flows.

UpdateCLARITY Act Stalls on Ethics and Illicit-Finance Impasse as the White House Convenes Law Enforcement — Passage Odds Collapse to 47%

“It’s pro-regulatory and pro-law enforcement.” Patrick Witt, White House crypto adviser, defending the CLARITY Act ahead of the law-enforcement meeting (via The Block)

New since the 9 June brief: the White House this week convened law-enforcement groups to address objections that CLARITY’s illicit-finance provisions — drawn partly from the Blockchain Regulatory Certainty Act — could hamper money-laundering investigations, even as Polymarket bettors cut the bill’s 2026 enactment odds to roughly 47% (from ~74% a month ago) and Galaxy Research trimmed its estimate to ~60%.

The calendar is the enemy. The bill was placed on the Senate Legislative Calendar on 1 June, but a 5 June procedural vote to reauthorise Section 702 of FISA failed, meaning FISA dominates floor time the following week; a late-May anti-weaponization-fund debate already burned another week. Usable floor time before the August recess has contracted sharply, and there is still no confirmed floor vote date.

The substantive blocker is unchanged but now decisive: Sen. Angela Alsobrooks (D-MD) — one of only two Democrats who advanced the bill in Banking on 14 May — says she will not support a floor vote unless it adds a provision covering government officials’ crypto holdings, a direct reference to the Trump family’s $TRUMP/$MELANIA and World Liberty businesses. The committee already killed a Van Hollen ethics amendment 13–11 on procedural grounds.

📎 Historical Precedent: FIT21 passed the House in May 2024 (279–136) then died in the Senate for want of floor time — the same calendar-and-crossover trap CLARITY is now walking into.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Elliptic and Fortune both frame the ethics fight as the binding constraint: the two Democratic crossover votes from Banking are conditional, and without them there is no 60-vote path. JPMorgan analysts (cited by The Block) have said enactment odds are shrinking as obstacles overlap — ethics, illicit-finance, and the stablecoin-yield question all live in the same bill. The Brief’s read: the White House law-enforcement meeting is a tell that the administration now sees illicit-finance objections, not just ethics, as a vote-mover — you don’t convene FBI/DOJ stakeholders to soothe a problem you think is solved. Base case is slippage past the July 4 target into a narrow pre-August window, with real tail risk the bill doesn’t clear in 2026.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The optimistic case (Sen. Lummis, the White House) is that a credible floor commitment from Majority Leader Thune for early-to-mid July still threads the needle, and that the SEC–CFTC joint interpretation has already delivered much of CLARITY’s substance administratively, lowering the stakes of delay. The Brief’s read: the administrative backstop is real and cuts against panic — but it also reduces the urgency that forces a vote, which is exactly why a 47% market price looks more honest than the “four steps to a July 4 signing” timelines.

🏗️ Industry Implications

The 200-plus firms that signed the June letter — Coinbase, Ripple, Kraken, a16z, Circle, Binance.US — have spent political capital on a bill whose odds are now a coin flip. Exchanges seeking CFTC spot-market jurisdiction and a registration path are most exposed to delay; DeFi developers waiting on CLARITY’s safe harbors stay in legal limbo. The Brief’s read: the contingency plan is the SEC’s token taxonomy and the CFTC’s pilot programs — expect industry to quietly lean harder on the regulators while keeping the legislative push alive for optics.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish Nothing clean — a clear July floor commitment would re-rate COIN and broad alt exposure, but that is not today’s base case.

🔴 Bearish COIN and CFTC-jurisdiction hopefuls — delay extends the registration-path vacuum; alt tokens awaiting “digital commodity” clarity drift.

⚪ Neutral BTC, ETH — already treated as digital commodities under the March SEC–CFTC interpretation; legislative outcome is second-order.

FCC Opens AI Preemption Proceeding on Trump’s Order — the State-Law Override Fight Moves From Congress to a Regulator With Murky Authority

“It asks the FCC to initiate a proceeding. We’re going to initiate a proceeding. We’re open-minded on where that goes.” FCC Chairman Brendan Carr, on the Trump executive-order directive (via FedScoop)

The 9 June trigger built into President Trump’s 11 December 2025 AI executive order has arrived: the FCC is to initiate a proceeding on whether to adopt a federal AI reporting-and-disclosure standard that preempts conflicting state laws. The directive pairs Chairman Carr with White House AI & crypto adviser David Sacks, and follows Congress’s repeated failure to legislate a moratorium — it was stripped from the budget-reconciliation bill 99–1 in 2025 and omitted from the 2026 NDAA.

The legal problem is foundational. Commentators across the spectrum — Public Knowledge, Ropes & Gray, the Institute for Law & AI — note that “nothing in the Communications Act confers FCC authority to regulate AI,” making any preemptive rule a near-certain target for litigation the moment it issues. The order routes around a Congress that won’t act by tasking an agency that may lack the power to.

📎 Historical Precedent: The FCC’s 2017–18 attempt to preempt state net-neutrality laws was rejected in Mozilla v. FCC (D.C. Cir. 2019), which held the agency could not preempt state law it lacked authority to regulate — the precise vulnerability here.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Manatt and Buchanan Ingersoll read the order as a “strategy, not a rule” — the preemption claim will be made through a future rulemaking and DOJ litigation task force, not the EO itself, which means years of uncertainty for multistate AI deployers. Ropes & Gray flags the Mozilla problem directly: preemption authority is parasitic on regulatory authority the FCC may not have over AI. The Brief’s read: the realistic near-term effect is not preemption but chilling — states with pending AI bills (and the 19 that passed measures in 2026) now legislate under threat of federal challenge, while companies face a widening, not narrowing, patchwork until a court rules. Carr’s “open-minded on where that goes” is a tell that even the agency doubts the endpoint.

🔄 Contrarian Take

The administration’s view (Sacks, Latham’s read of the EO) is that a single federal disclosure standard is genuinely pro-innovation and that the patchwork — Colorado, Illinois, California, Texas all diverging — imposes real compliance drag the FCC route can cut through faster than a deadlocked Congress. The Brief’s read: the policy goal is defensible; the instrument is not. Using the FCC to do what Congress refused twice invites a Mozilla rerun, and a loss in court would entrench the patchwork it aimed to erase.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) and large multistate AI deployers nominally benefit from preemption but bear the cost of years of legal uncertainty; state-AI-compliance vendors and plaintiffs’ firms gain from prolonged ambiguity. Colorado — which gutted and delayed its AI Act to 2027 — and Illinois, tightening, sit on opposite sides of the split the FCC is now wading into. The Brief’s read: until a court speaks, the prudent posture is comply-with-the-strictest-state, because a preemption rule that may be vacated is not a compliance plan.

📊 Portfolio Impact

🟩 Bullish Frontier AI labs and large deployers if preemption survives — a single federal disclosure standard lowers multistate compliance cost.

🔴 Bearish State-AI-compliance software vendors and GRC tooling whose TAM depends on a persistent patchwork.

⚪ Neutral Most public AI names — the murky FCC authority and litigation horizon make this a multi-year overhang, not a 2026 catalyst.

📋 What To Do — This Quarter
TraderDon’t trade this as a near-term catalyst — the binding event is a court ruling on FCC authority, likely 2027; AI-compliance vendors are the cleaner short/long expression.
CounselContinue compliance to the strictest applicable state (Illinois/California) and preserve Mozilla-style challenge arguments — a preemption rule is not yet a safe harbor.
ComplianceMap your AI systems against the 2026 state-law patchwork now; assume divergence persists through any FCC rulemaking and litigation.
FounderBuild transparency/disclosure tooling that satisfies the strictest state standard — it future-proofs against both the patchwork and any federal floor.

California’s Crypto Licensing Regime Goes Live: Firms Serving State Residents Must File a DFAL Application by July 1 or Exit

By 1 July 2026, any entity conducting covered digital-financial-asset activity with California residents must hold a DFAL licence, have submitted a completed application through the NMLS, or qualify for a statutory exemption. [paraphrased — DFPI guidance] California Department of Financial Protection & Innovation, Digital Financial Assets Law implementation

California’s Digital Financial Assets Law (DFAL, signed October 2023) reaches its operative deadline in three weeks. From 1 July, exchanging, transferring, storing or issuing digital financial assets for California residents requires a DFPI licence — or at minimum a completed application filed via the Nationwide Multistate Licensing System, which the DFPI has accepted since 9 March 2026. It is, in effect, a BitLicense-style regime for the largest US state economy.

Exemptions matter: federal/state government entities, insured banks and credit unions, registered broker-dealers and CFTC-regulated traders, merchants accepting crypto solely for goods/services, personal/household use, and businesses under $50,000 in annual California digital-asset activity fall outside the regime. Licensees must hold sufficient assets to satisfy resident entitlements and meet capital/liquidity standards.

📎 Historical Precedent: New York’s BitLicense (2015) is the template — and its cautionary tale: a wave of “Bitlicense exodus” departures followed as smaller firms geofenced the state rather than comply. Watch for California geofencing by sub-scale operators.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Womble Bond Dickinson and the National Law Review stress the “completed application” safe harbor: firms that file by 1 July can keep operating during DFPI review, so the real deadline is application-readiness, not licence-in-hand. The harder question is federal-state overlap — how DFAL coexists with a future CLARITY/CFTC spot-market regime and with the GENIUS Act’s federal stablecoin charter. The Brief’s read: this lands in the same fortnight as the MiCA transition (1 July) and the ASIC AFSL deadline (30 June) — a global compliance-deadline cluster that will stretch legal and licensing teams thin. Federal preemption could eventually narrow DFAL, but not before firms must comply.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Large exchanges (Coinbase, Kraken — both California-touching) have the resources to file; the squeeze falls on mid-tier and startup VASPs weighing California compliance cost against geofencing. GENIUS-chartered stablecoin issuers and federally registered broker-dealers benefit from exemptions, reinforcing the regulatory advantage of a federal charter. The Brief’s read: expect a modest “California exodus” at the long tail and a quiet acceleration of the federal-charter-as-shield thesis.

Bank of England Signals Retreat on Stablecoin Holding Caps After Lords Rebuke — Breeden Concedes the £20k Limit Was “Overly Conservative”

The proposed holding limits were “overly conservative,” and the Bank is prepared to ease them. [paraphrased] Sarah Breeden, Deputy Governor for Financial Stability, Bank of England (via CoinDesk / Cointelegraph reporting)

New development: the Bank of England is signalling it will soften the individual £20,000 / business £10m holding limits proposed in its November 2025 systemic-stablecoin consultation, with Deputy Governor Sarah Breeden reportedly conceding the caps were “overly conservative.” This is the regulator’s own pivot — distinct from the 2 June House of Lords committee report that urged the Bank to reconsider, which the Bank now appears to be heeding rather than resisting.

The substance: systemic sterling-stablecoin issuers would back coins with up to 60% short-term UK government debt and 40% unremunerated Bank reserves (95% gilts for issuers transitioning from the FCA regime at launch). The Lords had argued the Bank should monitor market growth and impose limits only if financial-stability risk clearly warrants — the position the Bank is now drifting toward.

📎 Historical Precedent: The Bank’s 2023 discussion paper floated even tighter (£10k–£20k) retail limits; the trajectory since has been steadily upward in tolerance — consistent with a UK trying not to cede stablecoin issuance to dollar incumbents or the EU.
⚖️ Practitioner Analysis

Slaughter and May and Reed Smith client notes had flagged the holding limits as the single most commercially damaging feature of the BoE regime — a cap that would make sterling systemic stablecoins unusable for corporate treasury at scale. The FMLC’s February response argued the limits were disproportionate to the financial-stability aim. The Brief’s read: Breeden’s concession is a meaningful tell that the UK is choosing competitiveness over precaution — a divergence from both the EU’s MiCA strictness and the US’s GENIUS framework that has no holding caps at all. Final Codes of Practice later in 2026 are the document to watch; expect higher or monitoring-based limits.

🏗️ Industry Implications

Sterling-stablecoin hopefuls and UK fintechs (and global issuers eyeing a GBP product) gain materially if caps rise — corporate-treasury use cases become viable. UK banks, which lobbied for tight limits to protect deposits, lose ground. The Brief’s read: this is one more data point that the UK wants to be the looser-than-EU venue for digital money post-Brexit — coherent with the FCA’s September authorisation gateway and the broader competitiveness mandate.
