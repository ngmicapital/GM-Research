An Empire of 12 Million People, Zero Writing, and a Code We Still Cannot Crack

The Inca state administered the largest empire in the Americas — stretching 4,000 kilometres from Ecuador to Chile, governing 12 million people across 100-plus ethnic groups — without a single written word. Every census, tax record, military tally, and tribute figure was encoded in knotted strings called quipus. The Spanish burned most of them in 1583. Around 923 survive. We still cannot fully read them.

The Inca Empire governed 12 million people across 4,000 kilometres of the Andes without a writing system of any kind, encoding every census, tribute, and military record in knotted strings called quipus operated by a professional class of specialists. The Spanish colonial authorities burned most of them in 1583, declaring them instruments of the devil. Around 923 survive in museums on three continents — and despite breakthroughs at Harvard and MIT, their full meaning remains one of the great unsolved puzzles of ancient history.

TL;DR

Every major pre-modern empire that reached comparable scale had writing: cuneiform for Babylon, hieroglyphics for Egypt, glyphs for the Maya. The Inca had none of it. No alphabet, no syllabary, no pictographic system. And yet Tawantinsuyu — the four-part empire of the sun — ran a functioning census, levied taxes across a dozen climate zones, coordinated armies across 4,000 kilometres, and managed the redistribution of surplus food to millions. The recording technology was knotted string. The specialists who operated it trained for years to read what looked, to everyone else, like decorative rope. When the Spanish arrived and couldn’t decode the system, they destroyed most of it. The survivors sit in museum cases from Lima to Berlin. We have decoded the arithmetic. The rest — the parts that may be narrative, historical, or something that doesn’t map to any category we have — remains open.

Section 01

The Story

By the early sixteenth century, the Inca state was the largest empire in the Western Hemisphere and the longest territorial empire on Earth. Tawantinsuyu — a Quechua word meaning “the four parts together” — ran from what is now the southern border of Colombia to central Chile: 4,000 kilometres of Pacific coast, Andean altitude, and Amazonian jungle, connected by a road network of approximately 40,000 kilometres. The population, estimated by scholars at between 9 and 14 million at peak, was organised into four administrative quarters, each subdivided down to units of ten households. It was one of history’s more formidable administrative achievements.

What makes it strange, given that historical context, is what the Inca did not have. The Aztecs maintained pictographic records. The Maya developed one of the most sophisticated hieroglyphic scripts of any civilisation. Egypt had papyrus and hieroglyphics. Babylon had cuneiform on clay tablets — and writing had, in fact, emerged in Mesopotamia precisely to solve the problem of administering complex economies at scale. The logic of the historical pattern suggests that large, complex empires require writing. The Inca are the conspicuous exception.

The Inca built roads, ran censuses, levied taxes, and provisioned armies across 4,000 kilometres of mountains and desert — without a single written word.

Their recording technology was the quipu (also spelled khipu, from the Quechua word for “knot”). A quipu was a bundle of cords — cotton or camelid wool, sometimes both — hanging from a primary horizontal cord between half a metre and a metre long. Each quipu contained anywhere from a few pendant cords to several hundred. Those pendant cords could branch into subsidiary cords, which could branch again, creating hierarchical data structures analogous to nested spreadsheet columns. The cords themselves varied in colour, twist direction, and the way they were attached to the primary cord. The knots on each cord carried numerical values. The whole object was essentially a three-dimensional database made of fibre.

The specialists who operated quipus were called quipucamayocs — literally “those in charge of the knots.” Spanish chroniclers described them as indispensable to provincial governance: summoned to the court at Cusco to recite census figures and tribute records, quipu in hand, from memory and from the object simultaneously. The quipu was both the record and the mnemonic, and the quipucamayoc was the interface between the two. When the Sapa Inca needed to know how many llamas the province of Hatun Xauxa had contributed to the state herds two years ago, there was a quipucamayoc who could answer that, precisely, from a bundle of coloured string he had prepared and stored in his household. That man’s role — accountant, archivist, census taker, historian — was hereditary, specialised, and socially prestigious.

The Inca road system — the qhapaq ñan — connected quipus to each other. Relay runners called chasquis carried messages and quipus between post stations spaced roughly every few kilometres along the main roads. A message could travel from Cusco to Quito (approximately 2,000 kilometres) in as little as five days, faster than any horse-based system of comparable distance, because the chasquis ran in short, high-speed relays rather than riding the full distance. The quipu was the payload: a set of knots that encoded military intelligence, population data, or tribute accounts that a runner could not himself read, but that the receiving quipucamayoc could interpret immediately on arrival. The empire communicated at the speed of running, and the information it communicated was written in string.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The numerical system encoded in quipus is now understood, and it is genuinely elegant. Quipus use a base-10 positional notation — the same mathematical structure as the numerals used worldwide today — but implemented physically in knots on a hanging cord. Each pendant cord is read from the bottom upward: the lowest cluster of knots encodes the ones digit, the next cluster up encodes the tens digit, the next hundreds, and so on. The position on the cord determines the place value; the type of knot determines the digit.

Three Knot Types, Infinite Numbers

A long knot — the cord wrapped around itself — encodes the digits 2 through 9, with the number of turns equal to the digit. A figure-eight knot encodes 1. A single overhand knot in the tens or hundreds position encodes that power of 10. An empty space where knots would otherwise appear encodes 0. The system can represent any positive integer, and quipus with values in the tens of thousands have been identified. A zero at the ones position — represented by a gap — is one of the earliest unambiguous instances of positional zero anywhere in the ancient world.

Beyond the numbers, quipus encode categorical information through at least four additional channels. Colour: the cotton or wool was dyed, and different colours indexed different categories of data — resources, populations, geographic regions, livestock types. Spanish colonial documents confirm some of these correspondences: one early account notes that yellow signified gold, white silver, and red military matters, though the full colour-code has not survived. Ply direction: cords were spun in either an S-twist or a Z-twist, and the direction was not decorative. Attachment method: how pendant cords connected to the primary cord (from the front, from the back, or in a looped join) carried meaning. Relative position: the spatial arrangement of cords relative to each other encoded hierarchical relationships — which subsidiary cord’s values rolled up into which parent cord’s total. A well-constructed quipu is not a list; it is a relational database.

The first major breakthrough in quipu reading beyond pure arithmetic came in 2017, when Harvard undergraduate Manuel Medrano, working with professor Gary Urton, compared a set of six quipus held at the Cajamarquilla archaeological site in Peru with a Spanish colonial-era census document from the same region. Medrano noticed that the way each cord was attached to the primary cord appeared to correspond to the social status of the 132 individuals listed in the census. More striking: the colour of each cord seemed to correspond to the phonetic sounds of the individual’s first name. If confirmed and generalised, this would transform the quipu from a numerical device into something closer to a phonetic script — one that could, in principle, encode names and possibly narrative.

Gary Urton, who has spent decades building a database of every known quipu’s formal properties at Harvard’s Peabody Museum, has long argued that quipus contain more information than arithmetic alone can account for. The total number of distinct “binary” choices in a quipu’s construction — twist direction, attachment type, colour combinations, knot type — gives the system enough combinatorial space to encode something like 1,500 distinct signs, comparable to the number of signs in the Sumerian cuneiform script. Whether this space was systematically used for non-numerical encoding, and how, remains the central open question in Andean studies. The MIT Khipu Research Group is applying computational analysis and machine learning to the corpus of surviving quipus to search for patterns that might bridge the numerical and the narrative. As of now, the numerical encoding is solved. The rest is not.

Section 03

The Connections

The burning, and what it cost. The Spanish colonial relationship with quipus was ambivalent for its first decades. Some priests recognised that quipus held administrative records essential to understanding the territories they were governing. Francisco de Toledo, Viceroy of Peru from 1569, ordered an empire-wide survey that produced a set of “statistical quipus” recording the labour obligations and tribute assessments of every community — quipus created by quipucamayocs but recording Spanish-imposed categories. The Jesuits initially studied quipus as potential vehicles for religious instruction. Then the Third Council of Lima, in 1583, classified all quipus as instruments of diabolical idolatry and ordered them burned. Provincial inspections, known as extirpaciones de idolatrías, confiscated and destroyed quipus across the Andes through the seventeenth century. The exact scale of the loss is unknown by definition, but the surviving 923 represent a corpus built on an estimated much larger base that was systematically eliminated within living memory of the conquest.

The village that never stopped reading. One of the most extraordinary surviving connections to the quipu tradition is in the Andean village of San Juan de Collata, in the Huarochirí region of Peru. The community has maintained two quipus in the attic of its colonial church for centuries — not as museum objects but as live community documents. Village elders have described them as containing the history of two Inca generals. Linguist Sabine Hyland, who studied the Collata quipus in 2017, found that they used a far greater variety of cord attachments than administrative quipus — at least 14 distinct types, compared to the 2 or 3 seen in numerical quipus — consistent with a narrative rather than mathematical encoding. If Collata’s elders are right about what they hold, these objects are not accounting records. They are texts. The community’s oral tradition maintains a continuous connection to the objects that no museum can replicate.

Why “No Writing” May Be the Wrong Frame

Writing is typically defined as a system using visible marks to represent language — and by that definition, quipus are not writing. But that definition was developed by scholars studying Mediterranean and Near Eastern traditions where marks on flat surfaces were the norm. Quipus represent language-adjacent information in three-dimensional fibre through a different sensory channel, one in which the quipucamayoc’s fingers read the object as much as the eyes do. The Inca may not have lacked writing so much as used a form of information encoding that the European tradition’s definition of writing was not built to recognise. Whether that constitutes writing is partly a question of terminology and partly the deepest open question in the field.

The parallel with other undeciphered scripts. The quipu sits alongside Linear A (Minoan, c. 1800–1450 BCE), Proto-Elamite (c. 3100–2900 BCE), and the Indus Valley script as one of the world’s major unsolved ancient writing problems. Each of these represents a civilisation that reached considerable complexity and left records humans cannot currently read. The quipu differs from the others in that it is three-dimensional and tactile rather than two-dimensional and visual, which means the standard computational tools developed for script decipherment — frequency analysis, sign inventory mapping, parallel text matching — require adaptation before they can be applied. The MIT group’s current approach treats each quipu as a sequence of encoded tokens and applies natural language processing techniques borrowed from computational linguistics. It is an experiment in progress with no guaranteed outcome.

What the Inca road system tells us. The qhapaq ñan, the Inca road system, was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2014 spanning six countries. Its 40,000 kilometres include roads tunnelled through cliff faces, bridges suspended across Andean gorges, and causeways raised across coastal swamps. The chasqui runner relay system that carried quipus across this network was itself a form of infrastructure, with post stations stocked with food, rested runners, and fresh quipus. The road and the quipu were complementary technologies: one moved objects and people through physical space, the other moved encoded information. Both depended on the same centralised political will and the same organisational logic. The road has left stone evidence that is impossible to dispute. The quipu left fibre evidence that depends on specialists who no longer exist. The asymmetry between the two legacies — one obvious, one opaque — is itself a lesson in what kinds of knowledge survive colonial contact and what kinds do not.

Further Down the Hole

🧵
The Collata Quipus and Living Memory
The village of San Juan de Collata in Peru’s Huarochirí province has maintained two quipus in its church attic for centuries, believed by elders to contain the history of two Inca generals. Linguist Sabine Hyland’s 2017 analysis found 14 distinct cord attachment types in the Collata quipus — far exceeding the 2 or 3 seen in purely numerical quipus — suggesting narrative encoding. The community’s oral tradition preserves a connection to the objects that formal decipherment efforts lack: living context for what the strings mean. A 2024 expedition documented similar “community quipus” in the church of the village of Jucul, previously unknown to outside researchers.
🗺️
The Inca Road System — 40,000 Kilometres Without Wheels
The qhapaq ñan road network stretched 40,000 kilometres through six modern countries, including high-altitude passes at 5,000 metres, suspension bridges over Andean gorges, and coastal causeways. The Inca built it without the wheel, without draft animals capable of pulling carts (llamas could not), and without iron tools. The chasqui relay runners who used it could move information from Cusco to Quito — 2,000 kilometres — in approximately five days. The road was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2014. Most of it remains physically intact enough to hike. It is one of the most durable engineering achievements of any pre-industrial civilisation.
📚
The Harvard-MIT Quipu Research Projects
Harvard’s Peabody Museum holds one of the largest quipu collections in the world. Gary Urton spent decades building the Khipu Database Project, cataloguing every formal property — cord colour, ply direction, knot type, attachment method — of every known quipu, producing a dataset now used by researchers worldwide. MIT’s CSAIL Khipu Research Group has applied computational analysis and NLP-style pattern matching to the corpus, searching for structural regularities that might indicate phonetic encoding. Manuel Medrano and Urton’s 2018 Ethnohistory paper, which matched quipus to colonial census records, is considered the closest thing to a Rosetta Stone the field has produced. The work continues with no completion date in sight.
🔍
Other Scripts That Remain Unread
The quipu is one of several major ancient recording systems that cannot currently be decoded. Linear A — used by the Minoan civilisation of Crete c. 1800–1450 BCE — is completely undeciphered despite its relative script Linear B (Mycenaean Greek) being cracked in 1952. Proto-Elamite from southwestern Iran (c. 3100 BCE) is older than any other writing system and remains unread. The Indus Valley script appears on thousands of stamp seals from Mohenjo-daro and Harappa; no bilingual text has been found. Each unsolved script represents a civilisation whose internal voice we cannot yet hear directly.
🧮
Positional Zero and Pre-Columbian Mathematics
The quipu’s use of an empty space to represent zero in a positional numerical system places the Inca in a small historical group of civilisations that independently invented positional arithmetic. The Babylonians had a placeholder symbol for zero by around 300 BCE; the Maya independently developed zero by roughly 350 CE; Indian mathematicians formalised zero as a number with its own properties around the 7th century CE. The Inca version — an empty section of cord where knots would otherwise appear — is less formally studied but functionally equivalent. How independently these traditions developed, and whether there are others still unrecognised, is an active area of research in the history of mathematics.
