Alpha — Toxic Flow & Adverse Selection

Saturday · 16 May 2026 · Sydney, Australia · Issue #22 11-min read
TL;DR

Adverse selection is the structural risk every market maker carries: that the trader hitting the quote often knows something the maker does not, and the fill is a loss the instant it prints. Toxic flow is the systematic version — order flow that consistently wins against the quotes it touches. The edge for a taker: when spreads expand and lit depth thins together with no news catalyst, the makers are pricing toxicity into the spread or pulling outright — taking liquidity into that regime means paying the full adverse-selection tax, and on the worst days (10/10 in October 2025 being the textbook), the quotes vanish entirely and price gaps until a real bid shows up.

§01
What It Is
The market maker's structural risk · why spreads exist
Adverse selection is the structural problem every market maker faces — that the trader on the other side of the quote often knows something the maker does not, and the fill is already a loss before it's marked.

A market maker is running a probability bet. She posts a two-sided quote — a bid and an ask — and earns the spread from anyone willing to pay it. The economics work only if the average trade hitting her quotes is roughly random. The moment a trade is not random — the moment the trader on the other side knows the price is about to move — the spread can't recoup the loss. That's adverse selection: trading systematically against participants whose order flow reflects superior information about future price dynamics. The maker buys, price falls; the maker sells, price rises. The fill itself is the loss.

Toxic flow is the operationalised version. Coined by FX market makers in the 2010s and now standard vocabulary at every serious crypto trading desk, it means flow that "will find itself to be out-of-the-money nearly immediately after the fill" ([Deribit Insights](https://insights.deribit.com/market-research/toxic-flow-its-sources-and-counter-strategies/)). It's not a one-off bad trade — that's noise. It's a pattern: a particular counterparty, a particular venue, or a particular market regime consistently extracting from the maker. The maker's job is to detect, price, and route around it.

Why this matters if you are not a market maker: the cost of adverse selection is built into every spread you cross. When BTC's top-of-book is 1 bp wide, the makers have priced your toxicity probability as roughly zero — you're noise, and they want your flow. When the spread blows out to 20+ bps mid-session with no news, they have repriced you — or, more precisely, repriced the regime. Spread is the adverse-selection premium, and reading it tells you what the smartest, fastest, best-capitalised counterparties on the venue think your flow is worth. The whole point of microstructure is that the spread is information.

Crypto sharpens the problem in two specific ways. First, liquidity provision is voluntary — unlike NYSE-style designated market makers who carry SLA-enforced obligations to two-sided quote, Wintermute, GSR, and the others can withdraw at will. Second, the toxic-flow sources are richer: latency arbitrageurs front-running cross-venue moves, mempool searchers reading public on-chain order flow before settlement, OTC desks leaking large-block intent, and now AI-augmented quant funds reading the same public feed faster than the makers can. The maker's defensive posture has to be paranoid by design.

If you only remember one thing A market maker doesn't lose to bad trades — she loses to systematically losing trades. Adverse selection is the name of the thing that makes them systematic, and the spread is her price for taking that risk.
§02
Mechanics
Glosten-Milgrom · VPIN · the four defensive responses
Glosten & Milgrom (1985) proved that the bid-ask spread must price in a premium for the probability of meeting an informed counterparty — and that premium scales with volatility, asymmetric information, and the share of toxic flow in the book.

The canonical academic frame is Glosten & Milgrom (1985). They modelled the market maker as facing a population of two trader types: informed traders who know the true price and trade only when it diverges from the quote, and uninformed (or liquidity) traders who trade for reasons unrelated to information. The maker can't tell them apart on any single trade. Her quoted spread therefore decomposes into three pieces: the cost of holding skewed inventory, a profit margin, and an adverse-selection premium that compensates her for the proportion of fills she expects to lose to informed traders.

Spread decomposition (Glosten-Milgrom): Quoted Spread = Inventory Cost + Adverse-Selection Premium + Profit Margin Adverse-selection premium (intuition): ASP ≈ Pr(informed | trade) × E[ |price impact| | informed ] VPIN — flow toxicity proxy (Easley, López de Prado, O'Hara 2012): VPIN = Σ |V_buy − V_sell| / Σ V_total computed over fixed-size volume buckets, not clock-time bars (e.g. 50 buckets of equal-volume slices over rolling window) Interpretation: VPIN ≈ 0 → balanced flow, low informed-trader share — quote tight VPIN → 1 → severe order-flow imbalance — informed activity rising VPIN spikes → market makers widen spreads or withdraw quotes
Note: VPIN is calculated in volume time, not clock time — the buckets are equal in volume traded, so they compress during fast tape and dilate during quiet sessions. This is the metric Easley/López de Prado/O'Hara showed spiked sharply before the May 2010 Flash Crash. Implementations in crypto require raw tick data (Binance, Bybit websockets) or platforms like Bookmap, ATAS, VisualHFT.

Operationally, the market maker has four responses as toxicity rises, in order of severity:

RegimeMM responseWhat it looks like on screen

The four sources of toxic flow in crypto, ranked roughly by how much volume they account for:

1. Latency arbitrage. Takers with faster connectivity to multiple venues take aggressively at one venue knowing the price has already moved at another. As Deribit's research desk puts it, sharp takers "have faster connectivity to other venues with similar products, allowing them to aggressively take against makers knowing that the market is already moving." The maker pays for being half a millisecond slow.

2. Cross-venue information leakage. A large OTC block trades on a desk; the counterparty hedges on a public venue; the public venue's makers eat the hedge before the block is even reported. Same mechanic as equity dark-pool leakage — the public quote pays for the private trade.

3. Liquidation cascades. Forced liquidations are perfectly informed flow — the liquidator has to sell, and her sale guarantees the next leg of price impact. Makers reading concentrated liquidation maps (Coinglass, Hyblock) pre-emptively widen as price approaches the cluster.

4. Mempool searchers. A crypto-specific source: bots monitor public mempools for large pending swaps, then frontrun on the same chain or hedge on centralised venues. The maker on the CEX gets adversely selected by a counterparty reading data she doesn't have time to read.

§04
In Practice
Four cards · how to trade with toxicity, not against it
The single rule: when spread expands and depth thins together without a news catalyst, market makers are pricing toxicity into the spread. Don't take liquidity into that regime — wait, or trade with the asymmetry.
1

Read Spread Expansion as a Forward Signal, Not a Lagging One

The makers see toxicity coming before the price prints it — they have to, or they bleed. When BTC's top-of-book spread doubles or triples with no news on the wire, the algos see something you don't. Pause execution; check funding, OI, and the liq map. The next 5–15 minutes typically resolve toward whichever side was already skewed. Spread leads price; price doesn't lead spread.

2

Watch Depth × Time-on-Book as a Toxicity Proxy

You can't see VPIN on most retail charts, but you can see depth × persistence: how much size is posted and how long it survives. Bookmap, Hyblock, and Velo expose this directly. When resting size collapses from $10M to $500K in the top five levels and orders are getting cancelled within 200ms of placement, makers are running ultra-defensive. Don't take liquidity into a flickering book — you are paying the maker's worst-case-scenario premium.

3

Pre-Event Spread Widening Is the Tell

Before CPI, FOMC, OPEX, or any scheduled high-impact event, makers widen pre-emptively — the toxicity probability for the next print is non-random by definition. As CPI prints on 13 May demonstrated, spreads were already 3× normal in the 90 seconds before release, and the makers who didn't widen got caught by the algos that read the print faster. The pro move: do nothing for the 5 minutes around print, then re-engage after the regime stabilises.

4

Never Market-Order Into a Depleted Book

If the top-of-book size is $200K and you need to move $2M, your market order pays the full adverse-selection premium across every level it eats. Either work the order patiently across 5–10 minutes via limit orders inside the spread, or wait for liquidity to return. The "I'll just send a market order, it's fine" mindset is exactly the uninformed-trader behaviour the maker is pricing the spread to extract from.

Worked Example — 13 May 2026 CPI Print
SetupCPI scheduled for 12:30 UTC. BTC trading ~$82,000 with normal 1-bp spread and ~$8M top-of-book depth on Binance perps.
T-5 minSpread widens from 1 bp → 3 bps. Depth halves to ~$4M. No price move yet — makers are repricing toxicity probability for the print.
T-90 secSpread now 5–8 bps. Depth $1–2M. Two-sided quotes are visibly flickering. Algorithmic makers shading bid down, ask up.
T=0 (print)CPI prints hot at 3.8%. Headline algos hit the bid in microseconds. Spread blows to 20+ bps. BTC drops $2,500 in 90 seconds.
T+3 minWintermute & Cumberland resume two-sided quoting at ~$79,500. Spread compresses to 4 bps. Regime priced in.
LessonAnyone who market-bought in the T-90s to T+3 window paid the full adverse-selection premium. The makers told you the toxicity was rising — pre-event spread widening was the signal.

Timeframe fit: Adverse selection signals are intraday-only — spread/depth context resets across sessions. Use 1m–5m for spread/depth monitoring, 15m–1h for regime classification. Higher-timeframe charts are blind to this — there is no daily-bar version of toxicity.

10 October 2025 — The Day the Quotes Vanished

The defining adverse-selection event of modern crypto: at ~17:00 UTC on Friday 10 October 2025, President Trump's Truth Social post announcing 100% tariffs on Chinese imports hit the wire. The macro algos read it before any human trader did. Within minutes, BTC perp order books "went nearly blank on the bid side" (insights4vc) and the cascade started. The makers had two choices: keep quoting and absorb whatever informed flow the headline algos sent at them, or pull. Most pulled.

Wintermute's Jasper De Maere later told the trade press: "The violence and the speed at which this entire liquidation cascade happened made it very difficult to continue to make markets in that specific window." The firm's rules-based delta-neutral system tagged the regime as untenable and stepped away — exactly the "PULLED" right-edge of the response curve in §03. LO:TECH's Marcus Horsley: "Our systems worked as intended: The risk engine's circuit breakers kicked in." Two of the largest crypto market makers, both gone in the same 15-minute window.

With the makers gone, price fragmented across venues. BTC traded ~$112K at one venue and a reported low of ~$24K on a USD1-BTC pair at Binance — a venue-specific wick that only happened because the makers who normally arbitraged between pairs were absent. Coinbase and Kraken showed ~9% spreads between each other on the same asset. Over 1.6M perp accounts liquidated. Total liquidations exceeded $19 billion.

Read Crypto market makers are not regulated to quote. When they assess that flow has turned toxic and the regime is untenable, they pull — and on the worst days, they all pull simultaneously because they're all running similar risk engines. The asymmetry isn't a maker-vs-taker problem; it's that the maker can leave and you can't. Which means your defensive posture has to mirror theirs: stand aside when they do.

The May 2026 Echo — Thin Books, Macro Sensitivity

Seven months on, the 10/10 hangover is still in the structure. BTC closed yesterday's Friday session around $79,000–$80,000 after a hot CPI print on 13 May (3.8% headline) and renewed bond-yield pressure. The setup is exactly the regime makers stay defensive in: macro-driven, two-way uncertainty, depth ~30–50% below pre-10/10 norms on major venues, and the Fed transition (Powell out, Warsh in) layering political toxicity on top of the data.

If you're sizing into this tape, treat every meaningful US session open as a potential adverse-selection event — the algos that read tariff posts, Truth Social, and Fed-speak headlines are calibrated to this environment now. The maker response is reflexive: spread first, depth second, pull third. Watch the spread before you watch the price.

§07
Common Traps
Five ways retail traders misread the maker's signal
The single biggest mistake: reading wide spreads as "manipulation" rather than as the makers correctly pricing the toxicity probability of your own session.
1
Treating Spread Expansion as Manipulation

The spread didn't "blow out because the exchange is rigged." The spread blew out because every algorithmic maker on the venue independently arrived at the same VPIN estimate and widened. It's a defensive consensus, not a conspiracy. Reading wide spreads as a signal — the makers see toxicity — is far more useful than reading them as a complaint.

2
Assuming Market Makers Lose Money in Volatility

This is precisely backwards. MMs make more per fill in volatility because the spread is wider; they take fewer fills because posted size is smaller. Their P&L is volatility-positive provided their toxicity model is calibrated. The only thing that hurts them is a regime they didn't price for — which is exactly when they pull, and exactly when the rest of the market gets punished.

3
"I'll Scalp the Spread" — No, You Won't

Posting a passive limit order inside the spread is technically possible, but you are playing the maker's game without their tools: no real-time toxicity model, no cross-venue latency, no inventory-risk algo. The maker is rotating their quotes 8–12 times per second based on signals you can't see. Trying to "scalp" the spread as a retail trader is paying the adverse-selection premium and the latency cost. Stick to taking liquidity when it's cheap, providing it only with full structural conviction.

4
Ignoring the Pre-Event Widening Window

Most adverse-selection blow-ups happen in scheduled windows: CPI, NFP, FOMC, OPEX, Fed-speak. The widening starts T-5 to T-2 minutes before the print. If you have a trade you want on, either get it on before the widening begins or wait until after the makers re-tighten post-event. The middle window — wide spreads, news pending — is where retail orders consistently get poached.

5
Treating VPIN/Toxicity Tools as Retail Alpha

VPIN is a great academic concept; the retail implementations are mostly approximations. True VPIN requires raw tick data, accurate trade classification, and rolling volume-time bucketing — Bookmap, Hyblock, VisualHFT, and ATAS get you close enough for context, but the TradingView "toxicity indicators" are mostly recycled imbalance scripts dressed up with new vocabulary. Use spread expansion and depth collapse — visible to the eye, free, real-time — as your toxicity proxies. They're 80% of the signal at 0% of the cost.
