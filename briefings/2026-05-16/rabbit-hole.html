TL;DR

In 1970, the CIA and West Germany's BND secretly purchased Crypto AG — a Swiss company whose encryption machines were the gold standard for government communications security. The machines were beautifully engineered. They were also deliberately broken. The cryptographic weaknesses were designed in, invisible to anyone who did not know where to look, and they allowed the NSA to read the secret diplomatic traffic of 120 nations for decades. Iran's communications during the hostage crisis. Argentina's during the Falklands War. Libya's cables that proved its role in the 1986 Berlin disco bombing. At its peak, this single covert purchase generated roughly 40 percent of all the foreign diplomatic decrypts the NSA could produce. West Germany sold its share in 1993; the CIA ran the operation alone until around 2018. When journalists finally published the story in February 2020 — using a 280-page internal CIA history that had leaked — they quoted the CIA's own verdict on what it had pulled off: "the intelligence coup of the century."

Section 01

The Story

Boris Hagelin was the most commercially successful cryptographer who ever lived. Born in Russia to a Swedish family in 1892, he trained as an engineer and ended up running a small cipher machine company in Stockholm. His machines were elegant: compact, mechanical, and relatively secure for their era. In 1940, as the Wehrmacht swept across Europe, he loaded prototypes into a suitcase and fled to the United States. Within three years, the US Army had ordered 140,000 units of his M-209 cipher machine — the most widely deployed encryption device of the Second World War. Hagelin became a millionaire. His machines encoded the orders for Operation Overlord. They protected Eisenhower's personal communications.

After the war, Hagelin moved to Zug, Switzerland — neutral, discreet, and geographically convenient for selling to European governments — and in 1952 formally established Crypto AG. The company's location was a selling point. A Swiss manufacturer had no Cold War allegiance, no loyalty to Washington or Moscow, no motive to compromise its products. Governments across the developing world, across Latin America, across the Middle East and South Asia, ordered Crypto AG machines with exactly this logic. If you wanted genuinely secure communications, you bought Swiss.

The flaw in that logic had a name: William Friedman. Friedman was the chief cryptanalyst of the US Army Signal Intelligence Service, the man who broke Japan's PURPLE cipher in 1940 and, in the eyes of most historians, the greatest cryptanalyst of the twentieth century. He and Hagelin had been friends since 1930, when Hagelin demonstrated his machines in Washington. The friendship was warm and genuine. It was also professionally useful to both men. In 1955, the two entered into what the NSA archive would later call a "gentleman's understanding": Hagelin would keep the NSA and GCHQ informed about which countries were buying which machines and what their technical specifications were. In return, the NSA would not publicize its ability to break older Hagelin designs, which would have destroyed his business and his reputation.

This arrangement gave Western intelligence visibility. But by the late 1960s, visibility was not enough. Crypto AG was supplying machines to Iran, Pakistan, India, Egypt, Argentina, Saudi Arabia, Libya, dozens of African and Latin American governments. The intelligence community wanted more than knowledge of the technical specs. It wanted to read the traffic in real time, at scale, without risk of detection.

On June 4, 1970, the CIA and the BND secretly purchased Crypto AG for 25 million Swiss francs. The company's engineers now had new clients. So did the world's governments — they just did not know it.

The purchase was structured through a shell company called Crypto Holdings AG. Hagelin, who was by then in his late seventies, reportedly negotiated the sale with full knowledge of what it meant. His son, Boris Hagelin Jr. — who worked at the company and reportedly had qualms about the arrangement — died in a car accident in 1970, the same year as the purchase. The circumstances were never satisfactorily explained. The elder Hagelin retired to Monaco and died in 1983, by which point the operation he had made possible was running at full capacity.

For the next two decades, Crypto AG's engineers designed machines that were, in every superficial respect, state-of-the-art. The hardware was real. The algorithms were real. The weaknesses were real too — they were just invisible. Governments that bought Crypto AG equipment believed they had purchased the best. In a sense, they had. It was just best for someone else.

Section 02

The Mechanism

The question of exactly how the machines were compromised is technically intricate, and the CIA has never published a full account. What is known, from the leaked history and from the accounts of Crypto AG engineers who were eventually told what the company actually was, is that the weakness operated at the algorithmic level rather than through a crude master key or hardware bypass.

Encryption security depends on key space — the number of possible keys an adversary would have to test in order to crack a message by brute force. A secure key of, say, 128 bits has 2128 possible values: roughly 340 undecillion combinations. Even with the most powerful computers imaginable, exhaustive search is not feasible. The Crypto AG machines used algorithms that appeared to generate large, random key spaces. What the CIA's engineers arranged was that the effective key space — the range of values the machine's randomness could actually produce, given its internal state — was far smaller than it appeared. The keys were not truly random. They were drawn from a constrained pool. An adversary who knew the constraint could search that pool, crack the key, and read the message.

The Two-Tier Customer System

Not all Crypto AG customers received equally compromised equipment. Western allies — including NATO members, close US partners, and a handful of particularly sensitive countries — received machines with stronger implementations or were quietly steered toward other products. The weakest versions of Crypto AG's algorithms went to the governments Western intelligence most wanted to surveil: Iran, Libya, Argentina, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and countries across Africa and Latin America. Employees who later came forward described an internal awareness among a small circle that certain "export" configurations were deliberately inferior — though most engineers were kept in the dark about why.

The physical machines reinforced the illusion of security. Crypto AG built beautifully engineered devices — precision mechanical and later electronic equipment manufactured to Swiss standards of quality. Customers who sent their machines to independent engineers for inspection found exactly what they expected: professional, sophisticated cryptographic hardware. The weakness was mathematical, not physical. It was not something you could find by opening the case.

The operation required ongoing maintenance. As Crypto AG introduced new product lines, new machine generations, new algorithms, the CIA had to ensure that each iteration preserved the properties that made decryption tractable. This meant that CIA and BND officers were involved in the company's engineering decisions for decades — reviewing proposed algorithms, flagging implementations that were too strong, guiding the product roadmap. Crypto AG's engineers believed they were building the best secure communications equipment in the world. Some of them were. They just had collaborators they did not know about.

From the NSA's end, the intercept operation was remarkably efficient. Foreign governments using Crypto AG machines encrypted their traffic and transmitted it via radio or teletype, often through channels the NSA was already monitoring. The NSA's analysts did not need to work to obtain the ciphertext — they collected it routinely. The Crypto AG machines turned decryption from a hard problem into a tractable one: the analysts knew the algorithm, knew the constrained key space, and could work through the possibilities at machine speed. At its peak in the 1980s, this translated into roughly 40 percent of all the foreign diplomatic machine-decryption the NSA was producing from a single corporate relationship.

Section 03

The Connections

The Falklands War, 1982: Reagan helped Thatcher because Argentina trusted Swiss neutrality. Argentina's military junta was a Crypto AG customer. When Argentine forces seized the Falkland Islands in April 1982 and the British task force sailed south, the Reagan administration — which had officially declared neutrality in the conflict — was reading Argentina's encrypted military and diplomatic communications in real time. The intelligence was passed to Britain. Argentine naval strategy, the locations of ships, diplomatic signals to potential mediators: all of it flowed through Crypto AG machines and all of it was readable. The Buenos Aires Times later reported, citing the 2020 revelations, that US signals intelligence derived from the Crypto AG operation gave Britain a significant advantage throughout the conflict. Argentina lost the war in part because its secrets were not secret.

The Iran hostage crisis, 1979–1981: 444 days of monitored negotiations. Iran was one of Crypto AG's earliest and most loyal customers. During the 444-day crisis in which Iranian students held 52 American diplomats, the US was reading Iranian government communications throughout the ordeal — including the internal deliberations of the Ayatollah Khomeini's circle about the terms under which the hostages might be released. The Reagan transition team's alleged back-channel communications with Iran to delay the hostage release until after the 1980 election — the so-called October Surprise — remains disputed. What is not disputed is that the US was not negotiating blind. Every message the Iranian government sent or received through Crypto AG equipment was an open book.

The La Belle Disco bombing, 1986: Reagan nearly burned the operation. In April 1986, a bomb exploded at La Belle, a discotheque in West Berlin popular with US servicemen. Two American soldiers and a Turkish woman were killed; 229 people were wounded. Within days, President Reagan went on national television to announce that the US had "direct, precise, and irrefutable" evidence that Libya had ordered the attack. The evidence was intercepts of Libyan diplomatic traffic between Tripoli and the Libyan embassy in East Berlin — traffic encrypted with Crypto AG equipment. Reagan ordered airstrikes on Tripoli and Benghazi. The operation worked. But intelligence officials reportedly panicked: Reagan had just announced publicly that the US could read Libya's encrypted diplomatic communications. If Gaddafi's government drew the obvious conclusion, it might change its encryption equipment. In the end, Libya did not change suppliers, and the operation continued. But it was a near miss — the kind of thing that could have ended the entire enterprise.

The Swiss neutrality myth. Switzerland's brand as a neutral, trustworthy intermediary is a real and carefully maintained asset. Swiss banks, Swiss mediation in international disputes, Swiss diplomatic offices representing US interests in countries with no US embassy — the neutrality is genuine as a legal and political matter. But the Crypto AG operation demonstrated that Swiss corporate law does not neutralize corporate ownership. A company registered in Zug and staffed by Swiss engineers could be secretly owned by the CIA, subject to CIA direction, and be producing CIA-compromised products for fifty years, all while trading on the credibility that "Swiss" connotes. Every government that chose Crypto AG specifically because it was Swiss had chosen it for a reason that did not protect them.

The encryption backdoor debate has a prehistory. When US law enforcement agencies in the 1990s began pushing for "key escrow" — a system where encrypted communications would include a copy of the key held by a government authority — cryptographers and civil liberties advocates argued that building in intentional weaknesses was inherently dangerous: whoever held the backdoor could be compromised, hacked, or corrupted. The Clipper Chip proposal, which would have mandated a government-accessible backdoor in telecommunications equipment, was defeated in 1994. The debate has never fully ended — the FBI's "Going Dark" problem, Apple's battles over device encryption, demands by the UK Home Office for exceptional access — and Operation Rubicon is its definitive historical case study. For fifty years, a backdoor was maintained successfully. It was also used against allies, monitored democratic governments, and was eventually exposed. The advocates for backdoors rarely cite it.

The Washington Post / ZDF / SRF Investigation (2020)
The original February 2020 reporting by Greg Miller at the Washington Post, produced jointly with ZDF and SRF, is the primary source and still freely available. The Post published substantial excerpts of the 280-page CIA internal history. The writing is unusually direct — the CIA analysts who wrote the history were, by the evidence of their own words, proud of what they had done and not inclined to minimize it.
🔐
The Clipper Chip and the 1990s Crypto Wars
The battle over the Clipper Chip — the NSA-designed encryption chip with a built-in government backdoor that AT&T was pressured to adopt in 1993 — is the direct policy sequel to Operation Rubicon. The chip was announced the same year West Germany quietly withdrew from the Crypto AG partnership. Steven Levy's book Crypto (2001) covers the full arc, from Whitfield Diffie's invention of public-key cryptography to the defeat of Clipper, and reads as the origin story of the modern internet's encryption architecture.
🧩
William Friedman — The Greatest Cryptanalyst
Friedman's life is one of the stranger careers in twentieth-century intelligence. He cracked Japanese PURPLE, coined the term "cryptanalysis," suffered a mental breakdown from overwork classified SECRET for decades, and ended his life negotiating the arrangement that made Operation Rubicon possible. David Kahn's The Codebreakers (1967) remains the definitive popular account of Friedman's work; Jason Fagone's The Woman Who Smashed Codes (2017) covers his wife Elizebeth, herself a major cryptanalyst.
🌍
The Zimmermann Telegram — Intelligence Interception That Changed a War
In January 1917, British naval intelligence intercepted and decoded a telegram from German Foreign Secretary Arthur Zimmermann to Mexico, proposing a military alliance against the United States. The British sat on it for weeks debating whether to reveal it — revealing it would expose their code-breaking capability — before deciding the intelligence value of bringing the US into the war outweighed the operational loss. America entered WWI two months later. The Zimmermann Telegram is the clearest historical precedent for the La Belle Disco dilemma Reagan faced in 1986.
🏦
Operation Gladio and NATO's Secret Stay-Behind Networks
Operation Rubicon was not the only Cold War intelligence operation that used European corporate and institutional cover to conduct decades-long covert activities. Operation Gladio — revealed in 1990 when Italian PM Andreotti disclosed its existence — was a NATO-backed network of secret stay-behind paramilitary organizations across Western Europe, designed to conduct sabotage behind Soviet lines in the event of an invasion. Like Rubicon, it ran for decades, involved European intelligence services as co-participants, and was revealed only after the Cold War it was designed for had ended.
