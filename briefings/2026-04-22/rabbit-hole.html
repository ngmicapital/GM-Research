TL;DR

In 1954, a US banana corporation convinced the American government to overthrow a democratically elected foreign president — and the men who signed the orders were personally on the company's payroll. The United Fruit Company owned nearly half of Guatemala's arable land, operated the country's railroads and ports, ran its telegraph network, and paid no meaningful taxes. When President Jacobo Árbenz introduced a land reform programme that would redistribute the company's unused fields to landless peasants, United Fruit hired the father of modern public relations to rebrand the moderate social democrat as a communist, and lobbied the CIA director — a personal shareholder and former board member — to remove him. Operation PBSUCCESS overthrew Árbenz in June 1954. The civil war that followed lasted 36 years and killed approximately 200,000 people. United Fruit was eventually renamed Chiquita Brands International, and in 2007 it pleaded guilty to funding a designated Colombian terrorist organisation. The term invented to describe what it had done — "banana republic" — entered the English language 50 years before the coup. O. Henry coined it watching the same company operate in Honduras.

Section 01

The Story: A Corporation So Big It Could Start a War

To understand what United Fruit did in Guatemala, you first have to understand what United Fruit was in Guatemala. By the early 1950s, the company had been operating in Central America for over half a century, and its footprint in the country bore less resemblance to a corporate enterprise than to an occupying administration.

United Fruit owned 42% of Guatemala's total land — an area larger than Switzerland — but cultivated only 15% of it. The remainder was held fallow, either as reserve capacity or as a deliberate barrier to competition. The company also owned Guatemala's only Atlantic port (Puerto Barrios), operated the country's entire railroad network through a subsidiary called the International Railways of Central America, and controlled the national telegraph infrastructure. Guatemalan exporters who wanted to ship anything abroad did so on UFC's terms, at UFC's prices, through UFC's port, on UFC's railway. The company paid virtually nothing in local taxes and had negotiated a 99-year customs exemption from the Guatemalan government. Internal company documents referred to Guatemala as "Bananaland." Guatemalans, less charitably, called United Fruit El Pulpo — The Octopus.

Into this arrangement stepped Jacobo Árbenz Guzmán, a military officer elected president of Guatemala in 1950 with 65% of the vote — the country's second free election in its history. Árbenz was a social democrat in the European mould, not a communist: he modelled his land reform on programmes already operating in democratic postwar Japan and Taiwan, both of which had been designed and implemented with US support. He was advised by a small number of Guatemalan communists, but they held no cabinet positions and constituted a minor fraction of his political coalition.

On 27 June 1952, Árbenz signed Decree 900, the Guatemalan Agrarian Reform Law. The decree was straightforward: farmland above 223 acres that was not in active cultivation would be subject to government expropriation, with compensation paid to the owner based on the land's declared tax value. The expropriated land would be distributed in parcels of 42.5 acres to landless peasants. Árbenz was explicit about his model: he wanted Guatemala to become "a modern capitalist country," not a socialist state. The reform targeted idle land, not productive farms. Its economic logic was impeccable.

"Our government proposes to take from the United Fruit Company those lands which it holds unproductively, and which are needed by our people."

— President Jacobo Árbenz, address to the Guatemalan National Congress, 1953

United Fruit held enormous quantities of precisely this kind of land. The government expropriated approximately 400,000 acres of UFC holdings and offered compensation of $1.2 million — the exact value that United Fruit's own accountants had declared on the company's Guatemalan tax returns, down to the dollar. United Fruit and the US State Department rejected this figure and demanded $16 million. The disparity was not a negotiating position. It was a confession: the company had been systematically underreporting its land value to the Guatemalan tax authority for decades, and the compensation offer simply called its bluff. Rather than accept the consequences of its own accounting, United Fruit decided to end the government doing the accounting.

Section 02

The Mechanism: Propaganda, Conflict of Interest, and a CIA Director on the Payroll

The mechanism by which United Fruit engineered the overthrow of a foreign government was threefold: it manufactured a Communist threat through a public relations campaign; it leveraged direct personal conflicts of interest at the highest levels of the US government; and it funded and coordinated the military operation that ultimately removed Árbenz.

The propaganda. In the late 1940s, United Fruit hired Edward Bernays as its chief public relations strategist. Bernays was not an ordinary PR man. The nephew of Sigmund Freud and the author of a book literally titled Propaganda (1928), he is widely credited as the founder of the modern public relations industry — the man who invented the concept of manufacturing public consent. His earlier campaigns had convinced Americans to eat bacon for breakfast, persuaded women to take up smoking by rebranding cigarettes as "torches of freedom," and helped elect a US president. He charged UFC a retainer of $100,000 per year (roughly $1.2 million in today's money).

Bernays's Guatemala campaign was a masterpiece of the dark arts. He organised sponsored press tours to Guatemala for American journalists, briefed editors and columnists, arranged congressional delegations, and produced a steady stream of materials framing Árbenz's moderate land reform as the beachhead of Soviet communist infiltration in the Western Hemisphere. He coined the phrase "Soviet beachhead in Guatemala" and used it relentlessly. He arranged for American newspaper editors to receive research kits "documenting" communist influence in the Árbenz government. He was so effective that, by 1953, the New York Times, Time, Newsweek, and much of the American press were describing a reformist social democrat who had won a free election with 65% of the vote as a communist threat requiring urgent action.

The Conflict of Interest Map — Who Was on the Payroll

Allen Dulles — Director of the CIA, the man who planned and authorised Operation PBSUCCESS. Former board member of United Fruit Company; former partner at Sullivan & Cromwell, UFC's primary Washington law firm.

John Foster Dulles — US Secretary of State, Allen's brother, who personally lobbied President Eisenhower to authorise the coup. Active partner at Sullivan & Cromwell during the period the firm negotiated UFC's Guatemalan concessions. His firm helped draft the very land-use agreements Árbenz's reform was now threatening.

Henry Cabot Lodge Jr. — US Ambassador to the United Nations, who blocked Security Council intervention after the coup. His family were shareholders in United Fruit.

John Moors Cabot — US Assistant Secretary of State for Inter-American Affairs, who publicly advocated for intervention. His family owned United Fruit stock.

The US government's stated justification: halting communist expansion in the Americas. The communists in question were a small advisory group with no cabinet positions in the government of a country that shared no border with any communist state.

The operation. In August 1953, President Eisenhower authorised the CIA to execute Operation PBSUCCESS, with an initial budget of $2.7 million — later estimated at up to $7 million total. The CIA selected Colonel Carlos Castillo Armas, a Guatemalan military officer living in exile in Honduras, to lead the coup. CIA officers trained his forces in Nicaragua (whose dictator, Anastasio Somoza, was personally enthusiastic about removing the reformist Guatemalan government), and supplied them with weapons, aircraft, and a propaganda radio station that broadcast false reports of military advances to demoralise the Guatemalan army.

On 18 June 1954, Castillo Armas crossed the Guatemalan border with a force of roughly 480 men. The military operation itself was almost farcical — his forces were small, poorly organised, and initially stalled. The psychological operation was what won. CIA-piloted aircraft bombed the capital and Puerto Barrios, the false radio broadcasts claimed major cities had fallen, and the Guatemalan military — uncertain of the truth and unwilling to commit to a losing fight — declined to resist. Árbenz, abandoned by his own generals, resigned on 27 June 1954 and went into exile. He spent the rest of his life moving between Mexico, Uruguay, Cuba, and France, never returning to Guatemala. He died in Mexico City in 1971.

Section 03

The Connections: The Civil War, the Rebranding, and the Legacy That Wouldn't Stay Buried

The immediate aftermath of the coup was the reversal of every reform Árbenz had introduced. Castillo Armas restored United Fruit's expropriated land within weeks, abolished the Agrarian Reform Law, disenfranchised illiterate voters (eliminating roughly 70% of the electorate), banned trade unions and political parties, and began arresting and executing suspected leftists. The United States officially celebrated Guatemala's "liberation from communist control."

What followed was not liberation. The coup destabilised Guatemalan society so fundamentally that in 1960 a civil war began between the government and leftist guerrilla movements. The conflict lasted 36 years and killed an estimated 200,000 people, with the vast majority of victims being indigenous Maya civilians killed by US-backed military governments in campaigns that a UN truth commission later described as constituting acts of genocide. The 1954 coup did not merely remove a president. It extinguished the brief democratic experiment Guatemala had conducted, and the resulting war became one of the longest and bloodiest in Latin American history.

The Chiquita AUC payments are worth dwelling on, because they complete a disturbing arc. From 1997 to 2004 — a period spanning three US presidential administrations — Chiquita paid protection money to a Colombian paramilitary group responsible for massacres, forced disappearances, and targeted killings of union leaders and civilians in the banana-growing regions of Urabá and Santa Marta. The AUC had been designated a foreign terrorist organisation by the US government in September 2001. Chiquita continued paying for nearly three more years after that designation. When the company disclosed the payments to its own lawyers, the lawyers advised it to stop immediately. Chiquita continued for another year before voluntarily disclosing to the Justice Department. The same banana, the same regions, the same basic dynamic — local labour and land rights subordinated to export economics, with violence as the enforcement mechanism — half a century after Guatemala.

The US government formally acknowledged its role in the 1954 coup in 1999, when the Clinton administration declassified the relevant CIA documents and Secretary of State Madeleine Albright stated that the intervention had been "wrong." In 2011, President Obama reiterated the apology on a visit to El Salvador, noting that the US had "supported military coups" in the region. The Guatemalan government received no reparations. United Fruit received its land back in 1954 and operated profitably for decades afterward.

"It is clear that support for military coups in Latin America was wrong."

— President Barack Obama, San Salvador, March 2011

What makes the Guatemala case enduringly instructive is not its exoticism but its transparency. Most exercises of corporate power over sovereign states are obscured by layers of intermediaries, plausible deniability, and contested causation. The United Fruit case is unusually legible: the company's lobbyists wrote the intelligence assessments, the CIA director owned company stock, the Secretary of State's law firm had drafted the concessions being threatened, and the operation's internal documents — now declassified and publicly available on the CIA's own website — describe the planning, the deceptions, and the reasoning with remarkable candour. The term "banana republic" was already in the language before any of it happened. O. Henry had just been watching the same company do the same thing, one country over, fifty years earlier.

Section 04

Further Down the Hole

Edward Bernays: The Man Who Invented Spin

Sigmund Freud's nephew pioneered the idea that public opinion could be manufactured rather than discovered. His 1928 book Propaganda argued that the "conscious and intelligent manipulation of the organised habits and opinions of the masses" was a legitimate exercise of democratic leadership. Joseph Goebbels reportedly kept a copy. Bernays spent decades rebranding this as "public relations." The Guatemala campaign was his most geopolitically consequential application: he convinced the American press that a moderate land reformer was a communist threat, without a single false fact — just selection, framing, and access.

O. Henry and the Original Banana Republic

William Sydney Porter — O. Henry — fled to Honduras in 1896 to escape embezzlement charges in Texas. He spent several months watching United Fruit's predecessor operations and wrote Cabbages and Kings (1904), a linked short-story collection set in the fictional "Republic of Anchuria." His term "banana republic" described a country so thoroughly captured by a foreign fruit corporation that its politics, military, and judiciary served corporate rather than national interests. He meant it as satire. It became a category of political science.

The Dulles Brothers' Remarkable Coincidence

The CIA Director who planned the coup and the Secretary of State who sold it to Eisenhower were brothers who had both worked for United Fruit's law firm. Allen Dulles sat on UFC's board directly. John Foster Dulles's firm, Sullivan & Cromwell, had negotiated UFC's Guatemalan railway and land concessions — the very arrangements Árbenz's reform threatened. This is not disputed: it is in the declassified record. Both brothers argued passionately and successfully that the Guatemalan land reform represented a Soviet-backed communist threat requiring military response. Neither disclosed their financial relationships.

Operation PBSUCCESS: The Declassified Playbook

The CIA declassified its Operation PBSUCCESS files in stages from 1997 onwards; the full archive is now available on the CIA's public reading room. The documents reveal a remarkably candid account of the planning process: budget allocations for "psychological warfare," instructions for the propaganda radio station, target lists for Guatemalan officials, and internal assessments that acknowledge Árbenz was not actually a communist but a nationalist reformer. The gap between the public justification and the internal assessment is documented in the agency's own files.

The Civil War That Lasted 36 Years

The Guatemalan Civil War began in 1960, six years after the coup, and ended in 1996 with a UN-brokered peace accord. The UN Commission for Historical Clarification (CEH) concluded in 1999 that state forces were responsible for 93% of the approximately 200,000 deaths, and that their actions against indigenous Maya communities constituted genocide under international law. The commission also specifically identified the 1954 US intervention as having created the conditions for the conflict. The peace accord included a formal acknowledgement that US support for military governments had prolonged and intensified the violence.
