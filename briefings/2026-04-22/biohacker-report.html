TL;DR — Driving Thesis

The American College of Sports Medicine just rewrote the rulebook on resistance training for the first time in 17 years — and the headline is not what most people expect. Volume beats intensity for hypertrophy; 2x/week per muscle group is the minimum that actually matters; and any resistance training — bands, bodyweight, home gym — beats nothing by a significant margin. Simultaneously, a 2026 meta-analysis has put a stake through creatine's anti-inflammatory claims (CRP and IL-6: no significant effect), while creatine's cognitive and mood profile keeps quietly strengthening. On the longevity front, an antiretroviral drug (FTC/TAF) is reducing epigenetic age markers in healthy adults — not cancer patients, not frail elderly — signalling that retrotransposon suppression is moving from theory to testable intervention. The action for this cycle: lock in your weekly volume per muscle group against the new ACSM benchmarks, and stop expecting creatine to fix inflammation.

01

Longevity & Biomarkers

⚡ An HIV Drug Is Reversing Epigenetic Age in Healthy Adults

RCT New

The most significant longevity finding this cycle comes from a controlled trial comparing two antiretroviral regimens in non-HIV populations. The FTC/TAF group (N=36) showed a reduction in DunedinPACE of –0.061 and a PhenoAge reduction of –6.33 years compared to the control arm. The mechanism: tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) suppresses reverse transcriptase activity, blocking retrotransposon replication that drives chronic inflammation and DNA damage accumulation. This is not a mouse model or a mechanistic hypothesis — it's a controlled trial in adults, measuring validated epigenetic clocks.

🔬 In Plain English

DunedinPACE is a blood test that measures how fast your body is biologically aging right now — think of it as a speedometer for aging, not just where you are. PhenoAge is a different clock that estimates your biological age from blood biomarkers. A PhenoAge reduction of 6.33 years means the drug made people's blood look nearly 6.5 years younger by that measure.

🔬 In Plain English

Retrotransposons are ancient viral DNA sequences embedded in your genome that normally stay silent — but as you age, they "wake up" and start copying themselves, causing DNA damage and triggering inflammation. Reverse transcriptase is the enzyme that lets them replicate. Block the enzyme; quiet the chaos.

View: This is the clearest human signal yet that retrotransposon suppression works in vivo. It won't be prescribed off-label anytime soon, but it validates the biological target. The implication for lifestyle: anything that reduces chronic low-grade inflammation (sleep, zone 2 cardio, low ultra-processed food load) is operating on the same pathway — just more slowly.

Source: Fight Aging! Newsletter, April 20, 2026 — citing the FTC/TAF RCT in healthy adults. DunedinPACE and PhenoAge values confirmed against newsletter primary reference.

SASP Score: A New Aging Clock That Predicts Mortality

RCT-Validated

A deep learning model analysing the SASP (senescence-associated secretory phenotype) protein panel is now validated as a "strong, independent predictor of mortality risk and incident serious, chronic medical conditions." The model's predictions were confirmed in an independent randomised clinical trial — meaning this isn't just correlational. The SASP score captures how many of your cells have gone senescent and how aggressively they're secreting inflammatory signals. It's more actionable than standard CRP because it tells you the source of the inflammation, not just that it exists.

🔬 In Plain English

Senescent cells (often called "zombie cells") are old or damaged cells that refuse to die and instead pump out a cocktail of inflammatory chemicals — the SASP. Too many of them accelerates every major aging disease from cardiovascular disease to cognitive decline. A high SASP score means more zombie cells, more inflammation, faster aging.

View: This is a biomarker worth tracking within 2–3 years as commercial panels catch up. For now, the actionable proxy is reducing modifiable SASP drivers: excess body fat, sleep debt, and sedentary behaviour — all of which increase senescent cell burden. SASP testing is not yet widely available in Australia but is emerging through longevity clinics.

Longevity Science Shifts: From "Fix Pathways" to "Restore Coordination"

Expert Opinion

The 2nd World Congress on Targeting Longevity (Berlin, April 8–9, 2026) signalled a paradigm shift: aging is increasingly framed as a loss of coordination between systems — metabolism, immunity, mitochondria, and the gut microbiome — rather than a failure of any single pathway. The practical implication is that targeting one lever (e.g., mTOR inhibition alone, or NAD+ alone) will always underperform compared to interventions that restore system-level dialogue.

🔬 In Plain English

mTOR is a cellular switch that controls growth and repair — when it's always on, you grow tumours; when suppressed, cells clean up debris (autophagy). NAD+ is a molecule your cells use for energy production and DNA repair that declines sharply with age. Neither alone is the full answer — the congress consensus is that the orchestra matters more than any single instrument.

⚡ Implication: The highest-leverage longevity action this cycle is not a supplement — it's ensuring your sleep, training, and inflammatory load are working together, since all three modulate the retrotransposon/SASP axis simultaneously.

Recovery & Sleep

Daily Exercise Is a Sleep Drug — The Consistency Effect

Observational

Research from UT Austin suggests that exercising more frequently — ideally every day, even lightly — improves sleep quality in ways that weekend-warrior sessions don't replicate. The key metric improved: slow-wave (deep) sleep, which is the stage responsible for growth hormone release, immune consolidation, and physical repair. The effect was observed even when total weekly exercise volume was controlled — meaning the distribution of effort, not just the total dose, matters for sleep.

🔬 In Plain English

Slow-wave sleep (also called deep sleep or Stage 3 NREM) is the deepest, most physically restorative sleep stage. It's when your body releases the most growth hormone, repairs muscle tissue, and consolidates immune memory. More of it = faster recovery, better body composition, stronger immune function.

View: For a PPL lifter, this means active recovery days (walk, Zone 2 bike, light mobility work) aren't just "not nothing" — they're actively improving the sleep quality that drives training adaptation. Low-intensity daily movement is free recovery tool that most people leave on the table.

Fundamentals: The Sleep Optimization Stack

RCT Meta

No single supplement changes sleep as dramatically as controlling light and temperature — but for those already doing that, the evidence-backed stack is short. Magnesium glycinate (300–400mg before bed): 3 meta-analyses confirm improvement in sleep onset and sleep efficiency, particularly in adults with low baseline magnesium. Apigenin (50mg): binds GABA-A receptors, mild sedative effect without next-day grogginess [Expert Opinion via Huberman Lab — no Phase 3 RCT]. L-theanine (200mg): consistently reduces sleep onset latency in RCTs and blunts cortisol reactivity.

🔬 In Plain English

GABA-A receptors are the brain's "calm down" switches — they're what alcohol and benzodiazepines (like Valium) target. Apigenin acts on them very gently, lowering neural excitability enough to ease into sleep without the next-day sedation. Sleep onset latency is how long it takes you to fall asleep after getting into bed — the shorter, the better.

Non-supplement stack (higher leverage): Bedroom temperature 17–19°C; blackout darkness; no bright light within 90 minutes of bed; consistent wake time (including weekends). These outperform any supplement in every head-to-head comparison.

On wearables: Whoop and Oura are now tracking slow-wave sleep and HRV together — the most useful daily signal is HRV trend over 7 days, not a single-day reading. A declining 7-day trend with no change in training = under-recovery. A rising trend after a deload = green light to push volume.

Quick Reference — Sleep Stack
  • Magnesium glycinate: 300–400mg, 30–60 min before bed
  • L-theanine: 200mg, 30 min before bed (stack with magnesium)
  • Apigenin: 50mg, before bed [Expert Opinion — use cautiously]
  • Temperature: 17–19°C bedroom; warm shower 1–2hr before bed (paradoxically aids cooling)
  • Light: No bright overhead light post-sunset; red/amber lighting only
⚡ Implication: Add a 20-minute walk or mobility session on rest days — even without raising heart rate significantly, daily movement consistency improves deep sleep quality and compounds recovery.

Nutrition & Fuel

New US Dietary Guidelines Raise Protein Target — Is the Science There?

Meta

The 2025–2030 US Dietary Guidelines (released January 2026) raised the recommended protein intake from 0.8g/kg to 1.2–1.6g/kg of body weight per day — a 50–100% increase. For dc at, say, 80kg: that's a target of 96–128g/day versus the old 64g. However, Dr. Christopher Gardner (Stanford Prevention Research Center) stated directly: "there really isn't any new evidence showing we were off by that much." The evidence that supports the higher range comes from resistance-trained populations — which is exactly the context that applies here.

🔬 In Plain English

The RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance) is set to prevent deficiency in 97.5% of the population — it's a floor, not an optimum. For active people doing resistance training, "don't be deficient" and "maximise muscle protein synthesis" require different amounts. The new guidelines move closer to the active-person optimum.

View: For a PPL lifter, the target is 1.6–2.2g/kg — the new guidelines are still below what the sports science literature supports for muscle protein synthesis optimisation. Stuart Phillips PhD (McMaster, one of the world's leading protein researchers) supports 1.6g/kg as the practical minimum for active adults, with diminishing returns above 2.2g/kg. The guidelines are more useful for the general population than for trained athletes — for dc, the number should be at least 1.6g/kg.
Quick Reference — Protein for PPL Training
  • Target: 1.6–2.2g/kg/day (e.g. 80kg = 128–176g/day)
  • Per meal: 30–50g for maximum muscle protein synthesis stimulation per sitting
  • Timing: Post-training meal within 2hr; pre-bed protein (40g casein or cottage cheese) adds marginal benefit
  • Vegan vs omnivore: 2026 data confirms equivalent muscle building when leucine content is matched — soy, pea protein at adequate doses work
  • Best-value sources: Eggs (6g/egg), Greek yoghurt (20g/cup), chicken breast (31g/100g), canned tuna (25g/100g)

Creatine + SSRI Combination Shows Meaningful Signal for Depression

Meta RCT

A 2026 systematic review by Fares et al. (published in the Canadian Journal of Psychiatry) analysed 5 RCTs, 126 creatine vs 112 placebo participants. The headline: creatine monohydrate combined with escitalopram (an SSRI) outperformed SSRI + placebo with a Cohen's d = 1.13 at 8 weeks — a large effect size. The same review found creatine combined with CBT (cognitive behavioural therapy) also outperformed CBT alone.

🔬 In Plain English

Cohen's d = 1.13 is a large effect size — it means the improvement in the creatine group was more than one standard deviation better than the control group. For context: most antidepressant trials achieve Cohen's d of 0.3–0.5. A d of 1.13 as an adjunct (add-on) is noteworthy. The caveat: total depression score improvement of ~2.2 points, which is below clinical significance thresholds — statistically real, clinically modest.

🔬 In Plain English

An SSRI (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor) like escitalopram is a standard antidepressant that keeps serotonin active longer in the brain. The working hypothesis for creatine's mood effect is that it boosts brain energy metabolism (ATP production), which underpins emotional regulation and cognitive resilience — a completely different mechanism to the SSRI.

⚠ Hype Watch
The "3.6x higher recovery from depression" headline circulating from one source (Ayuba Nutrition blog) is not supported by the Fares 2026 review's actual data. The 2.2-point depression score improvement is statistically significant but below clinical meaningfulness thresholds. Don't conflate the SSRI augmentation signal (meaningful) with cure-level claims (not supported).
⚡ Implication: If you're not already on creatine monohydrate 5g/day, start — the performance case is ironclad and the cognitive/mood benefit profile is growing. Do not expect it to reduce systemic inflammation (see Section 04).

Protocols

Protocol Deep-Dive: Creatine — What the 2026 Data Actually Says

Meta RCT

This cycle, creatine gets a full reassessment because the 2026 literature significantly updates two major claims. Claim 1 (confirmed): creatine improves short-term strength, power output, and recovery — a short-term RCT in resistance-trained athletes (PMC12833896) confirmed strength gains, fatigue reduction, and faster recovery in a double-blind crossover. Claim 2 (rejected): creatine reduces inflammation. A 2026 Frontiers in Immunology meta-analysis (8 RCTs, PRISMA 2020-compliant, PROSPERO-registered) found:

  • CRP (acute): SMD = 0.32, p = 0.30 — not significant
  • CRP (chronic): SMD = –0.11, p = 0.73 — not significant
  • IL-6 (chronic): SMD = –0.06, p = 0.84 — not significant
  • Evidence certainty: Moderate (GRADE framework)
🔬 In Plain English

CRP (C-reactive protein) is a blood marker that rises when there's inflammation anywhere in the body — it's one of the most commonly measured inflammation markers in blood tests. IL-6 is a signalling molecule (cytokine) that ramps up inflammation; chronically elevated IL-6 is linked to heart disease, depression, and accelerated aging. SMD (standardised mean difference) is a way of comparing effect sizes across different studies — an SMD near 0 means no effect.

Conclusion: Creatine is one of the most well-studied, safest, and most useful supplements available — but it is not an anti-inflammatory. Dose remains 3–5g/day of monohydrate (no loading phase necessary). Emerging evidence for brain health supports going to 10g/day in older adults, but 5g/day is the sweet spot for most people. No clinically significant adverse effects across hundreds of trials at standard doses.

Confirmed Benefits

  • Maximal strength & power ↑ 5–15% [RCT]
  • Muscle protein synthesis augmentation [RCT]
  • Recovery (less soreness, faster return to output) [RCT]
  • Memory, attention, processing speed [Meta]
  • Mood / SSRI augmentation [Meta — early signal]

Not Supported

  • Systemic inflammation reduction [Meta 2026]
  • CRP reduction [Meta 2026 — null result]
  • IL-6 reduction [Meta 2026 — null result]
  • Fat loss (no direct evidence)
  • Kidney damage at normal doses (no evidence)
⚠ Australia Legal Status Note
Creatine monohydrate is legal, unscheduled, and widely available in Australia. No TGA restrictions. Standard sports supplement. Use monohydrate — no evidence that HCl, ethyl ester, or buffered versions outperform it.
⚡ Implication: Run creatine at 5g/day consistently — and stop expecting it to substitute for anti-inflammatory lifestyle practices (sleep, low ultra-processed food load, Zone 2 cardio).

Movement & Load

⚡ ACSM 2026 Position Stand: The Resistance Training Rulebook Rewritten

Meta New

The American College of Sports Medicine published its first major update to resistance training guidelines in 17 years — synthesising 137 systematic reviews from more than 30,000 participants. This is the highest-level evidence summary available, and it changes a few things practically.

✓ What Changes / What Matters Most
  • Frequency: 2x/week per muscle group is the minimum that matters — the dominant variable over session structure
  • Hypertrophy: Driven by total weekly volume, not load. You can grow muscle with bands or bodyweight at sufficient volume
  • Strength: ≥80% 1RM is still the gold standard for maximal strength development
  • Power: 30–70% 1RM at high velocity — especially relevant for maintaining fast-twitch function over 35
  • Bar lowered: ANY resistance training — home, bands, bodyweight — is meaningfully effective. Gym access is not the gating factor
✗ What NOT to Change
  • Progressive overload — still the engine of adaptation
  • PPL structure — training each muscle 2x/week aligns perfectly with updated guidelines
  • Compound lifts as primary movers — no evidence favouring isolation-first programming
  • Rest periods — 2–3 min between heavy sets still optimal for strength; 60–90 sec for hypertrophy not overturned
  • Protein timing window — still relevant, just less acute (total daily protein matters more)
🔬 In Plain English

1RM (one-repetition maximum) is the most weight you can lift for exactly one rep with good form — it's the benchmark for measuring strength and calibrating training intensity. 80% 1RM means lifting at 80% of your maximum, which typically corresponds to ~8 reps to failure. Training volume is sets × reps × weight — total work done per muscle group per week.

View for PPL programming: The ACSM update validates the PPL structure as written — each muscle group hits 2x/week naturally. The practical audit question: are you hitting 10–20 sets per muscle group per week? Below 10 sets = likely leaving hypertrophy on the table. Above 20 sets = likely generating junk volume that reduces recovery quality. 12–16 sets per week per major muscle group is the evidence-backed sweet spot.
Quick Reference — Updated Volume Benchmarks (per muscle group per week)
  • Minimum Effective Volume: ~10 sets (ACSM 2026 consensus)
  • Optimal Hypertrophy Volume: 12–20 sets (per Dr. Mike Israetel / RP)
  • Maximum Recoverable Volume: 20–25 sets (varies by training age and recovery quality)
  • Frequency: 2x/week minimum; 3x/week for lagging muscle groups
  • Load for strength: ≥80% 1RM for peak strength gains
  • Load for hypertrophy: 30–80% 1RM all grow muscle if volume is matched — choose what allows best form

Do Partial Reps After Failure Add Anything? RP Weighs In

Expert Opinion

Renaissance Periodization (April 7, 2026) analysed whether grinding out partial reps after reaching full-range failure is an effective hypertrophy technique. The answer from current mechanistic literature: yes, with conditions. Partials in the lengthened position (bottom of a curl, bottom of a squat) appear to add meaningful mechanical tension beyond full-ROM failure. Partials in the shortened position (top of a curl, standing fully upright) add almost nothing additional.

🔬 In Plain English

The lengthened position is when a muscle is stretched out under load — like the bottom of a bicep curl or the deep part of a Romanian deadlift. Research is converging on the idea that loading a muscle when it's stretched (not contracted) produces the strongest hypertrophy stimulus. So partial reps after failure are only worth doing at the stretch point of the movement, not the squeeze point.

Practical takeaway: On isolation movements (curls, leg curls, flyes), after reaching failure at full ROM, grind out 2–4 partial reps in the bottom/stretched position. Skip the top-end partials — they're not doing meaningful work.
⚡ Implication: Audit your weekly sets per muscle group against the 12–16 set sweet spot — if you're above 20, pull volume back and watch recovery quality improve; if you're below 10, you're likely undertrained regardless of intensity.
